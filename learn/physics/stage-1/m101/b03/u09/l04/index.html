M101 · Unit 9 · Lesson 4

Integration by parts

The product rule can exchange a difficult integral for an easier one.

Substitution works particularly well when one factor is the derivative of an inner expression. For xex, that pattern is not present: the derivative of the exponent is 1, not x. Yet differentiating the product xex gives ex+xex, which contains the integrand we want. We can use this relationship to isolate its integral.

Four hours: 100 minutes reading and reconstructing examples, 80 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback and correction, and 20 minutes reserved for the unit exercises.

9.13 Recover a product-rule relationship

Let u and v be differentiable functions of x. The product rule says (uv)′=u′v+uv′. Rearrange it to isolate the second term:

uv′=(uv)′−u′v.

Integrating now gives a product minus another integral. The antiderivative of (uv)′ is uv, so

∫u(x)v′(x)dx=u(x)v(x)−∫u′(x)v(x)dx.

This is integration by parts. The right side must still be completed, including an arbitrary constant when we give the final family. The method succeeds when differentiating u and integrating v′ leaves a simpler integral.

Result 9.5 · Integration by parts

Writing du=u′(x)dx and dv=v′(x)dx,

∫udv=uv−∫vdu.

Choose u to differentiate and dv to integrate. Check that the remaining integral is simpler. Include C in the completed indefinite answer.

The letters have a different role from the substitution notation: here both u and v remain functions of x. We are reorganising a product rule, rather than changing the integration variable. Keeping a short table of u,du,dv,v makes the operation visible.

Example 9.12 · A polynomial times an exponential

Find ∫xe2xdx.

Choose u=x, because its derivative is the simpler constant 1. Take dv=e2xdx, whose antiderivative is already known. Thus du=dx and v=e2x/2. Substituting into the parts formula gives

∫xe2xdx=xe2x2−12∫e2xdx.

Finish the elementary integral:

∫xe2xdx=xe2x2−e2x4+C.

Differentiating the first term gives e2x/2+xe2x; differentiating the second gives −e2x/2. The extra terms cancel, leaving exactly xe2x. That cancellation is the product-rule mechanism behind the method.

Activity 9.13 · Explain the cancellation

(a) Use parts to find ∫xcos(3x)dx, recording all four entries u,du,dv,v. (b) Differentiate your result and identify the terms that cancel. (c) Explain why taking u=cos(3x), dv=xdx would be a less useful first choice.

Discussion of Activity 9.13

Choose u=x, du=dx, dv=cos(3x)dx, v=sin(3x)/3. Then

∫xcos(3x)dx=xsin(3x)3−13∫sin(3x)dx=xsin(3x)3+cos(3x)9+C.

The derivative of the first term is sin(3x)/3+xcos(3x); the second supplies −sin(3x)/3. The alternative choice leaves an integral involving x2sin(3x), raising the polynomial degree and making this problem harder.

9.14 Choose the split by looking ahead

A useful split must satisfy two requirements. We must be able to integrate dv, and the resulting vdu should be easier to integrate than the original product. With a polynomial times an exponential or sine, differentiating the polynomial often helps because its degree falls. This is a reason for a choice, rather than a rule that every product must follow.

There can also be a useful hidden factor of 1. The derivative of lnx is simple, but its integral has not yet appeared in our elementary table. Regard it as (lnx)·1, then integrate the factor 1.

Example 9.13 · Integrating a logarithm

Find ∫lnxdx for x>0.

Set u=lnx and dv=dx, so du=dx/x and v=x. Then

∫lnxdx=xlnx−∫1dx.

Hence the family is

xlnx−x+C.

The remaining integrand is 1 because x≠0 on our interval. For the check, (xlnx)′=lnx+1; subtracting the derivative of x removes the extra 1. The domain x>0 comes from the original logarithm.

When finding v, choose one convenient antiderivative, usually with zero added constant. This loses no final answers. If we replace v by v+K, the parts expression changes by Ku−K∫du, whose nonconstant terms cancel. The final arbitrary C accounts for the remaining constant freedom.

Activity 9.14 · Make a useful split

Find and check (a) ∫xlnxdx, for x>0; (b) ∫xe−xdx; (c) ∫ln(2x)dx, for x>0. Explain why differentiating the logarithm helps in (a) and (c).

Discussion of Activity 9.14

(a) Take u=lnx, dv=xdx, so v=x2/2. Then x2lnx/2−12∫xdx=x2lnx/2−x2/4+C. (b) Take u=x, dv=e−xdx, giving v=−e−x. The result is −xe−x−e−x+C. (c) Take u=ln(2x), dv=dx. Its derivative is 1/x, so the result is xln(2x)−x+C. The logarithm disappears from each remaining integral; differentiating the finished answers cancels the extra product-rule terms.

9.15 Apply parts again when it reduces the problem

A successful step need not finish a calculation. For a quadratic polynomial times an exponential, the first differentiation reduces the polynomial to a linear function. A second application can then reduce it to a constant. Plan those reductions before doing the arithmetic.

Example 9.14 · Two reductions

Find ∫x2exdx.

With u=x2, dv=exdx, we have du=2xdx and v=ex. The first step gives

∫x2exdx=x2ex−2∫xexdx.

For the remaining integral, take u=x, dv=exdx. This gives ∫xexdx=xex−ex, apart from its arbitrary constant. Put that whole expression inside the subtraction:

∫x2exdx=x2ex−2(xex−ex)+C.

Thus a compact answer is ex(x2−2x+2)+C. Differentiate it using the product rule:

ex(x2−2x+2)+ex(2x−2)=x2ex.

The linear and constant terms cancel. Keeping the brackets around the second result prevents the sign of the last term being lost.

This is still introductory parts: each step makes a polynomial simpler and the process ends visibly. Other integrals can produce a cycle or demand additional techniques. We will not build that broader catalogue here. A useful habit is to inspect the new integral after each step and state why it represents progress.

Activity 9.15 · Keep track of the second subtraction

(a) Find ∫x2cosxdx with two applications of parts. (b) Verify the answer without referring to your integration working. (c) In a separate attempt, a learner gives ex(x2−2x−2) for Example 9.14. Differentiate this proposal and identify the discrepancy.

Discussion of Activity 9.15

(a) The first step is x2sinx−2∫xsinxdx. The inner integral is −xcosx+sinx, so the result is x2sinx+2xcosx−2sinx+C. (b) Its derivative is 2xsinx+x2cosx+2cosx−2xsinx−2cosx=x2cosx. (c) The proposal differentiates to ex(x2−4), missing 4ex. The last term must be +2ex, not −2ex.

9.16 Keep the boundary term in a definite integral

For continuously differentiable u and v on [a,b], integrate the product-rule identity over that interval. The derivative of uv accumulates to its endpoint difference. Therefore

∫abu(x)v′(x)dx=[u(x)v(x)]ab−∫abu′(x)v(x)dx.

The product is evaluated at both ends; the remaining integral keeps the same bounds. There is no change of variable here. You can also find a complete indefinite answer first and apply endpoint subtraction afterwards.

Example 9.15 · A boundary value that does not vanish

Evaluate ∫01xe2xdx.

Use the split from Example 9.12. The definite parts formula gives

∫01xe2xdx=[xe2x2]01−12∫01e2xdx.

The first boundary term is e2/2. The remaining integral is (e2−1)/2, so

∫01xe2xdx=e22−e2−14=e2+14.

The value is approximately 2.097. Evaluating the complete antiderivative from Example 9.12 gives the same result. Although its first term vanishes at zero, its second term equals −1/4 there; subtracting that lower value supplies the final +1/4.

Activity 9.16 · Definite parts

Evaluate (a) ∫1elnxdx; (b) ∫0π/2xsinxdx; (c) ∫01x2exdx. Use either route, but display the endpoint subtraction and predict the sign first.

Discussion of Activity 9.16

All three integrands are nonnegative on their intervals and positive inside, so their integrals are positive. (a) [xlnx−x]1e=0−(−1)=1. (b) [−xcosx+sinx]0π/2=1. (c) [ex(x2−2x+2)]01=e−2. Lower-endpoint values matter in (a) and (c), even though one or more terms individually vanish.

Substitution reverses a chain rule; parts rearranges a product rule. Both transform the calculation, and neither is selected just by counting factors. In the next lesson, we will put similar-looking integrals side by side and explain which structure makes a method useful.
