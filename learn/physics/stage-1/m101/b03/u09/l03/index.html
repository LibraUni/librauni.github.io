M101 · Unit 9 · Lesson 3

Definite substitution and changed limits

Changing the variable also changes how the start and finish of an integral are labelled.

In an indefinite integral, we returned to the original variable before giving the answer. A definite integral offers another route: keep the new variable and express the endpoints in it too. The accumulated value is the same, but every part of the notation must refer to the same variable.

Four hours: 100 minutes reading and reconstructing examples, 80 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback and correction, and 20 minutes reserved for the unit exercises.

9.9 Transport the endpoints

Consider ∫022x(1+x2)3dx. The substitution u=1+x2 gives du=2xdx, just as in the previous lesson. But the numbers 0 and 2 label values of x. The corresponding values of u are 1 and 5. The complete transformed statement is

∫022x(1+x2)3dx=∫15u3du.

It is helpful to write the endpoint correspondence before integrating: x=0 gives u=1, and x=2 gives u=5. Then [u4/4]15=(625−1)/4=156. The subtraction uses two values of the antiderivative in its own variable.

Why does changing the limits work? Suppose f is continuous on an interval containing the values of g over [a,b], and g has a continuous derivative there. Choose F′=f. The chain rule and the evaluation theorem give

∫abf(g(x))g′(x)dx=F(g(b))−F(g(a)).

Applying the evaluation theorem in the new variable gives exactly that difference from ∫g(a)g(b)f(u)du. Thus both routes compute the same quantity.

Theorem 9.4 · Definite substitution

Under the continuity conditions just stated,

∫abf(g(x))g′(x)dx=∫g(a)g(b)f(u)du.

The start maps to g(a), the finish to g(b). Preserve that order.

Activity 9.9 · Record the endpoint map first

Use substitution to evaluate (a) ∫014x(2+x2)2dx; (b) ∫0π/2cosxesinxdx; (c) ∫1edx/(xlnx). For each, decide whether the continuity conditions hold before proceeding.

Discussion of Activity 9.9

(a) u=2+x2 maps 0 to 2 and 1 to 3; 4xdx=2du. Thus 2[u3/3]23=38/3. (b) u=sinx maps 0 to 0 and π/2 to 1, giving [eu]01=e−1. (c) At x=1, lnx=0, so the integrand is undefined. The tempting new lower limit u=0 would also make 1/u undefined. Our theorem does not provide an evaluation; this is outside the proper continuous integrals studied here.

9.10 Follow orientation through a decreasing substitution

When the new variable decreases as the original variable increases, the mapped starting value exceeds the mapped finishing value. This is an oriented integral of exactly the kind introduced in Unit 8. Keep those bounds first; if you later reverse them, introduce the corresponding minus sign.

Example 9.9 · Two signs with different jobs

Evaluate ∫01(4−2x)3dx.

Choose u=4−2x. Then du=−2dx, so dx=−du/2. The starting endpoint x=0 becomes u=4; the finish x=1 becomes u=2. Therefore

∫01(4−2x)3dx=−12∫42u3du.

We may evaluate in this order, obtaining −12(24/4−44/4)=30. Or reverse the limits once:

−12∫42u3du=12∫24u3du=30.

The first minus sign came from the differential; the second came from reversing the bounds. The original integrand lies between 8 and 64 over an interval of width 1, so the positive result 30 has a plausible size.

The words “lower limit” and “upper limit” refer to positions in the notation, not necessarily the smaller and larger numbers. Sorting the bounds automatically would discard information. The definition of the substitution tells us which endpoint comes first.

Activity 9.10 · Preserve the signs

Evaluate (a) ∫02e3−xdx using u=3−x; (b) ∫0π/2sinxcos2xdx using u=cosx; (c) ∫20e3−xdx. In each case, write a sign prediction before calculating.

Discussion of Activity 9.10

(a) The integrand is positive with forward bounds. The transformation gives −∫31eudu=e3−e. (b) The integrand is nonnegative. Since du=−sinxdx, the result is −∫10u2du=1/3. (c) Reversing the original interval gives e−e3<0. Direct substitution gives −∫13eudu, the same value.

9.11 Two consistent routes to the answer

You can choose whether to change the limits or return to the original variable. Both methods follow the same chain-rule identity. The dangerous step is mixing a function in one variable with endpoint values belonging to another.

Example 9.10 · Evaluate a logarithmic integral twice

Find ∫122x/(x2+3)dx.

Route A: change the bounds. With u=x2+3, du=2xdx, and the bounds become 4 and 7. Thus

∫47duu=[lnu]47=ln7−ln4=ln74.

Route B: return to the original variable. The indefinite calculation gives ln(x2+3)+C. Evaluate this function at the original endpoints:

[ln(x2+3)]12=ln7−ln4.

Both routes work because the antiderivative and endpoints use matching variables. The expression [lnu]12 would instead give ln2: it evaluates a different interval. The original denominator is positive throughout [1,2], so the continuity conditions are satisfied.

A simple magnitude check can be obtained without a second integration. Here 2x/(x2+3) is positive and stays below 1 because x2−2x+3=(x−1)2+2>0. Over an interval of width 1, its integral lies between 0 and 1. The exact value ln(7/4), approximately 0.560, passes that check. Such a check supports the result but is too broad to choose between every plausible answer.

Activity 9.11 · Locate the mixed-variable error

A solution to ∫031+xdx says: “Let u=1+x; then the answer is [2u3/2/3]03=23.” Repair the solution in both ways. Then explain why adding an arbitrary constant cannot correct the error.

Discussion of Activity 9.11

The transformed bounds are 1 and 4, giving [2u3/2/3]14=(2/3)(8−1)=14/3. Alternatively use [2(1+x)3/2/3]03=14/3. The arithmetic for the quoted bounds is sound: (2/3)33/2=23 is correct for those wrong bounds, so the defect is the unchanged bounds. Any constant cancels in the endpoint difference and cannot repair the interval.

9.12 Check the whole path of the substitution

The proof in Section 9.9 did not require g to be increasing. In fact it did not require it to be one-to-one. The identity applies to the entire product f(g(x))g′(x). If the new variable goes forward and then back, the signs in g′ account for that retracing.

Example 9.11 · Equal mapped endpoints

Evaluate ∫−112xex2dx using u=x2.

Both endpoints map to 1, so the theorem gives ∫11eudu=0. To see what has happened, split at x=0. On the first half, u decreases from 1 to 0; on the second, it increases from 0 to 1:

∫10eudu+∫01eudu=(1−e)+(e−1)=0.

Equivalently, the antiderivative is ex2, with equal values at −1 and 1. The original rate is negative on the first half and positive on the second. The cancellation is already present in the original problem.

Now remove the factor 2x, and consider ∫−11ex2dx. It is a positive integral. The previous transformation no longer applies because its differential factor is missing. Equal endpoint images alone do not determine an integral. Substitution requires an equality of whole integrands and differentials, not just a map of two numbers.

Activity 9.12 · Decide whether the transformation is valid

(a) Evaluate ∫−22x/(1+x2)dx by substitution, and confirm the sign cancellation. (b) Explain why ∫−22dx/(1+x2) cannot be evaluated as 12∫55du/u. (c) Someone transforms ∫−222x/(x2−1)dx into ∫33du/u=0. Which condition fails?

Discussion of Activity 9.12

(a) u=1+x2 gives xdx=du/2, so the result is zero. Splitting at zero gives 12ln(1/5)+12ln5=0. (b) The factor x needed for du=2xdx is absent; the claimed transformation changes the integrand. The original continuous integrand is positive, so its integral is positive. (c) The original function is undefined at −1 and 1, and the path u=x2−1 passes through zero. The continuous-integrand hypothesis fails even though the mapped endpoints agree.

Definite substitution is now more than a rule for changing two labels. It preserves a derivative relationship, transports start and finish in order, and requires the functions to remain valid along the interval. Next we turn to products for which the chain-rule pattern is absent.
