M101 · Unit 9 · Lesson 2

Substitution as a reversed chain rule

A composite expression becomes manageable when its inner derivative is accounted for.

At the end of Lesson 1, differentiating a guessed power produced an extra factor. That factor is the central clue in substitution. Instead of ignoring it, we look for an integrand in which it already appears, perhaps multiplied by a constant. We can then express the calculation using the inner function as a new variable.

Four hours: 100 minutes reading and reconstructing examples, 80 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback and correction, and 20 minutes reserved for the closing exercises.

9.5 See the whole chain-rule pattern

Differentiate (1+x3)4. The outside power supplies 4(1+x3)3, and the inside supplies 3x2. Their product is 12x2(1+x3)3. Read the statement backwards: the original expression is an antiderivative of that product.

The recognisable structure has two parts. One part is a function of the inner expression 1+x3; the other is its derivative 3x2. The power of the inner expression could be replaced by a sine, an exponential or a reciprocal without changing the reasoning. What makes the method work is the relationship between those parts.

Result 9.3 · Reverse the chain rule

If F′=f, then on an interval where the composition is differentiable,

ddxF(g(x))=f(g(x))g′(x),

and hence

∫f(g(x))g′(x)dx=F(g(x))+C.

Both the inner expression and its derivative matter.

Example 9.5 · Match an inner expression

Find ∫x2(1+x3)4dx.

The inner expression has derivative 3x2; the factor outside is x2, one third of it. A power 5 will differentiate to a power 4. Try A(1+x3)5, where the coefficient A is to be determined. Differentiating gives 15Ax2(1+x3)4, so A=1/15. Therefore

∫x2(1+x3)4dx=(1+x3)515+C.

The derivative check both finds and verifies the coefficient. Expanding the fourth power would also work, but would introduce several terms without adding insight.

Activity 9.5 · Identify before calculating

For each integrand, state a useful inner expression, its derivative and any compensating constant. Then find an antiderivative: (a) xex2+2; (b) cosxsin3x; (c) (2x−1)(x2−x+4)2. Work in radians for (b).

Discussion of Activity 9.5

(a) Inner x2+2, derivative 2x: the answer is ex2+2/2+C. (b) Inner sinx, derivative cosx: use sin4x/4+C. (c) Inner x2−x+4, derivative 2x−1: use (x2−x+4)3/3+C. Differentiating the answers gives the stated outer function times its inner derivative.

9.6 Give the inner expression a name

Recognition is easier to organise when we write u=g(x). Since du/dx=g′(x), substitution uses the notation du=g′(x)dx to record the factor that accompanies a change of variable inside the integral. This notation is justified by the chain rule above: it is a compact way to keep the transformation together.

For Example 9.5, set u=1+x3. Then du=3x2dx, so the combination x2dx becomes du/3. The original integral becomes

∫x2(1+x3)4dx=13∫u4du=u515+C.

The expression is now integrated with respect to u. To answer the original question, replace u with 1+x3. Notice that we transformed the combination x2dx; there was no need to divide by x2, a step that would create an unnecessary difficulty at zero.

Example 9.6 · A decreasing inner function

Find ∫x5−x2dx on −5<x<5.

The square root suggests u=5−x2. Its differential is du=−2xdx, so xdx=−du/2. Keep that minus sign throughout:

∫x5−x2dx=−12∫u1/2du=−13u3/2+C.

Returning to the original variable gives −(5−x2)3/2/3+C. Differentiate it:

−13·32(5−x2)1/2(−2x)=x5−x2.

The two minus signs cancel. The stated interval keeps the inner expression positive and the differentiation straightforward.

A complete substitution has three consistent ingredients: the new expression, its differential and the integrand rewritten in the new variable. An integral such as ∫xudu is unfinished if x is still a variable rather than an expressed function of u. In the example, the factor x belonged with dx, so both disappeared together.

Activity 9.6 · Write every stage

Use explicit substitution, returning to the original variable and checking the derivative: (a) ∫3x2cos(x3−2)dx; (b) ∫x/(4+x2)2dx; (c) ∫sinxecosxdx.

Discussion of Activity 9.6

(a) u=x3−2, du=3x2dx, so ∫cosudu=sin(x3−2)+C. (b) u=4+x2, du=2xdx, so 12∫u−2du=−1/[2(4+x2)]+C. Its derivative is (1/2)(4+x2)−2(2x). (c) u=cosx, du=−sinxdx, so the answer is −ecosx+C. Its derivative is −ecosx(−sinx). All three hold for real x.

9.7 Recognise a logarithmic derivative

A particularly useful pattern occurs when the derivative of a denominator appears in the numerator. Set u=g(x) in g′(x)/g(x). The transformed integral is ∫du/u, whose answer is ln|u|+C. Returning to x gives

∫g′(x)g(x)dx=ln|g(x)|+C.

Use an interval on which g is differentiable and nonzero. The absolute value collects the positive and negative cases of the logarithm from Lesson 1. It does not make the formula valid at a zero of g.

Example 9.7 · A numerator that matches exactly

Find ∫(2x+3)/(x2+3x+5)dx.

The derivative of the denominator is 2x+3, so take u=x2+3x+5. Then the integral becomes ∫du/u. Here the denominator is always positive because

x2+3x+5=(x+32)2+114>0.

Thus the answer is ln(x2+3x+5)+C for all real x. Differentiating the logarithm produces the reciprocal of its argument multiplied by 2x+3, which is the original integrand.

Do not apply this pattern merely because a fraction is present. For 1/(x2+3x+5), the numerator is not a constant multiple of 2x+3, so this immediate logarithmic step is unavailable. That observation is a statement about the proposed method, not a proof that no antiderivative exists.

Activity 9.7 · Logarithms and their intervals

Find (a) ∫x/(x2+9)dx; (b) ∫(2x−4)/(x2−4x+3)dx; (c) ∫dx/(xlnx), for x>1. For (b), identify the excluded points and state how the constant is interpreted between them.

Discussion of Activity 9.7

(a) Use u=x2+9, giving ln(x2+9)/2+C. (b) Use u=x2−4x+3=(x−1)(x−3), giving ln|x2−4x+3|+C. The intervals are (−∞,1), (1,3) and (3,∞); their constants need not agree. (c) Use u=lnx, du=dx/x. Since x>1, u>0, and the result is ln(lnx)+C. Its chain-rule derivative is (1/lnx)(1/x).

9.8 Rewrite all remaining factors

Sometimes a useful substitution does not absorb every factor immediately. We can still proceed if the remaining factors can be expressed simply in the new variable. Linear substitutions are especially convenient because their inverse relationship is easy to solve.

Example 9.8 · One factor remains

Find ∫xx+2dx for x>−2.

Choose u=x+2 to simplify the root. Then du=dx and x=u−2. Replace the remaining factor x as well:

∫xx+2dx=∫(u−2)u1/2du.

Expand the product in the new variable:

∫(u3/2−2u1/2)du.

The result is

25u5/2−43u3/2+C.

In the original variable, this is

25(x+2)5/2−43(x+2)3/2+C.

For the check, differentiate and factor out x+2. The derivative is (x+2)3/2−2(x+2)1/2. Factoring gives [(x+2)−2]x+2, which simplifies to xx+2. Expressing every factor in u was the step that made the calculation elementary.

The method is a choice, not an instruction to use substitution whenever a root appears. Try the rewriting and inspect the resulting integral. If it becomes harder, reconsider the choice. In later lessons we will compare substitution with another technique; for now, give a reason why your chosen new variable improves the expression.

Activity 9.8 · Complete and compare

(a) Use u=x+1 to integrate x/x+1, for x>−1, and check the result. (b) A proposed substitution for ∫xex2dx gives ∫xeudu. Identify the missing transformation and repair it. (c) Explain why u=x2 does not immediately turn ∫ex2dx into ∫eudu.

Discussion of Activity 9.8

(a) x=u−1 and dx=du, so integrate u1/2−u−1/2. The answer is 23(x+1)3/2−2x+1+C. Its derivative is x+1−1/x+1=x/x+1. (b) du=2xdx, so xdx=du/2; use ex2/2+C. (c) The differential introduces 2x, which is absent in the original integrand. Replacing only the exponent and the letter after the integral changes the problem. This substitution has not supplied an elementary evaluation.

Substitution makes a chain-rule relationship visible and keeps its factors organised. The final derivative check is performed in the original variable. Next we will see how the same change of variable transports the endpoints of a definite integral.
