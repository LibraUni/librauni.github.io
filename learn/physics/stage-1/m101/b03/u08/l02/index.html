M101 · Unit 8 · Lesson 2

Definite integrals and signed accumulation

A definite integral makes the limiting total precise, while preserving the signs and units of its contributions.

The two rectangle estimates in Lesson 1 closed around a single value. We now give that limiting value a notation that works for much more than a linear flow. We will also learn to reason about integrals before finding a formula for them.

Four hours: 90 minutes reading and examples, 90 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback, and 20 minutes reserved for the closing exercises.

Use the practice allocation actively: after studying each worked example, cover its solution and reconstruct the method on paper, then attempt the numbered activities. Use the feedback time to locate and correct the first difference in your reasoning.

8.5 A limit that defines a total

Let f be continuous on a finite closed interval [a,b], with a<b. Partition the interval into pieces, choose one sample ci in each piece, and form the sum ∑f(ci)Δxi. As every piece becomes small, these sums approach one number. We call that number the definite integral of f from a to b, and write

∫abf(x)dx.

The function f is the integrand. The lower limit a and upper limit b specify the interval and its orientation. The dx identifies the input whose small widths were used. The integral sign gathers the contributions, just as the sigma sign gathered a finite list.

The limiting requirement concerns the largest piece: it must tend to zero. Increasing the number of pieces while leaving one large piece unchanged does not refine the entire interval. The sample can be at the left endpoint, the right endpoint or elsewhere within its piece; for a continuous function, all such refinements have the same limit.

Definition 8.3 · Definite integral

For continuous f on [a,b], a<b,

∫abf(x)dx=limmaxiΔxi→0∑i=1nf(ci)Δxi,ci∈[xi−1,xi].

Continuity on this closed interval guarantees existence of the common limit. It is a sufficient condition, not a claim that every integrable function must be continuous.

A finite sum remains an approximation to this limit, unless some additional reasoning shows equality. For the constant function f(x)=k, every sum is exactly k(b−a): all the widths add to b−a. Thus

∫abkdx=k(b−a).

Lesson 1 established ∫04(2+t)dt=16. The letter inside the integral is a temporary label for the sampled input. Replacing every occurrence of t inside it by u gives the same number. The limits and the rule being accumulated remain the same.

Activity 8.5 · A number or a function?

Evaluate ∫273dx. Does the answer still depend on x? Is ∫273dt different? Explain what information would change if the upper limit 7 were replaced by a variable endpoint.

Discussion of Activity 8.5

The integral is 3(7−2)=15. Both limits are fixed, so the result is a number. Changing the integration letter gives the same number. A variable endpoint would change the interval as that endpoint changes, producing a function. We develop that construction in Lesson 3.

8.6 The meaning of a signed integral

For a<b, each width in the sum is positive. A negative integrand therefore contributes a negative product. Geometrically, regions above the horizontal axis contribute positively and regions below contribute negatively. The integral records their signed balance.

Take f(x)=x−1 on [0,3], with dimensionless axes. The graph crosses the axis at 1. On [0,1] it forms a triangle below the axis with base 1 and height 1; on [1,3] it forms a triangle above with base 2 and height 2. Hence

∫03(x−1)dx=−12(1)(1)+12(2)(2)=32.

The total geometric area between the graph and the axis is instead 1/2+2=5/2. The difference is not a computational discrepancy. The questions ask for different combinations of the same contributions. The signed integral is the construction that recovers a net change from a signed rate.

Example 8.3 · A net change of zero

A vessel has net inflow q(t)=3−t L/min for 0≤t≤6, where t is the numerical time in minutes. Find the net change in stored volume using geometry.

The positive triangle over the first three minutes contributes 3(3)/2=4.5 L. The negative triangle over the next three contributes −4.5 L. The net change is zero. The volume first rises and then falls to its starting value; a zero integral does not imply a zero rate throughout the interval. Assume the vessel has enough water and capacity for this supplied model to apply.

The units of an integral are integrand units times input units. A rate in L/min integrated over minutes gives litres. A linear density in kg/m integrated over metres gives kilograms. Even when a graph uses geometric area to represent the calculation, the physical answer carries the product of the axis units.

Activity 8.6 · Check a claim from a graph

A continuous graph on [0,5] consists of straight segments through (0,0), (2,4), (3,0) and (5,−2). Sketch it. Find its signed integral and its total geometric area. Explain why the two differ.

Hint for Activity 8.6

The two positive triangles can be combined into one triangle with base 3 and height 4.

Discussion of Activity 8.6

The positive area is 3(4)/2=6, and the negative region has area 2(2)/2=2. The signed integral is 6−2=4; the total geometric area is 6+2=8. The integral retains the negative sign on the last interval.

8.7 Reversing and joining intervals

Accumulating a change from an earlier state to a later one reverses sign when we compare the states in the opposite order. We extend the integral notation accordingly:

∫baf(x)dx=−∫abf(x)dx,∫aaf(x)dx=0.

The same function is being accumulated; only the direction of the comparison changes. For example, a constant positive rate gives a negative integral when the upper limit is smaller than the lower limit. The formula k(b−a) already includes this sign.

We can also join adjacent intervals. A partition over [a,c] and a partition over [c,b] together partition the whole interval. Adding the two finite sums and then taking their limits gives

∫abf(x)dx=∫acf(x)dx+∫cbf(x)dx.

This additivity also holds when the three points occur in another order, provided the integrals exist over the intervals involved. Reversal supplies the required signs. An intermediate stop that lies beyond the final point adds a contribution which the return leg then subtracts.

Example 8.4 · Recovering a missing interval

Suppose ∫15f(x)dx=9 and ∫13f(x)dx=4. Find ∫35f(x)dx and ∫53f(x)dx.

Additivity gives 9=4+∫35f(x)dx, so the missing integral is 5. Reversing its limits gives −5. We did not need a formula for f, only consistent interval bookkeeping.

Activity 8.7 · Direction matters

You know ∫−24g(x)dx=7 and ∫14g(x)dx=−3. Find ∫−21g(x)dx, ∫4−2g(x)dx, and ∫11g(x)dx.

Discussion of Activity 8.7

Writing 7=I+(−3) gives I=10. The reversed full integral is −7, and the zero-width integral is 0. A positive full integral is compatible with a negative contribution on part of the interval.

8.8 Combining integrands and bounding totals

Suppose two processes contribute rates f(x) and g(x). On each small interval, their combined contribution is [f(ci)+g(ci)]Δxi. Distributing the multiplication and adding shows that the total is the sum of the two totals. Multiplying every rate by a constant similarly multiplies every contribution by that constant. Thus integration is linear:

∫ab[αf(x)+βg(x)]dx=α∫abf(x)dx+β∫abg(x)dx.

Here α and β are constants and the integrals must exist. A product of two functions cannot generally be separated in this way. For example, on [0,2], the integral of the constant product 1×1 is 2, while the product of the two separate integrals is 2×2=4.

Bounds are another consequence of the sums. If m≤f(x)≤M throughout [a,b], with a<b, every contribution lies between mΔxi and MΔxi. Since the widths sum to b−a,

m(b−a)≤∫abf(x)dx≤M(b−a).

This can test an answer before any exact integration. If a flow remains between 2 and 5 L/s for six seconds, its delivered volume is between 12 and 30 L. An answer of 50 L contradicts the supplied bounds. The condition that the rate stays between those values throughout the interval is stronger than seeing two endpoint readings.

Activity 8.8 · Use what is known

On [0,2], suppose ∫02f(x)dx=3 and ∫02g(x)dx=−1. Find ∫02[2f(x)−3g(x)]dx. Separately, if 1≤h(x)≤4 on this interval, could its integral be 9? Explain both answers.

Discussion of Activity 8.8

Linearity gives 2(3)−3(−1)=9. For the different function h, the bounds give 2≤∫02h(x)dx≤8, so 9 is impossible. The first calculation says nothing about whether 2f−3g satisfies the stated bounds on h.

These properties let us interpret, rearrange and check definite integrals without yet possessing a general evaluation method. The next question is more revealing: what happens to the accumulated total when we move its upper endpoint? That will reconnect the interval construction with the local derivative.
