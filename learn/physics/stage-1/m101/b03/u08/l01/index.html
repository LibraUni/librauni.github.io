M101 · Unit 8 · Lesson 1

From rates to finite sums

A changing rate can be turned into a total by dividing the interval into pieces small enough to examine.

In Unit 7 a tangent estimated a small change by multiplying a local rate by an input change. We will use that idea repeatedly across an interval. Each product estimates one contribution; their sum estimates the accumulated change. The decisions about interval widths and sample points will remain visible throughout the calculation.

Four hours including the block and unit introductions: 90 minutes reading and examples, 90 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback, and 20 minutes reserved for the unit exercises.

Use the practice allocation actively: after studying each worked example, cover its solution and reconstruct the method on paper, then attempt the numbered activities. Use the feedback time to locate and correct the first difference in your reasoning.

8.1 Products that measure contributions

A pump delivering 3 litres each second supplies 12 litres in four seconds if its rate stays constant. The multiplication includes the units: litres per second times seconds gives litres. On a graph with time on the horizontal axis and flow rate on the vertical axis, the same product is represented by the area of a rectangle. Its width represents duration and its height represents rate.

Now suppose the rate changes. Let q(t) denote the flow rate at time t. During a short interval of duration Δt, choose a time t* in that interval and use the rate at that time as a representative value. The estimated delivery is

ΔV≈q(t*)Δt.

The subscript star labels the chosen sample time. It does not mean multiplication. The approximation comes from holding a changing rate fixed during this short interval. If the rate is actually constant there, the product is exact. If it varies, the product may be too large or too small.

Key idea 8.1 · A small contribution

A representative rate multiplied by an interval width estimates the change over that interval. Track the sign and the units before adding contributions.

Example 8.1 · Unequal durations

A controlled pump delivers 2 L/s for the first three seconds, then 5 L/s for the next two seconds. How much water is delivered?

There are two constant-rate intervals. Their widths differ, so calculate the products separately:

V=(2×3)+(5×2)=16 L.

The first interval contributes 6 L and the second 10 L. The unweighted mean of the two rates is 3.5 L/s, but multiplying that by five seconds would give 17.5 L. That method gives the shorter, faster interval too much influence. The durations are essential information.

Activity 8.1 · What is being added?

A delivery rate is 4 L/min for 30 s, then 1 L/min for 90 s. Calculate the total delivered. Explain why adding the two rates does not answer the question.

Discussion of Activity 8.1

Convert the durations to 0.5 min and 1.5 min. The total is 4(0.5)+1(1.5)=3.5 L. The sum of the rates, 5 L/min, still has rate units; multiplying each rate by its own duration converts it into a contribution to the volume.

A rate can also be negative. If q is a vessel's net inflow, a negative value means that outflow exceeds inflow. The product then estimates a decrease in the stored volume. Keeping the signs lets the same addition account for gains and losses.

8.2 Building and reading a finite sum

For a changing rate, we may need many intervals. Name their endpoints t0,t1,…,tn, in increasing order. These points partition the full interval into n pieces. The first piece runs from t0 to t1; the last runs from tn−1 to tn. There are n+1 endpoints because adjacent pieces share an endpoint.

The width of piece i is Δti=ti−ti−1. Choose a sample ci within it. Adding the estimated contributions gives

V≈q(c1)Δt1+q(c2)Δt2+⋯+q(cn)Δtn=∑i=1nq(ci)Δti.

The capital Greek letter sigma, ∑, means “add these terms”. The index i takes each integer from 1 through n. For each value of the index, use the sample and width belonging to the same piece. Sigma notation shortens a list of additions without changing the arithmetic.

Read ∑i=1n as “sum over all the pieces, from piece 1 to piece n”. Expand a short sum whenever the indexing becomes unclear.

Example 8.2 · A table of contributions

A model uses endpoints 0, 1, 3 and 4 minutes. Representative net rates in those intervals are 6, −2 and 3 L/min. Estimate the change in stored volume.

The widths are 1, 2 and 1 minutes. Pairing each width with its rate gives

∑i=13q(ci)Δti=6(1)+(−2)(2)+3(1)=5 L.

The middle interval removes 4 L; the other two add 9 L. The net change is a gain of 5 L. To find the final stored volume we would still need the initial volume. If the values are only representative samples, the 5 L is an estimate. It is exact for a model that holds each listed rate constant throughout its piece.

Activity 8.2 · Read the index

Expand and evaluate ∑i=14(2+i). Then expand ∑i=03(2+i/2)(1/2). What does the outside factor one-half do if the second sum describes a rate in litres per minute sampled at half-minute intervals?

Discussion of Activity 8.2

The first sum is 3+4+5+6=18. The second is (2+2.5+3+3.5)/2=5.5. The factor one-half is the interval width in minutes; it changes each rate into a volume contribution. The different starting indices change which samples are used.

With equal widths over [a,b], the width is Δt=(b−a)/n. The endpoints are ti=a+iΔt. Equal widths make the arithmetic convenient, although the underlying construction also works with unequal widths.

8.3 Left and right rectangle estimates

Consider the increasing flow model q(t)=2+t, with time measured in seconds and the numerical rate in L/s, over 0≤t≤4. Divide the interval into four pieces of width one second. Sampling at the left endpoint gives rates 2, 3, 4 and 5. Sampling at the right endpoint gives 3, 4, 5 and 6.

L4=(2+3+4+5)(1)=14 L.
R4=(3+4+5+6)(1)=18 L.

The subscripts record the number of pieces. The left estimate is smaller because each left-endpoint value is the smallest rate in its interval. The right estimate uses the largest rate. Hence the actual accumulated delivery for this model lies between 14 and 18 L. This conclusion comes from the known increase throughout every interval, not merely from four increasing readings.

For a general function f over [a,b], write the equal-width sums as

Ln=Δx∑i=0n−1f(a+iΔx).
Rn=Δx∑i=1nf(a+iΔx),Δx=b−an.

The left sum includes a and excludes b; the right sum excludes a and includes b. Each has exactly n terms. Using both endpoints with full width would count n+1 rectangles over an interval that contains only n pieces.

Activity 8.3 · A decreasing rate

Use three equal pieces on [0,3] for the positive rate q(t)=7−t, with seconds and L/s as before. Find both endpoint sums. Decide which gives the larger estimate and explain why.

Hint for Activity 8.3

Write the four endpoint rates first. Select three for each sum.

Discussion of Activity 8.3

The rates are 7, 6, 5 and 4. Thus L3=18 L and R3=15 L. The left sum is now larger: a decreasing rate makes each left endpoint the maximum on its piece. The endpoint names do not determine which estimate is larger; the function's behaviour does.

If a graph rises and then falls, some left rectangles may overestimate while others underestimate. Their errors may reinforce or cancel. You must inspect the behaviour within the pieces before claiming upper and lower bounds.

8.4 Refinement and what the evidence says

Return to q(t)=2+t on [0,4]. With eight pieces, each width is 0.5 s. The endpoint sums become 15 L and 17 L. Sixteen pieces give 15.5 L and 16.5 L. Both estimates move towards 16 L, and the gap between the bounds becomes smaller.

We can explain that pattern for every positive integer n, using a finite-sum identity. The sum of the integers from 0 to n−1 is n(n−1)/2. To see why, write the list once forwards and once backwards. Each paired column sums to n−1, and there are n columns. Divide by two because the original sum was counted twice. For our left sum,

Ln=4n∑i=0n−1(2+4in)=8+16n2n(n−1)2=16−8n.

The right sum similarly gives Rn=16+8/n. The accumulated delivery lies between them because the rate increases. Both bounds approach 16 as n increases, so they pin down a unique limiting total.

Key idea 8.2 · From estimates towards a total

Refining the partition means making every interval width small. For a continuous rate on a closed finite interval, rectangle sums approach a common total, independent of where each sample is taken. The next lesson makes this limiting total our definition of a definite integral.

Activity 8.4 · How many pieces?

For the same linear flow model, choose the smallest integer n that makes the gap between the left and right bounds at most 0.2 L. What error bound follows for either endpoint estimate?

Discussion of Activity 8.4

The gap is Rn−Ln=16/n L. Requiring 16/n≤0.2 gives n≥80. The exact formulas place the total at 16 L and each endpoint estimate at distance 8/n, at most 0.1 L. Without using the special symmetry of these formulas, the gap alone would still bound the error of either endpoint estimate by 0.2 L.

For a measured flow record, extra subdivisions do not create extra measurements. We would have to choose how to model the flow between recorded times. In the model calculation above, the function supplies a rate at every sample time. That distinction matters whenever we turn a sum into evidence about a physical total.

We have now built the essential construction: multiply a representative value by an interval width, add the contributions, and examine refinement. Next we give its limit a name and learn how signs and interval direction belong to that name.
