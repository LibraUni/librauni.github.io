M101 · Block 3 · Unit 10

Integral models, signs and dimensions

A changing rate can describe water passing a measuring station, a trolley moving along a track or a force acting through a displacement. Integration brings small contributions together. The important first decision is what those contributions should represent.

From a question to an integral

Suppose a trolley travels away from its starting point and then returns. Its final displacement is zero, although its wheels have covered a positive distance. Both quantities can be calculated from its velocity. The difference lies in what we do with the negative contributions during the return journey. A technically correct integration can answer the wrong question if we have not made that distinction first.

Units 8 and 9 established how a definite integral accumulates signed contributions and how an antiderivative can evaluate it. Here you will build the integral from the question. You will identify a small contribution, decide whether its sign matters, choose the interval, and check that the resulting units describe the requested quantity. These decisions connect mathematical operations with the geometry or supplied physical model they represent.

We begin with the distinction between signed and geometric area. It provides a visible basis for studying displacement and distance. We then ask which constant value would produce the same accumulation as a varying function: this gives the mean value of a function and makes sense of average flow and average velocity. Finally, thin cross-sections allow us to calculate simple volumes, and a supplied force model lets us interpret work as an accumulation over position.

The examples use bounded intervals and methods already available from Units 8 and 9. Recall how to find zeros, construct a sign chart, differentiate an antiderivative to check it, and evaluate its endpoint difference. Unit 2's distinction between a signed component and a magnitude will be especially useful. All physical relationships needed here are supplied; P101 develops their physical foundations.

By the end, you should be able to write and explain an integral model, split an interval when a sign or boundary changes, distinguish a change from a final amount, and test an answer using dimensions, bounds and simple limiting cases. You will also use familiar Python functions and plots to inspect models, with algebra settling the sign changes that a sparse plot might miss.

The unit has 18 hours, including two hours of Python in Lessons 3 and 5. The lesson budgets include reading, reconstruction of examples, activities and correction. They also reserve time for the cumulative exercises at the end of Lesson 5. Keep a short record of your modelling choices as you work: the quantity sought, the small contribution, the interval and the checks. Use the closing web table and compact reference PDF for revision.

Our first task is to decide how contributions on opposite sides of an axis should combine. That choice will recur throughout the unit, even when the axes no longer represent two lengths.
