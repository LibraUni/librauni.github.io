M101 · Unit 10 · Lesson 3

Mean values and accumulated flow

What constant rate would deliver the same total as a changing rate? Answering that question gives a useful definition of an average. We will use it to connect a flow record, the volume passing a point and the amount stored in a vessel.

Four hours: 70 minutes reading and reconstructing examples, 50 minutes written activities, 40 minutes feedback, 60 minutes Python, and 20 minutes reserved for closing exercises.

10.9 A constant with the same total

Suppose a delivery rate q(t) varies over [a,b]. A constant rate c sustained for the same duration would deliver c(b−a). To make that equal to the varying-rate delivery, set c(b−a)=∫abq(t)dt. Dividing by the positive duration gives the constant we seek.

The construction does not depend on the function being a rate. For any continuous function, its mean value over an interval is the constant height with the same signed integral over that interval. We write it as f―, read “f bar”.

Definition 10.3 · Mean value on an interval

f―=1b−a∫abf(x)dx,a<b.

The mean has the same units as f. If m≤f(x)≤M throughout the interval, then m≤f―≤M.

This agrees with the familiar arithmetic mean when we think in terms of many equally spaced samples. Divide the interval into n equal widths Δx=(b−a)/n. The sum approximating the integral, divided by the interval length, is

∑i=1nf(xi)Δxb−a=1n∑i=1nf(xi).

As the intervals become finer, this sample mean approaches the integral mean. Equal widths matter: each sampled value represents an equal portion of the interval. Unevenly spaced readings would need their represented widths taken into account.

Example 10.7 · An equivalent constant delivery

A pump has numerical flow rate q(t)=2+t2 L/min on 0≤t≤3, where t is in minutes. Find the delivered volume and mean flow rate.

The rate is positive, so it describes inflow throughout. Its total is

∫03(2+t2)dt=[2t+t33]03=15 L.

Divide by the duration, 3 min, to obtain q―=5 L/min. A constant delivery of 5 L/min for three minutes supplies the same 15 L. Since the actual rate ranges from 2 to 11 L/min, the mean lies within that range.

Averaging just the endpoint values gives (2+11)/2=6.5 L/min, which is too high. The curve spends more of the interval at its lower values than that endpoint average represents. The interval mean accounts for the entire curve.

The horizontal mean line balances signed departures from it: ∫ab(f−f―)dx=0. The portions above the line compensate for the portions below. This is a useful interpretation even when the original function takes negative values.

Activity 10.10 · Average height and average slope

Find the mean of f(x)=1+x2 on [0,3]. Compare it with the average rate of change (f(3)−f(0))/3. Explain what each number describes.

Discussion of Activity 10.10

The integral is [x+x3/3]03=12, so the mean value is 4. The average rate of change is (10−1)/3=3. The first is a mean height; the second is the secant slope. If x carried a unit, the second would have units of f per unit x, while the first would retain the units of f.

10.10 Averages depend on the interval

A mean belongs to a function and an interval. If a process spends one minute at 8 L/min and three minutes at 2 L/min, it delivers 8×1+2×3=14 L in four minutes. Its mean is therefore 3.5 L/min. The unweighted mean of the two rates, 5 L/min, would describe equal durations, which this process does not have.

This example is piecewise constant. Integrate its two constant pieces separately; the instantaneous change between them has no effect on the total. More generally, if adjacent intervals have lengths L1,L2 and respective means m1,m2, the total accumulation is m1L1+m2L2. Dividing by the combined length gives the weighted mean.

Example 10.8 · Average velocity and average speed

For the five-second trolley journey in Lesson 2, the displacement was 5 m and the distance 13 m. Its average velocity is 5/5=1 m/s. Its average speed is 13/5=2.6 m/s.

The first equals 15∫05v(t)dt; the second equals 15∫05|v(t)|dt. A constant velocity of 1 m/s gives the same endpoint change. A constant speed of 2.6 m/s gives the same travelled distance. Taking the absolute value of the average velocity gives 1 m/s and cannot recover the average speed, because the earlier signed integral already cancelled opposite travel.

Activity 10.11 · Duration is a weight

  1. A rate has mean 3 L/min for the first two minutes and mean 7 L/min for the next minute. Find the total delivery and the mean over all three minutes.
  2. A particle returns to its starting point after ten seconds, covering 18 m. Find its average velocity and average speed.
  3. For f(x)=2+x, show that its mean on [1,5] equals its value at the midpoint. Does Example 10.7 support using the midpoint for every function?
Discussion of Activity 10.11

(a) The total is 3×2+7×1=13 L, so the combined mean is 13/3 L/min. (b) Average velocity is zero; average speed is 1.8 m/s. (c) The integral is [2x+x2/2]15=20, giving mean 5, equal to f(3). In Example 10.7 the midpoint rate is q(1.5)=4.25 L/min, whereas the mean is 5. The midpoint shortcut works for affine functions but not in general.

10.11 Flow and stored amount

A flow rate describes volume crossing a boundary per unit time. To model the amount in a vessel, count inflow positively and outflow negatively. If qin(t) and qout(t) are nonnegative rates, the net rate is q(t)=qin(t)−qout(t). Assuming these account for every transfer, the supplied balance model is V′(t)=q(t).

Thus the stored volume at time t is the initial volume plus the accumulated net rate. This distinguishes three potentially useful quantities: total inflow, total outflow, and net change. The integral of the absolute net rate measures total upward and downward variation of storage; it does not in general count all fluid entering and leaving. Simultaneous inflow and outflow can cancel in the net rate.

Example 10.9 · A vessel fills, then drains

A vessel initially contains 10 L. On a six-minute interval, its supplied inflow is 6−t L/min and its outflow is 2 L/min. Its capacity is 20 L. Find its final volume, maximum volume, total inflow and total outflow.

The net rate is 4−t L/min, so

V(t)=10+∫0t(4−u)du=10+4t−t22.

At six minutes the volume is 16 L. The net rate is positive until four minutes and negative afterwards, so the maximum is V(4)=18 L. The minimum on the interval is the initial 10 L. The vessel therefore stays above empty and below capacity, consistent with the supplied rates.

Total inflow is ∫06(6−t)dt=18 L. Total outflow is ∫062dt=12 L. Their difference is 6 L, matching the change from 10 L to 16 L. The mean net rate is 1 L/min, while the mean inflow is 3 L/min. The word “mean” needs its quantity as well as its interval.

Activity 10.12 · A balance must remain possible

  1. For Example 10.9, calculate ∫06|4−t|dt. Compare it with total inflow plus total outflow. Explain why they differ.
  2. A different vessel starts with 1 L and drains at 2 L/min with no inflow. For how long can that model apply before it predicts an impossible stored amount?
Discussion of Activity 10.12

(a) Storage rises by 8 L then falls by 2 L, giving total variation 10 L. Inflow plus outflow is 30 L. Much of that transfer happens simultaneously and cancels in the net rate. (b) The formula is V(t)=1−2t, reaching zero at 0.5 min. A constant drain cannot continue to remove liquid from an empty vessel. The rate model must change there; reporting a later negative volume would overlook its physical validity.

10.12 Inspecting a model with Python

The next activity uses the function, list and plotting tools introduced in P101 and used in earlier M101 units. You need to be able to define and call a function, form a list of sample inputs, and run a notebook from a fresh start. If these are unfamiliar, revisit the P101 Python foundation before attempting the allocated computing work.

Download the unit notebook or matching Python script. Part A implements the balance model with a constant outlet rate supplied as a parameter. In numerical units of minutes and litres, the rate and volume functions are:

def net_rate(t, outlet=2.0):
    return 6.0 - t - outlet

def volume(t, outlet=2.0):
    return 10.0 + (6.0 - outlet)*t - t**2/2.0

The parameter changes the model, not the meaning of the variables. The formula is supplied for 0≤t≤6; its physical use also stops if the vessel empties or reaches its 20 L capacity. A graph of the polynomial can be drawn outside that physical range, but the resulting numbers no longer describe the stated vessel.

Activity 10.13 · Part A: predict, plot and check 60 minutes including feedback

  1. Spend 10 minutes predicting q(0),q(4),V(0),V(4),V(6) for outlet 2. Write units beside your values.
  2. Run Part A and spend 10 minutes comparing the separate rate and volume plots with those predictions. Explain the difference between a zero rate and a zero stored amount.
  3. For 20 minutes, change the outlet to 4 and then 6 L/min. Predict how the time of maximum storage changes before running the plots. Check whether the vessel remains physically possible throughout.
  4. Use 10 minutes to solve algebraically for the first empty time when the outlet is 6. Compare it with the samples adjacent to that time. State where you would stop interpreting the plot physically.
  5. Use 10 minutes to write a short conclusion, restore the default and restart/run all cells. Include one derivative check and one initial-value check.
Discussion and checks for Activity 10.13

At outlet 2, the five predictions are 4 L/min, 0 L/min, 10 L, 18 L and 16 L. Zero net rate occurs at the maximum volume, not at empty storage. At outlet 4, V(t)=10+2t−t2/2, with maximum 12 L at 2 min and final volume 4 L. The model remains within capacity and above zero.

At outlet 6, V(t)=10−t2/2 decreases immediately for positive time. It reaches zero at t=20, approximately 4.472 min. The later polynomial values are negative, so the prescribed liquid outflow cannot continue unchanged. Samples at 4.4 and 4.5 min bracket that event, but solving the equation locates it more precisely.

Differentiating the general volume formula gives 6−outlet−t, the net rate. Setting t=0 gives 10 for every outlet. Those checks validate the implemented formula; the storage bounds test the physical interval. Save your explanatory notes with your notebook.

Means and balances both begin with accumulation. Dividing by duration gives an equivalent constant rate; adding an initial value gives a stored amount.
