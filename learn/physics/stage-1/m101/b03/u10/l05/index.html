M101 · Unit 10 · Lesson 5

Model selection and unit synthesis

A complete integral solution explains what is accumulated, why the bounds are appropriate and how the result answers the question. We will bring those choices together, test a computational shortcut, and then practise selecting models across the whole unit.

Four hours: 25 minutes synthesis reading and the opening activity, 60 minutes Python, 65 minutes closing exercise attempts, 65 minutes exercise feedback and correction, and 25 minutes reference revision. The 80 minutes reserved in Lessons 1–4 bring closing attempts to 145 minutes.

10.17 Formulate before calculating

Several quite different questions can lead to the same algebraic integrand. A function 2+x might be a height, a cross-sectional area or a signed force component. Integrating it over a specified interval can then produce an area, a volume or work. The symbols alone do not decide which interpretation is correct.

Begin a solution with the quantity sought and the small contribution that builds it. A geometric strip has height times width; a volume slice has area times thickness; a change of storage has net rate times elapsed time. State the independent variable and interval. Then ask whether signed contributions should cancel. Only after those decisions should you select an antiderivative technique from Unit 9.

Example 10.15 · A model and a method together

A supplied inflow rate is q(t)=te−t L/min for numerical time 0≤t≤2 min. A vessel initially contains 3 L, has capacity 5 L and has no outflow. Find its final volume and mean inflow, checking whether the model remains possible.

The required addition is ∫02te−tdt, because inflow times time gives volume. Integration by parts, or a derivative check, gives the antiderivative −(t+1)e−t. Hence

ΔV=[−(t+1)e−t]02=1−3e−2 L.

The final volume is 4−3e−2 L, approximately 3.594 L. Mean inflow is (1−3e−2)/2 L/min, approximately 0.297 L/min. The rate is nonnegative, so storage increases; its largest value is therefore the final value, below capacity. The initial 3 L is its smallest value. These checks establish physical consistency over the entire stated interval.

There are two distinct checks of the calculation. Differentiating the antiderivative recovers te−t. Adding the initial amount and checking the storage bounds establishes the meaning and admissibility of the resulting volume. Neither check replaces the other.

Activity 10.18 · Explain the choice Within the 25-minute synthesis allocation

For each question, state the integral and its units before evaluating anything: (a) total distance for a signed velocity v(t) in m/s over 0–8 s; (b) volume of circular slices of radius r(x) in cm over 1–4 cm; (c) mean signed force F(x) in N over 2–5 m. Which needs an absolute value, which needs a square, and which needs a division by interval length?

Discussion of Activity 10.18

(a) ∫08|v(t)|dt, in metres; taking speed before accumulation prevents direction cancellation. (b) ∫14πr(x)2dx, in cm³; the square constructs section area from radius. (c) 13∫25F(x)dx, in newtons; dividing work by the 3 m interval gives mean force. Signs remain in (c), because the question asks for the mean signed component.

10.18 Audit a model in Python

A plot can reveal an unexpected sign change, but a finite set of sampled points can also miss one. The next model makes that limitation visible. The supplied velocity in m/s is v(t)=100(t−0.45)(t−0.55), for numerical time 0≤t≤1 s. Its factors locate two closely spaced reversals.

Part B of the notebook and script samples this velocity at intervals of 0.2 s. It also contains a clearly labelled provisional distance calculation. Your task is to test the interpretation, not merely to check whether the program runs.

Activity 10.19 · Part B: find what the samples miss 60 minutes including feedback

  1. Use 10 minutes to find the zeros and sign intervals algebraically. Predict the sign at each sampled time 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1 s, and at 0.5 s.
  2. Run Part B. Spend 10 minutes comparing the coarse samples with the finer plot. Explain why joining the coarse samples would miss part of the motion.
  3. For 15 minutes, check the supplied antiderivative by differentiation. Calculate the signed contribution of each of the three sign intervals, using exact fractions or sufficient precision.
  4. Spend 15 minutes repairing the provisional distance calculation by adding the magnitudes of the separate changes. Compare distance and displacement; explain why their inequality alone would not have detected the original mistake.
  5. Use the final 10 minutes to add a zero-width check and a no-reversal interval check, then restart/run all cells. Record what the algebra, plotted samples and tests each establish.
Discussion and sample repair for Activity 10.19

The zeros are 0.45 and 0.55 s. Velocity is negative between them and positive outside. All coarse samples are positive, but v(0.5)=−0.25 m/s. The derivative of F(t)=100t3/3−50t2+24.75t is exactly the expanded velocity.

The signed displacement over the whole second is 97/12 m. The middle interval contributes −1/60 m; the two outer intervals each contribute 81/20 m. Thus distance is 487/60 m, approximately 8.11667 m, while displacement is approximately 8.08333 m. A repair using the known reversal times is:

def checked_distance():
    return (abs(F(0.45) - F(0.0))
            + abs(F(0.55) - F(0.45))
            + abs(F(1.0) - F(0.55)))

The provisional code takes the magnitude of the whole signed change. Its result equals |Δs|, so the check D≥|Δs| passes even though distance is underestimated. On [0,0.4], there is no reversal and the positive endpoint difference is a valid distance. On a zero-width interval the change is zero. These are useful consistency checks, but only the sign analysis justifies where to split.

The numerical routine evaluates a verified antiderivative at known boundaries; it does not discover every root for you. Unit 11 will develop numerical integration and its checks. Here the central result is that a correct formula still needs a correct interpretation of what is being accumulated.

10.19 Unit exercises

These core exercises cover all five lessons. Allow 145 minutes for attempts, including the 80 minutes reserved earlier, then 65 minutes for feedback and correction. Work on paper before opening a hint or solution. In a model question, state the quantity, variable, interval and small contribution; include a sign, dimensional or limiting-case check wherever it helps.

Exercise 10.1 · Signed total and area

For f(x)=x2−4 on [−3,3], find the signed integral and geometric area. State where cancellation occurs.

Hint for Exercise 10.1

Find both zeros and test the sign between them.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.1

An antiderivative is x3/3−4x, giving signed integral −6. On [−2,2] the signed contribution is −32/3; the two outer pieces together contribute 14/3. Thus area is 32/3+14/3=46/3. The negative central region cancels the positive outer contributions in the signed total. The area exceeds the magnitude 6 of that total.

Return to Exercise 10.1

Exercise 10.2 · A bounded region

Find the area enclosed by y=x2 and y=x+2. Explain how you choose the bounds and the order of subtraction.

Hint for Exercise 10.2

Solve the intersection equation, then test a point between its roots.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.2

Intersections satisfy x2=x+2, giving x=−1,2. Between them the line is above the parabola, so area is ∫−12(x+2−x2)dx. Using x2/2+2x−x3/3 gives 9/2. The integrand is nonnegative on this interval, as required for strip heights.

Return to Exercise 10.2

Exercise 10.3 · Read the axes

A region has a nonnegative height function with numerical integral 12 on a graph whose horizontal axis is in metres and vertical axis in centimetres. Both coordinates measure lengths. Express the area in m². Would the same numerical integral on a graph of L/min against min describe an area?

Hint for Exercise 10.3

Multiply the axis units before converting them.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.3

The immediate unit is m·cm, so 12 mcm=0.12 m2. On a flow–time graph, the integral has units litres and describes an accumulated volume (or net volume, according to the sign convention). The plotted area interpretation does not make its physical units square metres.

Return to Exercise 10.3

Exercise 10.4 · A complete journey

Numerical velocity is v(t)=t2−4t+3 m/s on [0,4] s, with starting position 2 m. Find the reversal times, final position, displacement, distance, average velocity and average speed.

Hint for Exercise 10.4

Use the zeros of velocity to split the journey; retain the starting position separately.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.4

The roots are 1 and 3, with signs positive, negative, positive. The position is s(t)=2+t3/3−2t2+3t. At times 0,1,3,4 its values are 2,10/3,2,10/3 m. Therefore final position is 10/3 m, displacement 4/3 m and distance 4/3+4/3+4/3=4 m. Average velocity is 1/3 m/s; average speed is 1 m/s. Differentiating the position and evaluating at zero checks the reconstruction.

Return to Exercise 10.4

Exercise 10.5 · An instantaneous stop

A particle has v(t)=(t−2)2 m/s on [0,3] s. Does it reverse? Find its distance and mean speed, explaining why no sign reversal is needed in the integral.

Hint for Exercise 10.5

A square is nonnegative on both sides of its zero.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.5

It stops at 2 s but does not reverse: velocity remains nonnegative. Distance equals displacement, [(t−2)3/3]03=3 m. Mean speed is 3/3=1 m/s. The position increases through a horizontal tangent.

Return to Exercise 10.5

Exercise 10.6 · Which average?

Find the mean of f(x)=3+2x2 on [−1,2]. Compare it with the mean of its endpoint values and with its midpoint value.

Hint for Exercise 10.6

The interval length is three, not two.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.6

The integral is [3x+2x3/3]−12=15, so the mean is 5. The endpoint values are 5 and 11, whose mean is 8. The midpoint is x=1/2, with value 7/2. Neither shortcut gives the interval mean. The correct mean lies between the minimum 3 and maximum 11.

Return to Exercise 10.6

Exercise 10.7 · Unequal durations

A delivery rate has mean 2 L/min for five minutes and mean 8 L/min for one minute. Find the total and combined mean. Explain the mistake in averaging the two stated rates to get 5 L/min.

Hint for Exercise 10.7

Convert each interval mean back into its delivered volume.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.7

The total is 2×5+8×1=18 L. Divide by six minutes to obtain 3 L/min. The answer 5 weights the two time intervals equally despite their unequal durations; most of the delivery time belongs to the lower rate.

Return to Exercise 10.7

Exercise 10.8 · Storage, transfer and capacity

A vessel starts with 4 L and has capacity 13 L. For numerical time 0≤t≤5 min, inflow is 5−t L/min and outflow 1 L/min. Find the final and maximum stored volumes, total inflow, total outflow and mean net rate. Check whether the model stays physically possible.

Hint for Exercise 10.8

Net rate is inflow minus outflow. Check extrema as well as endpoints.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.8

The net rate is 4−t, and V(t)=4+4t−t2/2. Final storage is 23/2 L; the maximum occurs at 4 min and equals 12 L. The minimum is the initial 4 L, so storage stays within [0,13]. Inflow is [5t−t2/2]05=25/2 L; outflow is 5 L. Net gain is 15/2 L and mean net rate is 3/2 L/min. Initial volume plus net gain agrees with the final result.

Return to Exercise 10.8

Exercise 10.9 · A varying positive flow

For numerical time 0≤t≤π s, an inlet supplies q(t)=3+2sint L/s. Find the total delivery and mean flow. Give useful bounds from the range of the rate.

Hint for Exercise 10.9

Use radians; first determine the smallest and largest rates on the interval.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.9

The total is [3t−2cost]0π=3π+4 L. Mean flow is 3+4/π L/s. On [0,π], the rate lies between 3 and 5 L/s, so delivery lies between 3π and 5π L and the mean between 3 and 5 L/s. Both checks pass.

Return to Exercise 10.9

Exercise 10.10 · Build the section area

A solid has square sections with side 4+x cm, where x is numerical distance in cm on [0,5]. Find its volume and mean section area. Check by bounding the section areas.

Hint for Exercise 10.10

Square the side to obtain the area before integrating.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.10

The section area is 4+x cm². Volume is [4x+x2/2]05=65/2 cm³. The mean area is (65/2)/5=13/2 cm². Since the section areas range from 4 to 9 cm², volume must lie between 20 and 45 cm³; 65/2 does.

Return to Exercise 10.10

Exercise 10.11 · Half the height, how much volume?

A cone has radius 3 cm and height 8 cm. Find its total volume and the volume in the half nearest the tip. Explain why the latter is not half the total.

Hint for Exercise 10.11

Use distance from the tip. The cross-sectional radius grows linearly with it.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.11

The total is π(32)8/3=24π cm³. At distance x cm from the tip the radius is 3x/8, so the tip half has volume ∫04π(3x/8)2dx=3π cm³. This is one eighth of the whole. Both radius and height are halved, giving factors 1/4 and 1/2 in volume.

Return to Exercise 10.11

Exercise 10.12 · Signed work

A supplied force component is F(x)=9−3x N on [0,5] m. Find its work from 0 to 5 m and the sum of the magnitudes of its positive and negative contributions. Under the same position-dependent law, what is its work from 5 to 0 m?

Hint for Exercise 10.12

Work keeps signs. Use the force zero to separate contributions only for the second requested total.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.12

Total work is [9x−3x2/2]05=15/2 J. The force crosses zero at 3 m; the first contribution is 27/2 J and the second −6 J. Their magnitude sum is 39/2 J. Reversing the bounds gives −15/2 J for the return under the specified same force law.

Return to Exercise 10.12

Exercise 10.13 · Convert before evaluating

An applied force is F(x)=150x N with x in metres. Find its work while extension increases from 1 cm to 4 cm. A learner obtains 1125 J by using bounds 1 and 4. Diagnose the discrepancy.

Hint for Exercise 10.13

Both bounds must use the units stipulated by the function.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.13

The correct bounds are 0.01 and 0.04 m. Work is [75x2]0.010.04=0.1125 J. Bounds 1 and 4 represent metres, not centimetres; squaring the length conversion creates a factor of 1002=10000. Multiplying 0.1125 by 10000 gives the erroneous 1125. The force range 1.5–6 N over 0.03 m provides a check: the result lies between 0.045 and 0.18 J.

Return to Exercise 10.13

Exercise 10.14 · Repair an average routine

A supplied antiderivative of a rate is implemented correctly as def Q(t): return 2*t + t*t/2. A routine reports the mean on [2,5] as (Q(5)-Q(2))/5. Find the correct mean and repair the expression. Explain why an interval starting at zero could hide the mistake.

Hint for Exercise 10.14

Divide by elapsed interval length, not the final coordinate.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.14

The rate is 2+t. Its accumulation is Q(5)−Q(2)=33/2, and the duration is 3, so the mean is 11/2. Use (Q(5)-Q(2))/(5-2). When the initial time is zero, the final time happens to equal the duration, so that special test would not detect the missing subtraction.

Return to Exercise 10.14

Exercise 10.15 · A model that runs out

A 3 L vessel has supplied constant net outflow 0.75 L/min and no inflow. Write its storage function and maximal physical time interval starting at zero. If a graph continues the formula to six minutes, explain what part of it describes the vessel.

Hint for Exercise 10.15

Locate when the storage first reaches zero.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.15

The balance gives V(t)=3−0.75t L. It reaches zero at 4 min; the supplied draining model applies on [0,4]. The negative polynomial values from 4 to 6 min cannot represent liquid storage. If the outlet stops when empty, the later volume would remain zero, but that is a changed rate model and must be stated.

Return to Exercise 10.15

Exercise 10.16 · Design a discriminating check

A program estimates distance as the absolute value of the net position change. Design a continuous velocity model on a finite interval that exposes its error. Calculate both answers and describe a different no-reversal test that the faulty program would pass.

Hint for Exercise 10.16

A linear velocity with a zero inside the interval gives two triangular contributions.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.16

One choice is v(t)=2t−2 m/s on [0,2] s. Net displacement is [t2−2t]02=0, but the two triangular leg lengths are 1 m each, so distance is 2 m. On [1,2] the velocity is nonnegative and both methods give 1 m, so that test alone would pass. Other models are acceptable if continuity, the sign change, both calculations and the contrasting test are explained. A strong answer identifies why its first test distinguishes the two interpretations.

Return to Exercise 10.16

10.20 Conclusion and reference

We began with two different accounts of the same journey: a displacement can vanish while distance remains positive. The distinction led to a general question about accumulation: which contributions should cancel, and which should add by magnitude? Sign charts answered that question for geometric area and motion. Averages kept the accumulated total but replaced the varying function by an equivalent constant.

Flow and slice models then showed how the small contribution determines the integral's dimensions and meaning. An initial amount turns a net change into a final amount; a section area times thickness gives volume; a supplied force component times displacement gives signed work. Throughout, the bounds belonged to the chosen variable, and a coordinate change required the function to change with it.

You now have a way to explain an integral before calculating it and to test the result afterwards. Keep the interpretation, antiderivative check and model conditions together. Unit 11 will extend the computational side when an exact antiderivative is inconvenient or unavailable. The decisions about quantities, signs, intervals and units remain essential there.

Consult · Python

def change(F, a, b):
    return F(b) - F(a)

def mean(F, a, b):
    if b <= a:
        raise ValueError("Require a < b")
    return change(F, a, b)/(b - a)

# Example: v(t) = 2*t - 2, reversal at t = 1
def F(t):
    return t*t - 2*t
D = abs(change(F, 0, 1)) + abs(change(F, 1, 2)) # 2
