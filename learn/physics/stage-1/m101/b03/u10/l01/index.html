M101 · Unit 10 · Lesson 1

Signed area and geometric area

An integral keeps the signs of its contributions. A geometric area counts the size of every region. To move confidently between the two, we need to know where the integrand changes sign and what the question asks us to add.

Three hours, including the unit introduction: 80 minutes reading and reconstructing examples, 45 minutes activities, 35 minutes feedback and correction, and 20 minutes reserved for the closing unit exercises.

10.1 What the small contribution measures

In Unit 8 we formed a sum of terms of the form f(xi)Δxi, with positive interval widths. When f(xi) is negative, that term is negative too. Refining the partition does not remove its sign. Thus the definite integral adds the contributions above the horizontal axis and subtracts the magnitudes of those below it.

Imagine cutting the region between a graph and the horizontal axis into narrow vertical strips. For a strip above the axis, its height is approximately f(xi). For a strip below it, its height is approximately −f(xi). A height is a nonnegative length in either case. Both descriptions can be written as |f(xi)|, so the geometric area is obtained by integrating the absolute value.

Key idea 10.1 · Two different totals

For continuous f on [a,b], with a<b,

I=∫abf(x)dx,A=∫ab|f(x)|dx.

I is the signed integral; A is the geometric area between the graph and the axis. If the above-axis regions have total area P and the below-axis regions total area N, then I=P−N, while A=P+N.

The equation I=P−N shows why a small signed answer need not mean a small region: two large contributions may nearly cancel. In contrast, the area P+N retains both. In particular, A≥|I|. This inequality is a useful check before we know the exact values.

Example 10.1 · Cancellation and area

Find the signed integral and geometric area between f(x)=x−2 and the axis on [0,5]. Both coordinate scales are dimensionless.

The zero is at x=2. The graph lies below the axis on [0,2] and above it on [2,5]. The two regions are triangles. Their nonnegative areas are N=2×2/2=2 and P=3×3/2=9/2. Hence

I=92−2=52,A=92+2=132.

We can independently evaluate the signed integral using F(x)=x2/2−2x. Its endpoint difference is F(5)−F(0)=5/2, agreeing with the geometric calculation. Taking the absolute value of this final result gives 5/2, not the area 13/2: the cancellation has already happened.

Activity 10.1 · Recover the two contributions

A graph has signed integral 3 and geometric area 11 over the same forward interval. Find its above-axis and below-axis areas. Then decide what a signed integral of zero would tell you about those two areas.

Discussion of Activity 10.1

Adding P−N=3 and P+N=11 gives 2P=14, so P=7 and N=4. A zero signed integral gives P=N; the common value may be positive or zero. It does not determine how much area there is.

10.2 Split before taking magnitudes

An absolute value is an instruction about signs, rather than a new antiderivative rule. To evaluate it, find the zeros of the original function in the interval and determine its sign between them. On each sign interval, replace the absolute value by the appropriate expression. You can then use ordinary integration.

For continuous functions, a change of sign must pass through zero. A zero need not be a sign change: (x−1)2, for example, is nonnegative on both sides of 1. Checking a point in each interval, or using the signs of factors, settles which expression is needed.

Example 10.2 · A curved region crossing twice

Find both totals for f(x)=x2−1 on [−2,2].

The factors (x−1)(x+1) give zeros at −1 and 1. Outside those roots both factors have the same sign, so f is positive. Between them the signs differ, so f is negative. An antiderivative is F(x)=x3/3−x, giving

I=F(2)−F(−2)=43.

The middle interval contributes F(1)−F(−1)=−4/3. Each outer interval contributes 4/3. For the geometric area we reverse only the negative middle contribution:

A=∫−2−1(x2−1)dx−∫−11(x2−1)dx+∫12(x2−1)dx=4.

The three pieces each have area 4/3. Their different signs explain why the original integral was only 4/3. Differentiating F checks the antiderivative, while the sign chart checks how we assembled the area.

There is a second convenient way to organise the arithmetic: calculate each signed piece first, take its magnitude, and add. That works because the function has one sign on each chosen piece. It would fail if we left an unexamined sign change inside a piece.

Activity 10.2 · Zeros, signs and pieces

  1. For f(x)=(x−1)(x−3) on [0,4], write a split-integral expression for its geometric area, then evaluate both the area and the signed integral.
  2. For g(x)=(x−2)2 on [0,4], decide whether the zero at 2 requires a change of sign in the area calculation. Find the area.
Hint for Activity 10.2

In (a), the function is positive, negative, positive on the successive intervals. In (b), a square is nonnegative.

Discussion of Activity 10.2

(a) Use ∫01fdx−∫13fdx+∫34fdx. An antiderivative is x3/3−2x2+3x. The signed pieces are 4/3,−4/3,4/3, giving signed integral 4/3 and area 4. (b) No sign reversal is needed. An antiderivative is (x−2)3/3, so the area is 16/3. Splitting at 2 is allowed, but both pieces keep positive signs.

10.3 A gap between boundaries

A region may lie between two curves rather than between a curve and the axis. Look at a narrow vertical strip through that region. Its height is the upper coordinate minus the lower coordinate, even when both coordinates are negative. The strip contribution is therefore approximately (f(xi)−g(xi))Δxi, if f is the upper boundary.

This gives the area ∫ab(f−g)dx when f≥g throughout the interval. If the curves exchange order, find their intersections and split there. Equivalently the whole area is ∫ab|f−g|dx. Here the zeros that matter are zeros of the difference, not zeros of either curve separately.

Example 10.3 · Boundaries exchange order

Find the area between y=x2 and y=2 for 0≤x≤2.

The curves meet at x=2 within the interval. For smaller nonnegative x, the horizontal line is above the parabola; for larger x, the parabola is above the line. Therefore

A=∫02(2−x2)dx+∫22(x2−2)dx.

The first piece is [2x−x3/3]02=42/3. The second is

[x33−2x]22=4(2−1)3.

Adding gives

A=82−43.

Both pieces are positive, as a strip-height calculation requires. Integrating 2−x2 without changing order would subtract the second region.

Activity 10.3 · Which difference?

The boundaries of a region are y=x−4 and y=−x−2, with 0≤x≤3. Sketch them, find their intersection, and calculate the area. Explain why their negative heights do not make the area negative.

Discussion of Activity 10.3

The intersection is at x=1, where both heights are −3. On [0,1], the upper boundary is −x−2, giving height 2−2x. On [1,3], the upper boundary is x−4, giving height 2x−2. The integrals are 1 and 4, so the area is 5. The strip height is the separation of the boundaries; subtracting the lower negative coordinate from the upper one still gives a positive length.

10.4 Dimensions and plausibility

The area picture helps organise an integral, but its physical dimensions come from its axes. When both axes measure length, a strip has units of length squared. When the vertical axis measures a rate and the horizontal axis measures time, their product has the units of an accumulated quantity. You must read both labels before attaching a physical meaning to the result.

For example, a graph of velocity in metres per second against time in seconds has strip units (ms−1)s=m. The plotted region represents a displacement or distance, depending on its treatment of sign. It does not represent a physical area in square metres. Conversely, when horizontal distance is in metres and vertical distance in centimetres, the immediate product is mcm; convert to consistent units before reporting square metres.

Dimensions also supply a scale check. If a nonnegative strip height lies between m and M, a region of width b−a must have area between m(b−a) and M(b−a). These are the areas of enclosing constant-height rectangles. They provide an independent check on an antiderivative calculation.

A dimension check tests the kind of quantity. It cannot distinguish a distance from a displacement: both have dimensions of length.

Activity 10.4 · Test an answer before trusting it

A strip of land is 12 m long. Its width varies continuously between 2 m and 5 m. A calculation reports an area of 18 m². Is that possible? A separate graph has vertical units L/min and horizontal units min: what units should its integral have?

Discussion of Activity 10.4

The land area lies between 12×2=24 m² and 12×5=60 m². The reported 18 m² has the right dimensions but fails the bound. The second integral has units litres, since minutes cancel. The meaning of its sign depends on whether positive rate denotes inflow, outflow or another chosen direction.

The central decision in this lesson was how to add the strips: retain their signs for a signed total, or use their nonnegative sizes for geometric area. We checked which expression belonged on each interval before integrating. In the next lesson, exactly that decision will distinguish a trolley's displacement from its distance travelled.
