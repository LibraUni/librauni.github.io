Numerical integration and trustworthy checks

A rate measured at a few times gives us pieces of a story. To estimate the total change, we must decide what happens between those times. A numerical integral makes that decision explicit: replace the varying function on each short interval by something simple, calculate its contribution, and add the contributions.

In Unit 8, rectangle sums helped define an integral. Units 9 and 10 then used antiderivatives to calculate and interpret accumulated changes. We now return to finite sums as practical methods. They work when an antiderivative is inconvenient, and they can also work with a table of measurements for which no formula has been supplied. Their usefulness brings a new responsibility. How much confidence should we place in the resulting number?

This unit develops that question alongside the methods themselves. You will construct rectangle, midpoint and trapezoidal estimates, explain their signs and units, and compare them with exact integrals where those are available. You will then increase the number of intervals and interpret the changes. A deliberately awkward function will show why two matching answers can still miss an important contribution. The final lesson brings together mathematical reasoning, readable code and a short account of the evidence behind an answer.

What to bring forward

You need definite integrals and their orientation from Unit 8, elementary antiderivatives and checking by differentiation from Unit 9, and the interpretation of rates, averages and signed totals from Unit 10. Derivative signs and concavity from Unit 6 will help explain why some approximations lie above or below an integral. Unit 7's distinction between a numerical approximation and the quantity it approximates returns here.

The Python work uses functions, loops, lists, NumPy arrays and labelled Matplotlib plots taught in P101 Units 1 and 3. Before starting the coding activities, check that you can define a function, loop over a list of resolutions, create an array of coordinates and label a graph with units. If that foundation is still missing, complete it before the coding strand; the written reasoning can be read first. The five Python hours are included in this unit's sixteen hours.

Working through the unit

Each of the four lessons has a four-hour allocation, including attempts, discussion and correction. Lessons 1–3 reserve a total of 80 minutes for the cumulative closing work. Keep that reserve: the final lesson uses it alongside its own allocation for the unit exercises, formative checkpoint and reference review. Timings are study estimates; use your actual attempts to identify where a tutorial or revision is needed.

Write a small sum by hand before trusting a larger program. Keep a record of the function or data, interval, units, rule and number of panels next to each numerical result. When results change, ask what the change tells you about the calculation. When they stop changing, ask what information the samples might still be missing.

The route ahead

  1. Rectangle and midpoint quadrature: choose sample positions, construct signed sums and establish a hand benchmark.
  2. Trapezoidal estimates and refinement: join endpoint values, compare resolutions and distinguish observed error from a proved bound.
  3. Missed features and trustworthy comparisons: test sampling blind spots, jumps, data limitations and arithmetic.
  4. Block synthesis and formative checkpoint: build an evidence-based numerical report, practise across the unit and revisit the central ideas of Block 3.

The separate TMA 03 follows this block with its existing six-hour allocation; it is not part of these practice hours. Unit 12 will carry the question of trustworthy approximation into the final block. No new integration rule is needed here beyond rectangles, midpoints and trapezoids.
