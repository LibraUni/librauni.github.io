Trapezoidal estimates and refinement

A midpoint rectangle follows the function at one place in each panel. If we already know the values at both endpoints, another natural choice is to join them by a straight line. Integrating that line gives a trapezoidal contribution. We will derive the resulting weights and then investigate how the approximation changes when the panels become narrower.

11.5 Joining the endpoint values

On a panel of width h, let the endpoint values be u and v. A straight line between them has average height (u+v)/2, so its signed integral is h(u+v)/2. For positive heights this is the familiar area of a trapezoid. The same algebra remains valid if one or both heights are negative: the integral adds signed contributions.

Apply the construction to each panel and add. Every interior endpoint belongs to two adjacent panels, receiving half-weight from each. The first and last endpoints belong to only one panel each. That explains the half-weights in the composite trapezoidal rule; they are not an arbitrary correction.

Key idea 11.2 · Composite trapezoidal rule

Tn=h[f(a)2+∑i=1n−1f(a+ih)+f(b)2].

Here h=(b−a)/n. For one panel the interior sum is empty and contributes zero. On the same equally spaced grid, Tn=(Ln+Rn)/2. Midpoint quadrature samples different positions and generally gives a different result.

The straight segment lies above an upward-curving graph and below a downward-curving one. Thus, when f″≥0 throughout a forward interval, trapezoidal quadrature overestimates the integral. If f″≤0, it underestimates. This geometric comparison concerns the curve relative to its chords; the function may be negative or decreasing and still curve upward. When curvature changes sign, errors from different panels can compete, so inspect the whole interval before making a global claim.

Example 11.4 · Recover the bracket centre

For q(t)=1+t2 on [0,2] with four panels, the endpoint rates from Lesson 1 give

T4=0.5[12+1.25+2+3.25+52]=4.75 L.

This equals (3.75+5.75)/2, as expected from the endpoint-sum identity. Since q″=2>0, the segments lie above the curve, consistent with the exact error T4−14/3=1/12 L. The first panel contributes 0.5(1+1.25)/2=0.5625 L; calculating one panel separately is a useful check on the weighting.

Activity 11.5 · A line that crosses zero

Approximate ∫02(3x−2)dx with one trapezoidal panel. Explain why the result is exact even though the line crosses the axis. Would the same computation give the geometric area?

Discussion of Activity 11.5

The endpoint heights are −2 and 4, giving 2(−2+4)/2=2. The interpolating segment is the function itself, so its signed integral is exact. For geometric area, split at x=2/3. The negative triangle has area 2/3, the positive triangle 8/3, and their sum is 10/3. Taking the absolute value of the signed total would give 2 and would miss the cancellation.

11.6 A table of measurements

When values come from measurements, we may have endpoint readings but no midpoint readings. Trapezoidal quadrature is then convenient: it specifies a linear interpolation between adjacent observations. If times are not equally spaced, use each actual width. With coordinates x0<x1<⋯<xn and readings yi, the estimate is

T=∑i=0n−1(xi+1−xi)yi+yi+12.

We can derive this formula panel by panel just as before. There is no single common width to factor out. Replacing the actual widths by a typical spacing would change the contribution assigned to each part of the record.

Example 11.5 · Rainfall from a short rate record

An invented teaching record gives rainfall rates 0, 6, 12 and 0 mm/h at 0, 10, 30 and 60 minutes. Estimate the accumulated rainfall by joining successive readings with straight lines.

Convert each duration to hours because the rates are per hour. The three contributions are

1060×0+62=0.5 mm,
2060×6+122=3 mm,
3060×12+02=3 mm.

The total is 6.5 mm. This is the exact integral of the piecewise-linear interpolating model. Its agreement with actual rainfall depends on how well that model represents the rate between readings and on the measurements themselves. A burst between readings could add rain that this record does not resolve.

A wider view · optional context

Checking the instrument behind the numbers

The USGS describes this instrument as weighing collected rainfall and converting the weight into rainfall depth. A calibration check addresses the instrument's response. A quadrature benchmark addresses the numerical method used on a rate record. Both comparisons use a known reference, but they test different parts of the route from a physical event to a reported total.

A stable quadrature result cannot repair a miscalibrated instrument. Equally, accurate readings do not tell us exactly what happened during an unsampled interval. Keep these sources of uncertainty distinct when explaining a calculation.

Activity 11.6 · More rows without more information

Insert an extra reading at 20 minutes by linearly interpolating the rainfall table. What rate do you insert? Recalculate the contribution from 10 to 30 minutes. Does this provide a new test of the actual rainfall?

Discussion of Activity 11.6

The interpolated rate is 9 mm/h. The two contributions are (10/60)(6+9)/2=1.25 mm and (10/60)(9+12)/2=1.75 mm, still totalling 3 mm. Subdividing a straight segment leaves its integral unchanged. These extra rows express the same assumption more densely; they supply no new observation between the original readings.

11.7 Reading a refinement table

For a supplied function, we can evaluate genuinely new sample values. A refinement replaces a grid by a finer grid, usually doubling the number of panels while leaving the interval and rule unchanged. Compare the outputs for n, 2n and 4n. A benchmark integral lets us also compare each output with the truth.

Refinement for 1+x2 on [0,2], with exact integral I=14/3
PanelsMnTnI−MnTn−I
44.6254.751/241/12
84.656254.68751/961/48
164.66406254.6718751/3841/192

Both errors fall by a factor of four when the width halves. We can explain this exactly for the quadratic. Write the coordinate within one panel as x=c+u, where c is the midpoint and −h/2≤u≤h/2. Then 1+x2=1+c2+2cu+u2. The constant term is integrated exactly by both methods. The odd term 2cu cancels symmetrically. The remaining integral is

∫−h/2h/2u2du=h312.

The midpoint rule assigns zero to the u2 part, underestimating by h3/12 per panel. The trapezoidal rule uses endpoint values h2/4, giving h3/4; it overestimates by h3/4−h3/12=h3/6. There are n panels and h=2/n, so the total errors are 2/(3n2) and 4/(3n2). This proves the pattern in the table for this particular function.

For many sufficiently smooth functions, midpoint and trapezoidal errors behave approximately like C/n2 once the grid resolves the relevant features. This describes a trend, not a universal exact identity. If Qn−I≈C/n2, then Q2n−I≈C/(4n2). Subtracting the two gives a practical estimate for the finer-grid error:

|Q2n−I|≈|Q2n−Qn|3.

The division by three comes from comparing C/n2 with C/(4n2). It is justified only as an estimate when that error pattern is a reasonable model. Look for successive differences decreasing by roughly four, check smoothness and look for unresolved features. The next lesson will deliberately break the assumptions behind this inference.

Activity 11.7 · Difference, estimate and bound

Use T4=4.75 and T8=4.6875 to estimate the error in T8. Compare it with the actual error. Then use the monotonicity bracket to give a guaranteed error bound for T8. State why the two numbers have different roles.

Discussion of Activity 11.7

The refinement difference is 1/16; division by three gives 1/48, which here equals the exact error. The endpoint bracket at n=8 has width 8/8=1, so its centre T8 is within 1/2 of the integral. The first value relies on the quadratic error pattern; the second follows from monotonicity alone and is consequently less sharp.

11.8 Implement, compare and report

The formula suggests a compact implementation: begin with half of each endpoint value, add every interior value once, and multiply by the width. Retain the panel-count and finite-bound checks from Lesson 1. The core calculation, after those checks and a zero-interval return, is:

h = (b - a)/n
total = (f(a) + f(b))/2
for i in range(1, n):
    total += f(a + i*h)
result = h*total

This snippet belongs inside a function; it shows the weighting rather than a complete guarded routine. For n=1, range(1, n) is empty, so only the endpoint pair contributes. When writing your complete routine, check every sampled value is finite, as in the midpoint implementation. Validate a hand-computable panel before building a long refinement table.

Activity 11.8 · A refinement investigation · 60 minutes Python

Implement and test a complete trapezoidal function. First reproduce the quadratic table and confirm exact integration of f(x)=2x−1 on [1,4], including reversal. Then use f(x)=1/(1+x) on [0,1] with n=4,8,16,32. Compare midpoint and trapezoidal values with ln2, obtained from the antiderivative. Print the absolute errors and successive refinement differences. Plot absolute error against panel count with labelled axes. Explain the direction of error and whether the factor-of-four pattern is developing.

Discussion and checks for Activity 11.8

The linear benchmark is [x2−x]14=12, or −12 in reverse. For the reciprocal function, f″(x)=2/(1+x)3>0: trapezoids overestimate, while midpoint estimates underestimate. Approximate values at n=4 are T4=0.69702381 and M4=0.69121989; at n=8, T8=0.69412185 and M8=0.69266055. Both approach ln2≈0.69314718. Errors decrease by close to four rather than exactly four. Use full stored values for differences and round only when displaying the table.

Keep the explanation with your table: function, interval, rule, resolution, benchmark and remaining uncertainty.
