M101 · Mathematics for physics I

Mathematics and the
description of the world

A photograph of a moving object records where it was when the shutter opened. A second photograph, taken a little later, gives us something more: the beginnings of a description of its motion. With a scale and a clock, we can start to ask how far the object has moved, how quickly its position is changing, and whether that change is steady.

These questions are simple to state, but answering them takes us a surprisingly long way into mathematics. We need numbers to record measurements, a way of specifying position, and a means of comparing what happens at different times. If the motion changes, we must also decide what we mean by its speed at a particular instant. The mathematical ideas that emerge from these questions are useful far beyond moving objects. They help us describe the heating of a material, the flow of an electric current and the changing brightness of a star.

This module introduces some of those ideas. You will learn to work with quantities that have direction, investigate how one quantity changes in relation to another, and calculate totals built up from many small contributions. Along the way, you will use equations, diagrams and short computer calculations to look at the same problem from different points of view.

From an observation to a mathematical question

Imagine filming a bead as it moves along a straight track. A ruler beside the track allows you to estimate its position in each frame. You could list those positions in a table, draw them on a graph, or try to find an equation that describes how position depends on time. Each representation brings out something different. A table preserves individual readings; a graph makes a pattern visible; an equation may allow you to calculate a position at a time when no photograph was taken.

That last step requires care. An equation inferred from a few measurements need not describe everything the bead will do. Perhaps friction changes along the track, or the bead eventually reaches an end stop. We therefore have to distinguish between the object itself and the mathematical description we have chosen for it. Such a description is called a model. A useful model includes the features needed for the question we are asking, while leaving out others.

In Figure M1, the bead changes position by 2 cm during the first second and by 6 cm during the next. We can already compare these changes using arithmetic. Later, you will learn how to describe a rate of change over successively shorter intervals. This leads to the derivative, one of the central ideas of calculus. We will also investigate the reverse kind of question: if we know how quickly a quantity is changing, how can we find the change that has accumulated? That question leads to integration.

You are not expected to know calculus before beginning. The purpose of this introduction is to show where the work is heading. The definitions, calculations and qualifications will be developed when they are needed.

A wider view · optional reading

An address in space

The James Webb Space Telescope operates in an orbit around the Sun near the Sun–Earth L2 point, about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth. It does not remain motionless at that point: it follows an orbit around the L2 region. NASA explains that this arrangement helps keep the Sun, Earth and Moon on the same side of the telescope, where its sunshield can block their light and heat. [1]

Even this short description raises mathematical questions. Where is a position measured from? In which direction does the telescope lie? How does that position change with time? A distance alone cannot answer all three.

Calculating such an orbit requires physics and mathematics beyond this module. But the first task—turning a description of position into something we can calculate with—is exactly where we will begin.

The route through M101

The first block, Vectors and linear relationships, begins with position and direction. You will describe these using coordinates and vectors, then learn how vectors can be combined and compared. Matrices will give us a compact way of representing transformations and related equations. A rotation or reflection can then be studied both as a change in a diagram and as a calculation with numbers.

The second block, Change and local behaviour, develops differentiation. We will begin with the behaviour of functions and work towards methods for finding rates of change. These methods help us investigate where a quantity increases, where it decreases and where it may have a maximum or minimum. Their usefulness depends on understanding the question being asked, rather than simply recognising a formula to apply.

In the third block, Accumulation and integral models, the emphasis turns to totals: how small contributions combine to produce an area, an average or an accumulated change. You will see how integration and differentiation are connected. You will also compare exact calculations with numerical estimates, which are especially useful when a convenient exact calculation is unavailable.

The final block, Approximation, validation and mathematical judgement, brings these methods together. We will ask what makes an answer trustworthy. Does it have the right units? Does it behave sensibly in a simple case? Does a numerical calculation support the reasoning, and what does that support actually establish? These questions will recur throughout the module, becoming more substantial as your mathematical tools develop.

Working with the material

Mathematics is often understood in two stages. A worked solution can seem clear while you are reading it; only when you try a related question do you discover which parts you can reproduce and which remain uncertain. For that reason, the activities form part of the teaching. Keep paper beside you, draw the diagrams, and write enough working to make your reasoning visible. An unfinished attempt is often a better starting point for a tutorial than an answer copied from a solution.

When you read a worked example, attend to the decisions as well as the arithmetic. Why was that representation chosen? What does the symbol refer to? Why is the next step allowed? When you check your own work against a discussion, look for the first place where the reasoning differs. Sometimes the problem will be a sign or a calculation; sometimes it will be the interpretation of the question.

Green boxes collect important definitions and results after we have developed them. Sepia boxes contain worked examples, including the decisions behind the calculation. Numbered activities invite you to pause and try an idea; a substantial exercise section closes each unit. Short notes beside the text clarify notation or recall an earlier idea. Blue boxes marked A wider view or Historical perspective offer applications and context; you can leave these aside on a first pass without losing a prerequisite. Essential results will be identified separately. The closing reference table and printable sheet will collect the notation, methods and conditions you need to return to. Use these after working through the explanations: they are reminders of reasoning you have met, rather than substitutes for it.

M101 is studied alongside P101, Physics, space & scientific thinking. P101 introduces the shared Python tools. We will use them in M101 once that foundation is in place, for such tasks as checking a vector calculation or exploring a numerical approximation. The opening unit requires no programming. Familiarity with signed numbers, elementary algebra, graphs and trigonometry will be useful across the module; short reminders will appear where they are needed.

The mathematical and physical strands will meet repeatedly, but they have different responsibilities. Here we will take time over the mathematical reasoning. When a physical situation is used before its theory has been taught, the assumptions and relationships needed for the calculation will be supplied. You should not have to guess an unfamiliar law of physics in order to learn a mathematical method.
