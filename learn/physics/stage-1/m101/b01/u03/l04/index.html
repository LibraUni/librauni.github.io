M101 · Unit 3 · Lesson 4

When solutions fail
or are not unique

Elimination can reveal an impossible requirement, or show that two equations say the same thing. Both are useful answers. The next task is to read what remains, rather than force every calculation to produce one pair of numbers.

This lesson has a 3-hour allocation: 60 minutes of reading, 90 minutes of written activities and 30 minutes of feedback. Use 15 minutes of that feedback here; keep the remaining 15 minutes for the cumulative unit-exercise solutions in Lesson 6. Allow about 2 hours 45 minutes for this page. No programming is required.

Use the reversible equation and row operations from Lesson 3. Unless a context gives a restriction, unknowns and parameters may take any real value, including fractions and negative numbers.

3.21 When elimination produces a contradiction

In Lesson 3, each completed elimination fixed one value of the last unknown, and back substitution recovered the others. That outcome was a property of those systems. It was not promised merely by the presence of two equations and two unknowns. Two requirements can disagree, even when each is perfectly reasonable on its own.

Imagine a supplied exact model that requires one expression to have two different values. Trying more candidate pairs cannot repair that disagreement. A useful method should tell us when the requirements cannot be met, as well as help us find values when they can. Elimination does both jobs because it preserves the whole set of solutions at each reversible step.

Example 3.22 · A calculation that ends without unknowns

Consider

x+2y=6,2x+4y=15.

The second left-hand side is twice the first. If the first equation holds, doubling both sides gives 2x+4y=12. The second equation requires that same expression to equal 15. No choice of x and y can do both.

Elimination records this disagreement. Keep the first equation and subtract twice it from the second:

(2x+4y)−2(x+2y)=15−2(6),0=3.

The last row means 0x+0y=3. Its left side is zero for every pair, so it cannot equal 3. There is no solution to the transformed system and, because the operation is reversible, no solution to the original system either.

An equation that cannot be true is a contradiction. A system with no simultaneous solution is called inconsistent. The word describes the combined requirements; it does not imply that elimination was performed incorrectly. In Example 3.22 the calculation succeeded in exposing why the requirements cannot be satisfied.

There is no reason to divide the last row by a coefficient: both unknown coefficients are zero. Nor does 0=3 mean that an unknown is zero or three. The unknowns have disappeared from this equation. The statement is about the impossibility of satisfying the system.

A contradiction is decisive wherever it occurs. Additional equations can only impose further requirements. They cannot make a pair satisfy an already impossible requirement. This is why we inspect the right-hand side as carefully as the coefficient entries.

A proposed answer is (3,0) because it satisfies the first equation. Check it in the second. Then explain why rejecting this one pair is weaker evidence than your elimination argument. Finally, change only the second right-hand side so that it becomes exactly twice the first equation.

3.22 When an equation adds no information

Change the 15 in Example 3.22 to 12. Nothing changes in the coefficient part, but the conclusion changes completely. This small edit is a useful warning: a coefficient matrix alone does not always tell us whether a particular right-hand side is compatible with it.

Example 3.23 · Agreement without a second restriction

Start from

x+2y=6,2x+4y=12.

Apply the same replacement, R2←R2−2R1:

[126000].

The lower row now reads 0=0, an identity, true for every pair. It adds no restriction. The upper row still says x+2y=6. We must keep it.

For instance, (6,0) satisfies both original equations, and so does (2,2). But (0,0) satisfies neither. Thus the zero row does not mean that all pairs solve the original system. Only pairs satisfying the retained equation do.

An equation is redundant when it adds no requirement beyond the other equations retained in the system. Here the second equation is twice the first, including its right-hand side. Repeating the same condition in a different form does not pin down a second unknown.

The system is consistent, meaning that it has at least one solution. In fact it has infinitely many, as we shall describe precisely. In this two-equation example the equations are dependent: one is a multiple of the other. We will use “consistent dependent system” for cases of this kind, to distinguish them from inconsistent systems whose coefficient rows are also proportional.

Compare the lower rows in Figure 3.19 by reading them aloud as equations. The difference between the two right-hand sides is the difference between “impossible” and “no additional restriction.” Neither is an instruction to set an unknown to zero. The meaning comes from the entire row, including the column positions and the separator.

Key distinction 3.8 · Read a row with zero coefficients

If every unknown coefficient in a row is zero, a nonzero right-hand side gives a contradiction and rules out all solutions. A zero right-hand side gives the identity 0=0 and adds no restriction. In the second case, inspect the other equations before deciding how many solutions remain.

Activity 3.24 · Keep the surviving condition 12 min

Reduce 3x−y=6 and −6x+2y=−12 using a row replacement. Find two different pairs that satisfy both originals, including one with a negative entry. Give one pair that satisfies the resulting zero row but fails the retained equation.

Explain the error in each conclusion: “The zero row proves x=y=0” and “The zero row proves that every pair is a solution.”

Discussion of Activity 3.24

Add twice row 1 to row 2. The result is

[3−16000].

The condition 3x−y=6 remains. Pairs (2,0) and (1,−3) satisfy it. In the second original equation their left-hand sides are −12 and −6−6=−12, respectively. The pair (0,0) satisfies 0=0 but gives 0 rather than 6 in the retained equation.

A zero row does not assign zero to any unknown. It is true independently of their values. The system still requires every other row to hold, so it does not allow every pair.

3.23 Describe every solution with a parameter

Finding two successful pairs establishes that a solution is not unique. It does not yet solve the system completely: there may be infinitely many other pairs, and a list of examples does not tell us how to find them. We want one description that generates every solution and nothing else.

Return to the surviving equation x+2y=6. Once a value of y is chosen, the equation fixes x=6−2y. The choice of y is unrestricted for this mathematical system, but the choice of x is then tied to it. We cannot choose the two independently.

Example 3.24 · From a surviving equation to a family

Introduce a real number t and set y=t. The complete solution is

x=6−2t,y=t,t∈ℝ.

The symbol ℝ means the real numbers. Here t is a parameter: a freely chosen number used to label the different solutions. It is not an extra unknown waiting for another equation. In ordered-pair notation, the same description is (x,y)=(6−2t,t) for every real t.

For t=0 we obtain (6,0); for t=2 we obtain (2,2); for t=−1 we obtain (8,−1). These are members of the solution family. A fractional choice works too: t=1/2 gives (5,1/2).

To check the whole family, leave t unspecified and substitute into both original equations:

x+2y=(6−2t)+2t=6,2x+4y=2(6−2t)+4t=12.

The terms involving t cancel for every real value. This verifies that every generated pair is a solution.

We also need the reverse argument: have we missed any? Any solution has some real y-coordinate. Call that value t. The retained equation forces its x-coordinate to be 6−2t, so it appears in our family. Together these two arguments establish that the description is complete.

The parameter is often chosen to equal an unknown that has not been fixed by elimination; that unknown is called a free variable. In this example we chose y. “Free” describes the remaining choice within a consistent system. It does not mean that the original equations can be ignored.

Figure 3.20 links a parameter value to a point. Changing t changes both coordinates in a coordinated way. The point stays on the line because the equation holds for every choice. The control shows only a bounded selection of values; the mathematical family extends along the whole line in both directions.

Example 3.25 · A different parameter can describe the same family

Instead choose x=s. Rearranging x+2y=6 gives

x=s,y=3−s2,s∈ℝ.

This is the same family with different labels. In the earlier description x=6−2t, so the labels are related by s=6−2t. Conversely t=3−s/2. Every real choice of either label corresponds to one choice of the other.

For example, (2,2) is obtained with t=2 in the first description and s=2 in the second. That numerical coincidence is not a general rule: (6,0) uses t=0 but s=6. To compare two family descriptions, compare the pairs they generate, not just the letters or parameter values.

  2. Return to 3x−y=6 and −6x+2y=−12 from Activity 3.24. Set x=t, express y in terms of t, and check the family in both original equations without choosing a numerical t.
  3. Explain why your description includes every solution. Then give a second description with y=s.

Discussion of Activity 3.25

The predicted pairs are (8,−1), (5,1/2) and (0,3). Increasing t by one decreases x by two and increases y by one. The result stays on x+2y=6.

For the second system, the family is

x=t,y=3t−6,t∈ℝ.

Substitution gives 3t−(3t−6)=6 and −6t+2(3t−6)=−12. Every generated pair therefore satisfies both originals. Conversely, any solution has an x-value that can be called t, and 3x−y=6 then forces y=3t−6. No solution is omitted.

Choosing y=s instead gives x=(s+6)/3, with every real s allowed. The two checked pairs are members of this infinite family, not its entire membership. Checking two pairs disproves uniqueness, but does not describe all possible solutions.

3.24 Read the geometry without trusting appearances

A solution of two equations in x,y must lie on both corresponding lines. The algebraic outcomes now have three geometric interpretations. Distinct lines that intersect have one common point. Distinct parallel lines have none. Coincident lines occupy the same set of points, so every point on that line is common to both.

In the examples below, each equation really does define a line: its two coefficients are not both zero. That condition matters. The equation 0x+0y=0 describes the whole plane, not a special line through the origin, while 0x+0y=3 describes no points at all.

Example 3.26 · Three outcomes on the same axes

Keep x+2y=6 as the first equation. If the second is 2x+y=6, subtracting twice the first gives −3y=−6. Thus y=2 and x=2: one common point. If the second is 2x+4y=15, the replacement gives 0=3: no common point. If the second is 2x+4y=12, it gives 0=0: the second line coincides with the first.

For the last two cases, rearranging the equations makes the difference visible. The retained line is y=3−x/2. The inconsistent second line is y=15/4−x/2, with the same slope but a different vertical intercept. The dependent second equation rearranges to exactly y=3−x/2.

Coincident lines can look like one line even though two equations were plotted. The solid and dashed markings in the final panel deliberately share the same path. By contrast, the parallel lines in the middle panel remain separated everywhere, not just within the displayed window.

That last conclusion comes from their equations, not from an unsuccessful search for an intersection on a small screen. Two lines with slightly different slopes can meet far beyond a chosen plotting window. Two distinct lines can also be too close to distinguish at the available resolution. A graph is valuable evidence about meaning; exact algebra decides whether an exact system has a solution.

A wider view · A photograph is not a coordinate graph Optional · about 2 minutes within the reading allowance

Long edges in a perspective photograph can appear to approach one another as they recede. This does not establish that the corresponding physical edges intersect. A photograph records a view from a camera position; equal distances in the scene need not occupy equal distances in the image.

Our coordinate diagrams have a different purpose. They place ordered pairs on labelled axes at a stated scale. The lines are the sets of pairs satisfying equations, rather than photographs of physical rails. Before interpreting a crossing or a gap, ask what the picture represents and what its coordinates mean.

The rail transport shown here belongs to the practical infrastructure behind spaceflight. Its role in this lesson is to make the distinction between a pictured scene and a mathematical diagram visible; it is not data for any of our systems.

Activity 3.26 · Decide from equations 12 min

Each system starts with x+y=2. For the second equation, consider (a) 2x+2y=4, (b) 2x+2y=4.1, and (c) 2x+2.1y=4.1. Treat the decimals as exact supplied numbers.

Eliminate x in each case. Classify the result and describe the relationship between the lines. For any unique solution, give its coordinates and check both equations. Explain why a sketch that looks almost the same for (b) and (c) is not enough to classify them.

Discussion of Activity 3.26

Subtract twice x+y=2 from each second equation. In (a), the result is 0=0: coincident lines and the family (x,y)=(2−t,t) for every real t. In (b), it is 0=0.1: distinct parallel lines and no solution.

In (c), the result is 0.1y=0.1. The coefficient is small but nonzero, so division gives y=1, then x=1. The checks are 1+1=2 and 2(1)+2.1(1)=4.1. These lines intersect at (1,1).

Small differences in a drawing may be difficult to see. An exactly zero coefficient and an exactly nonzero coefficient have different meanings here. We are classifying exact equations, not making a rule for ignoring small experimental discrepancies.

3.25 Classify the whole system

We can now organise the decision without introducing a new calculation method. Use valid elimination and read the resulting equations. First ask whether any row is a contradiction. If so, the system is inconsistent, regardless of what the other rows say. If there is no contradiction, ask whether the remaining equations fix every unknown or leave a choice.

For the two-unknown systems studied here, a nonzero pivot for each unknown fixes one pair. A single surviving equation with at least one nonzero unknown coefficient leaves one variable free and gives a family. If every equation reduces to 0=0, neither unknown is restricted and every pair is allowed. This final possibility is different from having all solutions on one line.

Example 3.27 · A zero row can coexist with a unique solution

Consider three equations in two unknowns:

x+y=5,x−y=1,2x+2y=10.

The third is twice the first, so replacing row 3 by row 3 minus twice row 1 gives a zero row. But the first two equations remain. Replacing row 2 by row 2 minus row 1 gives the complete array

[1150−2−4000].

The middle row forces y=2; the first then forces x=3. The pair checks in all three originals: 3+2=5, 3−2=1 and 2(3)+2(2)=10. The third equation is redundant, but the system still has exactly one solution.

If its right-hand side had been 11 instead of 10, the last row would have been 0=1. The first two equations would still pick out (3,2), but that pair would fail the third. The whole system would then have no solution.

This example explains why the phrase “a zero row means infinitely many solutions” is unsafe. We have to inspect what the other rows determine. Likewise, finding a convenient pair from some equations does not establish consistency of the whole system.

Key idea 3.9 · Classify before reporting an answer

Report either no solution with a contradiction, a unique solution with the values and their justification, or all solutions as a family with the parameter range stated. A successful numerical substitution verifies membership of the solution set; it does not by itself prove uniqueness or completeness.

Activity 3.27 · Similar-looking systems 14 min

Classify the following systems using explicit elimination. For each consistent system, give all solutions and justify that your description is complete.

(a)x−y=1,2x−2y=2;
(b)x−y=1,2x−2y=3;
(c)x−y=1,x+y=5.

Explain why the shared coefficient matrix in (a) and (b) does not decide between no solution and infinitely many solutions. Explain why “two equations, two unknowns” is not a justification for a unique solution.

Discussion of Activity 3.27

For (a), subtract twice the first equation from the second to obtain 0=0. The retained equation gives x=1+y, so all solutions are (x,y)=(1+t,t) for every real t. Substitution verifies both originals, and any solution must have this form because the retained equation forces it.

The same operation in (b) gives 0=1, so there are no solutions. The coefficient matrix is identical, but the different right-hand side creates a contradiction.

In (c), subtract the first equation from the second: 2y=4, hence y=2 and x=3. Both originals check. The reversible steps force those values, so the solution is unique. Counting equations and unknowns would not distinguish any of these three cases.

Return to Activity 3.27

Discussion of Activity 3.28

In (a), y=2 and x is unrestricted. The family is (x,y)=(t,2), with t any real number. A zero first column did not prevent us from reading the second column correctly.

In (b), every row is 0=0, so every pair is allowed. Write (x,y)=(s,t) with s and t chosen independently from the real numbers. Choosing only t in an expression such as (t,t) would describe just a diagonal line and omit, for example, (0,1).

In (c), the last row is the contradiction 0=1. The pair (3,2) from the first two rows fails it. There is no simultaneous solution.

3.26 Ask what the model can determine

When equations come from a context, their classification tells us something about the question we can answer. An inconsistent exact model cannot match all the supplied requirements. A dependent model may match them in many ways. It does not contain enough information to recover one choice of inputs, even if we can calculate a perfectly correct example.

These conclusions concern the supplied model. With real measurements, disagreement might come from recording, rounding, uncertainty or an unsuitable assumption. Algebra alone does not identify which. In this lesson we keep the arithmetic exact and the relationships explicit, so that the mathematical issue is visible.

Example 3.28 · Two readouts, one piece of information

A hypothetical device has two dimensionless input settings u,v. Its supplied exact rule gives readouts p=u+2v and q=2u+4v. Determine the settings if the requested readouts are p=6 and q=12.

The resulting equations are the consistent dependent system already studied. Every pair (u,v)=(6−2t,t) produces those readouts. The rule always gives q=2p, so the second requested value confirms compatibility but does not further restrict the settings.

If the requested readouts were p=6 and q=15, no settings could produce them. The rule would require q=12 once p=6 was fixed. Both questions have the same coefficient matrix; changing the requested output changes consistency.

Return to the compatible readouts p=6 and q=12. Suppose the device also supplies a different readout r=u−v, and its specified value is zero. Substituting the family gives 6−3t=0, hence t=2. The three requirements now select (u,v)=(2,2). The new condition was useful because it was not merely another copy of the old total.

A context can also restrict allowable values without adding another equation. For example, if both settings must be nonnegative, the family requires t≥0 and 6−2t≥0, giving 0≤t≤3. This leaves infinitely many choices on a segment. Nonnegative does not mean integer: a setting may be fractional unless the model says otherwise. State such restrictions separately from the solution of the unrestricted equations.

Activity 3.29 · What would determine the inputs? 12 min

Another exact device model gives p=2u+v and q=6u+3v. Requested readouts are p=8 and q=24.

  1. Find the complete input family by setting u=t, and check it in both readout rules.
  2. If both settings must be nonnegative, find the allowed range of t. Give two different allowed pairs.
  3. Decide whether an extra condition 4u+2v=16 determines one pair. Explain.
  4. Instead impose u−v=1. Find the unique pair, check all three requirements and check the nonnegative restriction.
  5. What would change if q were requested to be 25 while p remained 8?
Hint for Activity 3.29

First write v=8−2t. Substitute that same expression into every added condition. An identity leaves the parameter free; an equation with a nonzero coefficient can fix it; a contradiction rejects the requirements.

Return to Activity 3.29

Discussion of Activity 3.29

The family is (u,v)=(t,8−2t) for every real t. The readouts are 2t+(8−2t)=8 and 6t+3(8−2t)=24. Conversely, the first equation forces this value of v for any chosen u=t, so every solution is included.

Nonnegativity gives 0≤t≤4. For example, (0,8) and (4,0) are both allowed. The extra condition 4u+2v=16 is twice the first equation. Substitution gives 16=16, so it supplies no new restriction.

Using u−v=1 instead gives t−(8−2t)=1, hence 3t=9 and t=3. The inputs are (3,2). The checks are 2(3)+2=8, 6(3)+3(2)=24, and 3−2=1. Both values are nonnegative.

If q=25 while p=8, subtracting three times the first equation from the second gives 0=1. The exact model cannot supply those two readouts together, whatever extra condition is added.

Return to Activity 3.29

We now have a fuller meaning of “solve”: establish whether the requirements are compatible and describe every allowed solution. Elimination supplies the evidence, geometry helps interpret it, and substitution checks the proposed description. A family is a complete answer when the information really leaves a choice; “no solution” is a complete answer when the requirements contradict one another.

In Lesson 5 we return to matrices as transformations. Some preserve enough information to recover a unique input from an output; others collapse different inputs onto the same output. We will connect that distinction with the determinant of a two-by-two matrix. Keep the present distinction in view: when uniqueness fails, the right-hand side still matters in deciding whether any solution exists.

Use your 15 minutes of local feedback to compare methods, correct the first unjustified step in any attempt, and explain the difference between a contradiction, a redundant equation and a free choice. The other 15 minutes remains reserved for the cumulative solutions in Lesson 6.
