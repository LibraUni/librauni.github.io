M101 · Unit 3 · Lesson 5

Determinants, invertibility
and computational checks

A transformation can move and distort a shape while preserving enough information to recover its starting point. Another can flatten a whole plane onto a line. We now connect that difference to a number calculated from the matrix, then use Python to check answers without confusing a successful check with a proof of uniqueness.

Break between the two coding activities.

3.27 Area, orientation and a two-by-two determinant

In Lesson 1, the columns of a matrix told us where the two coordinate vectors went. Together those images also tell us what happens to a unit square. Its four corners are the origin, the first coordinate vector, the sum of the two coordinate vectors, and the second coordinate vector. A linear transformation preserves this sum, so the square becomes a parallelogram, possibly flattened.

Write the matrix and its column images as

A=(abcd),

with column images

u=(ac),v=(bd).

The order matters: u comes from (1,0), and v from (0,1). Recall from Unit 2 that the magnitude of a cross product gives the area of a parallelogram. Regard these two plane vectors as (a,c,0) and (b,d,0). Their cross product has only a third component:

u×v=(0,0,ad−bc).

Thus the image square has area |ad−bc|. The signed expression itself records something that ordinary area does not: whether the ordered pair of directions retains or reverses its orientation. With our usual rightward and upward axes, positive means the turn from the first column to the second is counterclockwise through an angle between zero and a straight angle; negative means the corresponding shorter turn is clockwise. At zero the directions fail to enclose area.

Key result 3.10 · The determinant in two dimensions

For a two-by-two matrix, the determinant is the scalar

detA=ad−bc.

The image of the unit square has ordinary area |detA|. A positive determinant preserves orientation; a negative one reverses it. A zero determinant means collapse into a line or, for the zero matrix, a point. A determinant is one number, not another matrix.

Example 3.29 · Read the image through its columns

Take

A=(2112).

The column images are (2,1) and (1,2). Their sum is (3,3), so the four image corners are (0,0), (2,1), (3,3) and (1,2) in that order. The determinant is 2(2)−1(1)=3: the image has area 3 and retains orientation. Figure 3.24 lets us check the area independently. The enclosing three-by-three square has area 9. Outside the parallelogram are four right triangles, each with perpendicular side lengths 2 and 1, and two unit squares. The removed area is 4(1)+2=6, leaving 3.

For a rectangle whose sides run along the coordinate directions and have lengths r and s, the image edges are ru and sv. The cross-product area is rs|ad−bc|. Since the original area is rs, the absolute determinant is an area scale factor. The same factor applies to any parallelogram: substituting its two edge vectors into the cross-product expression gives the original signed area multiplied by ad−bc. We will use this planar result; no three-by-three determinant rule is needed here.

Area scale does not specify length scale. A shear can change lengths and angles while keeping area unchanged. Nor does a negative determinant describe a negative physical area. Its sign belongs to orientation, while the absolute value gives the area factor.

3.28 Collapse and the loss of information

Consider the family of transformations

Tk=(110k),
Tk(xy)=(x+yky).

Its determinant is k. When k is positive, the second column ends above the horizontal axis; when it is negative, below. At k=0, both columns lie along the horizontal axis. Every output then has second coordinate zero, whatever the input's second coordinate was. Figure 3.25 follows the same ordered corners through these three cases.

Example 3.30 · Two different inputs, one output

For T0, both (3,1) and (2,2) give (4,0), because only their sum survives. In fact all inputs (4−t,t) give that output. No rule receiving only (4,0) can know which member was used. Conversely, (4,1) cannot be an output at all, because its second coordinate is nonzero.

If k=2, an output (p,q) gives y=q/2 and then x=p−q/2. Both input coordinates are recovered. This calculation divides by k, so it cannot be used at k=0. The failure of division matches the geometric loss of information.

This is the transformation version of Lesson 4's distinction. A collapsed map can have many inputs for a compatible output and no inputs for an incompatible one. A zero determinant alone does not choose between these cases; we must examine the requested right-hand side.

A wider view · An apparent shape is not a calibrated map

A broad surface can appear narrow when viewed obliquely. That observation gives a useful visual analogy for compression, but the photograph is not the coordinate diagram of Tk: it includes depth, perspective and overlapping objects. We cannot read a numerical determinant from it.

In the ideal map above, both the input plane and the rule are supplied. For every nonzero k, even a very small one, exact input recovery remains possible. At exactly zero it fails. A real image adds other questions—what was measured, what was hidden and what geometric model is justified. Keeping those questions separate prevents us from treating a suggestive picture as a mathematical proof.

3.29 Construct an inverse by recovering the input

An inverse matrix reverses a matrix transformation for every input. If B is the inverse of a square matrix A, both compositions leave every vector unchanged:

BA=I,AB=I,

where the identity matrix is

I=(1001).

We write B=A−1. The superscript names the inverse; it does not instruct us to take the reciprocal of each entry. Matrix multiplication combines rows and columns, so entrywise reciprocals would not generally undo it. A matrix with an inverse is invertible; one without an inverse is singular.

Example 3.31 · Recover first, then package the rule

Let

A=(2111).

Its output (p,q) obeys p=2x+y and q=x+y. Subtract the second equation from the first to obtain x=p−q. Then y=q−x=2q−p. The recovered pair is another matrix action:

(xy)=(1−1−12)(pq).

Thus the candidate inverse is

B=(1−1−12).

Check both products, without changing their order:

For AB, the entry calculations give

(2−1−2+21−1−1+2)=I.

For BA, they give

(2−11−1−2+2−1+2)=I.

The check establishes the inverse for every vector. For a numerical illustration, (1,2) goes to (4,3), then B(4,3)=(1,2) recovers it. That one illustration alone would not have established the matrix identity.

There is a second way to discover the same columns. The first column of an inverse must be the input that A sends to (1,0); the second must be the input sent to (0,1). Solve these two systems separately by elimination and place the answers in columns. For Example 3.31 they are (1,−1) and (−1,2), exactly the columns of B. This connects inverse construction to the familiar column interpretation, without requiring a new general elimination algorithm.

Why is a nonzero determinant the dividing line? For general output (p,q), start with ax+by=p and cx+dy=q. Multiplying and subtracting the equations, without first dividing by a or c, gives

d(ax+by)−b(cx+dy)=dp−bq,(ad−bc)x=dp−bq,a(cx+dy)−c(ax+by)=aq−cp,(ad−bc)y=aq−cp.

When Δ=ad−bc≠0, divide both equations by Δ. For example, substitute the recovered expressions into the first left-hand side. The q terms cancel and the remaining numerator is (ad−bc)p; dividing by Δ therefore gives p. The second left-hand side similarly gives q. Thus a solution exists for every (p,q), and the two derived equations force its values, so it is unique. We can collect the recovery rule as

A−1=1ad−bc(d−b−ca).

This expression applies only when ad−bce0.

The scalar in front multiplies every entry. This two-by-two formula summarises the derivation; understanding and checking it is more useful than applying it before inspecting the denominator.

At Δ=0 we also need a reason for non-invertibility, rather than merely observing that the formula fails. If the first row is not all zero, the nonzero vector (b,−a) is sent to (0,bc−ad)=(0,0). If the first row is zero but the second is not, use (d,−c) instead; it too is nonzero and is sent to zero. If both rows are zero, every input is sent to zero. In every case, at least two different inputs—the zero vector and a nonzero vector—share an output. An inverse cannot recover both from that same output.

Key result 3.11 · The exact two-by-two test

A real two-by-two matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero. Then Ax=b has one solution for every right-hand side, namely x=A−1b. If the determinant is zero, investigate compatibility: a system may have no solutions or infinitely many. These are statements about exact matrices and equations.

3.30 Check a candidate in the original equations

Finding an answer and checking it are different jobs. Suppose a proposed vector is x^, where the hat simply marks it as a candidate. For Ax=b, define its residual vector by

r=Ax^−b.

Each residual entry is the left-hand side minus the required right-hand side of one original equation. In exact arithmetic, every entry is zero exactly when the candidate solves the system. A nonzero entry identifies an equation it fails. Use the original equations: reusing an incorrectly reduced system may repeat the mistake you meant to detect.

Example 3.32 · Inspect both discrepancies

For the matrix A of Example 3.31 and right-hand side (4,3), compare candidates (1,2) and (3,−1).

A(12)=(43).

The residual is (0,0).

A(3−1)=(52).

The residual is (1,−1).

The second candidate fails both equations, although its residual entries sum to zero. Adding discrepancies and testing only that sum would conceal the error. The first candidate is a solution. Its uniqueness follows separately from detA=1, not from the zero residual alone.

The dependent matrix S in Activity 3.31 supplies the counterexample to confusing these claims. Both (6,0) and (2,2) have zero residual for right-hand side (6,12). Substitution establishes that each works; the family argument establishes why there are infinitely many. A check of a single candidate cannot tell us whether another exists.

3.31 Translate the mathematics into Python

The Python work will implement small transparent checks. It will not call a solver or introduce a matrix library. We retain Lesson 2's representation: a matrix is a flat list [a, b, c, d] in row order, and a vector is [x, y]. In this convention the first column is formed by entries 0 and 2, not entries 0 and 1.

Programming readiness. Before independent coding, demonstrate that you can open and run your existing P101 notebook, read list entries such as v[1], evaluate scalar arithmetic, use an if condition, and define and call a function that returns a value. P101 Unit 1, Lesson 4 prepares lists and decisions; Lesson 5 prepares returning functions and explainable checks. If these are unfamiliar, complete that preparation before using the reserved coding time. Reading supplied code is not evidence that you can write and explain it.

Work in a copy of the notebook. Predict small cases on paper, implement the functions, then compare with the worked code. The supplied functions assume correctly sized lists of finite numbers and compatible dimensionless quantities. They are teaching calculations, not a general input-validation library.

Example 3.33 · Keep the calculation separate from the conclusion

Here is the familiar matrix action, followed by two small functions. The determinant function returns a scalar; the residual function returns two discrepancies.

def apply2(m, v):
    first = m[0] * v[0] + m[1] * v[1]
    second = m[2] * v[0] + m[3] * v[1]
    return [first, second]


def determinant2(m):
    return m[0] * m[3] - m[1] * m[2]


def residual2(m, candidate, target):
    output = apply2(m, candidate)
    return [output[0] - target[0], output[1] - target[1]]

Read the residual function from the inside out. It first evaluates the proposed left-hand sides, then subtracts the corresponding target entries. Returning both values preserves information that adding them would lose. Neither function prints a verdict or infers uniqueness. The caller must decide what the result means.

For an exact integer test we can inspect both entries with a condition:

A = [2, 1, 1, 1]
target = [4, 3]
candidate = [1, 2]
r = residual2(A, candidate, target)
print("Residual:", r)
if r[0] == 0 and r[1] == 0:
    print("This candidate satisfies both equations.")
else:
    print("This candidate fails at least one equation.")

The output is Residual: [0, 0], followed by This candidate satisfies both equations. The keyword and requires both comparisons to be true. It is not an addition of the entries. These small integer calculations are exact in Python; later we will distinguish them from calculations involving rounded decimals.

Discussion of Activity 3.34

The matrix is C=(3111), with determinant 2. Candidate (2,1) gives output (7,3) and residual (0,0). Candidate (1,2) gives output (5,3) and residual (−2,0). The coordinate-vector tests return (3,1) and (1,1), its columns. Interchanging the equal off-diagonal entries changes nothing, so this matrix cannot reveal that indexing mistake.

For D, the correct columns are (2,0) and (3,1). The exchanged list [2, 0, 3, 1] instead returns (2,3) and (0,1). Either coordinate-vector test exposes the defect. This is why tests should include a matrix whose off-diagonal entries differ.

The inverse of C is

C−1=12(1−1−13).

Applied to (7,3), its first coordinate is (7−3)/2=2 and its second is (−7+9)/2=1. A numerical implementation may use [0.5, -0.5, -0.5, 1.5]. These halves have exact binary representations, so this particular check introduces no rounding error. A successful record includes the deliberately revealing D test, not just the correct final output. Other implementations are acceptable if they return the same objects, preserve row order and explain the computations.

3.32 Design checks that can reveal a mistake

A test earns its place by distinguishing a correct calculation from a plausible error. The zero vector tests very little: every linear matrix action sends it to zero. An identity matrix can hide incorrect ordering or indexing. The two coordinate vectors are more revealing because they expose the columns, but numerical success still depends on the implementation you are checking actually behaving linearly. A finite set of tests does not prove arbitrary software correct.

Separate the jobs. A hand prediction checks arithmetic and representation. An exact matrix identity justifies a mathematical inverse. A residual checks a particular candidate against the equations. A restart checks whether the notebook depends on stale state. None by itself establishes that a physical model is appropriate for observations.

Example 3.34 · A zero test that passes for the wrong reason

Suppose a residual routine accidentally adds its two entries and returns that total. For the incorrect candidate (3,−1) in Example 3.32 it would return zero, just as it would for the correct candidate (1,2). Test with the former on purpose. The proper routine must return [1, -1], and the two-entry condition must reject it. This tests a specific failure, rather than relying on several similar successful cases.

There is also a boundary between exact mathematics and numerical representation. Python's ordinary decimal-looking values, such as 0.1, are usually stored as binary floating-point approximations. For example, 0.1 + 0.2 - 0.3 gives a very small nonzero value, about 5.55×10−17, rather than exact zero. This is not a contradiction in arithmetic; the calculation uses stored approximations to those decimal fractions.

For our modest integer test cases, inspect exact zero entries. For general floating-point calculations, print and interpret the discrepancies; do not install an unexplained universal tolerance and call every small result “correct”. Scale, rounding and the question being asked matter. A small residual concerns the equations' output mismatch. It does not automatically imply a small error in the recovered input.

Example 3.35 · A small discrepancy can hide a large input error

Treat all decimals in this mathematical example as exact:

E=(1111.001),
b=(22.001).

The determinant is 0.001, which is nonzero. The unique solution is (1,1). But candidate (0,2) gives (2,2.002) and residual (0,0.001). Both input coordinates differ from the solution by 1, although the equation mismatch is only one thousandth in one entry. The equations are almost repeats of each other, so their small difference carries crucial information about the split between the inputs.

This example does not supply a general numerical reliability rule based only on determinant size. It supplies a counterexample to “small residual means nearby input”. Do not round 1.001 to 1 during the calculation: doing so replaces this system by a different, singular one.

Discussion of Activity 3.35

The five expected residuals, in table order, are (0,0), (1,−1), (0,0), (0,0) and (0,−1). The determinants are 1 for A and 0 for S. Its cancellation case must be rejected even though 1+(−1)=0. With S=[1,2,2,4] and target [6,12], both [6,0] and [2,2] work, so a zero residual cannot prove uniqueness. Changing the target to [6,13] gives [0,-1] for either candidate; the proportional-equation argument rules out every other candidate too.

With the correct inverse, all six round trips return their starting vectors. For v=(−2,3), Av=(−1,1) and B(−1,1)=(−2,3); in the other order Bv=(−5,8) and A(−5,8)=(−2,3). With the wrong inverse, A(1,0)=(2,1) is sent to (1,−1), not (1,0). The zero input would hide this fault.

The decimal subtraction is approximately 5.55×10−17. Example 3.35's computed residual is approximately [0.0, 0.001]; its last digits may display binary rounding. The exact nonzero determinant proves uniqueness for the stated mathematical system. It does not certify that an approximate candidate is accurate or that measured coefficients are exact.

A satisfactory conclusion separates these claims and identifies the test that detected each deliberate defect. Simply writing “all tests pass” omits the mathematical interpretation.
