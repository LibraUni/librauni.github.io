M101 · Unit 3 · Lesson 6

Block synthesis, iCMA 01
and unit review

A reliable answer connects a description, a method and a check. This lesson asks you to make those connections without being told in advance which calculation to use.

Unit 3 began with two questions: how can one rule change a whole diagram, and when can its effect be reversed? Matrices now let us answer both. Their columns describe the action on coordinate vectors; their products combine successive actions; and equations let us ask which inputs produce a specified output. The same calculation can therefore be read forwards as a transformation or backwards as a problem to solve.

The purpose of this closing lesson is to bring those readings together and connect them with the vectors of Units 1 and 2. There is no new mathematical technique to learn. Work on paper before consulting feedback, keep the original question beside your calculations, and explain what each check establishes. A wrong answer with a located and corrected cause is useful evidence for your next study session.

Your study time

The lesson's 3 hours contain 15 minutes of synthesis reading, 45 minutes attempting the unit exercises, 30 minutes of Python, 60 minutes for iCMA 01 including feedback and correction, and 30 minutes using the reference. The 15 minutes reserved in Lesson 4 are used here for the unit-exercise solutions. Thus this page brings together 3 hours 15 minutes already within the unit's 20 hours; it adds no extra allocation. Divide the work across sittings if helpful.

The notebook is the same Python activity as the page. Before using it, you need the lists, conditions and returning functions from P101 Unit 1, Lesson 4 and Lesson 5, plus independent notebook restart and run. Availability of those lessons is not evidence that you have completed that preparation.

3.33 From a description to a justified answer

A matrix calculation starts before the first multiplication. Decide what the vector represents: a position, a displacement, a direction, an unknown input or a proposed solution. Check the order of its entries, the coordinate frame and the units. Two pairs of numbers can have the same shape while answering different questions.

Next identify what is known. When the input is known, applying the matrix gives an output. When the output is known, finding an input requires solving equations. These are not interchangeable instructions. A singular matrix still acts on every input of the appropriate size, even though it cannot be inverted. Depending on the target, its equations may have no solution or more than one.

Example 3.36 · Follow a displacement through a transformation

In dimensionless perpendicular coordinates with equal scales, two points have position vectors p=(−12) and q=(21). Apply the shear

S=(1101).

Find the displacement from the first image point to the second. Then explain whether the original displacement can be recovered.

Working. The original displacement is final position minus initial position:

d=q−p=(3−1).

Applying the shear to the displacement gives

Sd=(3+(−1)−1).

Thus the image displacement is (2−1).

An independent route transforms the endpoints. We obtain Sp=(12) and Sq=(31). Subtracting those image positions also gives (2−1). This is an instance of the linearity used throughout the unit: applying a matrix to a difference gives the difference of the two images.

For recovery, solve x+y=2 and y=−1. The second equation fixes y, then the first fixes x=3. There is exactly one answer. Equivalently, detS=1≠0, and

S−1=(1−101).

Its action on the image displacement returns (3−1). The displacement length has nevertheless changed from 32+(−1)2=10 to 22+(−1)2=5. Invertibility does not mean preservation of length. The determinant of 1 tells us about area and orientation, not every geometrical property.

The two routes agree because the algebra has the same meaning in each representation. Agreement is most useful when the routes can reveal different mistakes: subtracting transformed endpoints might catch a reversed displacement, while solving the original component equations can expose an incorrect inverse. Repeating the same erroneous formula twice would be weaker evidence.

Key idea 3.12 · Match the check to the claim

Substitution tests a candidate. Reversible elimination describes the solution set. For a two-by-two matrix, a nonzero determinant establishes a unique input for every target. A program checks the cases it actually runs. State which of these claims your evidence supports.

Exercise 3.1 · Read the array 3 min

Let M=(2−14035). State its dimensions and the entry m23. How many entries must an input column vector have, and how many will the output have?
Solution to Exercise 3.1
There are two rows and three columns, so M is 2×3. Row 2, column 3 contains m23=5. Each row combines three input entries; the two rows produce two output entries. Thus the input has three entries and the output two. The order “rows by columns” also supplies the dimension check.

Return to Exercise 3.1

Exercise 3.2 · Reconstruct the action 4 min

A linear transformation sends e1 to (2−1) and e2 to (13). Write its matrix A and find the image of (−12). Check by combining the two given column images.
Solution to Exercise 3.2
The images are columns, in the order of the coordinate vectors:
A=(21−13).
Row calculations give first coordinate 2(−1)+1(2)=0 and second coordinate (−1)(−1)+3(2)=7. Independently, take minus the first column plus twice the second:
−(2−1)+2(13)=(07).
Treating the supplied images as rows would describe a different transformation.

Return to Exercise 3.2

Exercise 3.3 · Keep the intermediate vector 6 min

Let A=(1201), B=(200−1) and v=(1−1). Apply A first, then B. Give the intermediate vector, compute the single matrix for this order, and check its action on v.
Hint for Exercise 3.3

Start with the innermost action in B(Av). Keep its two entries visible before applying B.

Return to Exercise 3.3

Solution to Exercise 3.3
The intermediate vector is Av=(−1−1). Applying B gives (−21). The single matrix is BA, because A acts first:
BA=(240−1).
Its row calculations on v give 2−4=−2 and 0+1=1, agreeing with the two-stage route. The output of the first step becomes the input of the second; that is why the written product reads in the opposite direction to the verbal order.

Return to Exercise 3.3

Exercise 3.4 · Test the other order 4 min

For the matrices in Exercise 3.3, compute AB and apply it to v. Use the result to explain why exchanging the order can matter. Does your example prove that no two matrices commute?
Solution to Exercise 3.4
The reverse product is
AB=(2−20−1),
so ABv=(41), different from BAv=(−21). One input with different outputs is enough to show these transformations differ. It does not prove that every pair differs: for example, AI=IA=A. Noncommutativity is a warning to check order, not a universal prohibition on commuting pairs.

Return to Exercise 3.4

Exercise 3.5 · Eliminate and return to the originals 6 min

Solve 2x+y=7 and x−y=−1 by reversible elimination. Check the result in both original equations and explain why your elimination does not lose solutions.
Solution to Exercise 3.5
Replace the first equation by the sum of the two equations, keeping the second: 3x=6 and x−y=−1. Hence x=2 and y=3. The originals give 2(2)+3=7 and 2−3=−1. Adding the second equation is reversible by subtracting it; division by the nonzero number 3 is also reversible. These steps force both unknowns, so the solution is unique. A check in only the new equation 3x=6 would fail to constrain y.

Return to Exercise 3.5

Exercise 3.6 · Three solution sets 6 min

Classify each system as unique, inconsistent or having infinitely many solutions. Give the decisive reasoning and, in (a), check your answer.
  1. x+y+z=6, y+z=3, z=1.
  2. x+y=2, 2x+2y=5.
  3. x+y=2, 2x+2y=4.
Solution to Exercise 3.6
(a) Back-substitution gives z=1, then y=2, then x=3. The originals give 3+2+1=6, 2+1=3, and 1=1. Each unknown is fixed, so the solution is unique.
(b) Subtract twice the first equation from the second: 0=1. This contradiction means no solution.
(c) The same operation gives 0=0. Only x+y=2 remains, so (x,y)=(2−t,t) for any real t. There are infinitely many solutions. A zero equation does not mean every pair works: the surviving equation still matters.

Return to Exercise 3.6

Exercise 3.7 · Describe the whole family 5 min

For 2x−y=5 and −4x+2y=−10, use x=t to describe every solution. Check the members with t=−1 and t=3, then explain why no solutions lie outside your description.
Hint for Exercise 3.7

Separate “every listed pair works” from “every solution is listed”. For the second direction, start with an arbitrary solution.

Return to Exercise 3.7

Solution to Exercise 3.7
The second equation is −2 times the first. With x=t, the first gives y=2t−5, so
(xy)=(t2t−5).
Here t∈ℝ. For t=−1 the pair is (−1,−7): the left sides are −2+7=5 and 4−14=−10. For t=3 the pair is (3,1): they are 6−1=5 and −12+2=−10. Every real t gives a solution because substitution gives the identities 5=5 and −10=−10. Conversely, any solution has some real first coordinate x=t, and the first equation then forces y=2t−5. The two checked members alone would not establish this completeness.

Return to Exercise 3.7

Exercise 3.8 · Area, inverse and conditions 5 min

Let C=(1223). Find its determinant, state the image area and orientation of the unit square, and find C−1. Verify both products with your inverse.
Solution to Exercise 3.8
The determinant is 1(3)−2(2)=−1. The image area is |−1|=1, with reversed orientation. Nonzero determinant allows
C−1=(−322−1).
For CC−1 the entries are −3+4=1, 2−2=0, −6+6=0, 4−3=1. For C−1C they are −3+4=1, −6+6=0, 2−2=0, 4−3=1. Both products are I. Area preservation here does not imply that all lengths are preserved.

Return to Exercise 3.8

Exercise 3.9 · Retrieve orientation from vectors 4 min

In a right-handed, perpendicular frame, let a=(2,1,0) and b=(1,−2,0). Find a·b and a×b. State the angle between the vectors and the parallelogram area. Relate the signed z-component to the determinant with a and b as its plane columns.
Solution to Exercise 3.9
The dot product is 2(1)+1(−2)=0. Both vectors are nonzero, so their angle is 90∘. The cross product has only a z-component: That component is
2(−2)−1(1)=−5,
so
a×b=(0,0,−5).
Its magnitude, and therefore the parallelogram area, is 5. The negative sign selects the negative z direction; it is not a negative area. The matrix with plane columns (2,1) and (1,−2) has determinant −5, the same signed component. Reversing the columns reverses the sign while preserving area.

Return to Exercise 3.9

Exercise 3.10 · What did the check establish? 2 min

A program reports zero residual for (12) in the system x+y=3, 2x+2y=6. A learner concludes, “I have found the unique solution.” Give a counterexample and replace that conclusion by one justified statement.
Solution to Exercise 3.10
The distinct candidate (30) also satisfies both equations. The justified conclusion is that the checked candidate is a solution of the stated system. It is not unique: all pairs (3−t,t) work. A zero residual tests membership; it does not count or classify the other possible solutions.

3.35 Repair a check and explain its limits

In Lesson 5 you kept both entries of a residual. The next task checks whether the decision made from those entries is equally careful. The supplied program uses familiar flat lists in row order, [a, b, c, d], and vectors in [x, y] order. All inputs here are modest integers, so the exact zero test is appropriate for these examples.

Download the repair notebook · Download the companion with labelled faulty and repaired checks. Save a working copy. The notebook contains the same predictions, repair and interpretation as Activity 3.36; it is not additional study.

Deliberately faulty code for diagnosis. The arithmetic routine returns the residual entries, but the final decision contains an error.

def residual2(m, candidate, target):
    first = m[0] * candidate[0] + m[1] * candidate[1]
    second = m[2] * candidate[0] + m[3] * candidate[1]
    return [first - target[0], second - target[1]]


def faulty_accepts(m, candidate, target):
    r = residual2(m, candidate, target)
    # Deliberate fault: diagnose before repairing.
    return r[0] == 0 or r[1] == 0

Activity 3.36 · One equation is not the whole system 30 min Python

  1. Predict and trace (10 min). With m = [1, 2, 0, 1] and target = [5, 2], calculate residuals for candidates [1, 2] and [3, 1]. Predict what the faulty function returns. Explain each entry and why one case is revealing.
  2. Repair and test (10 min). Keep the residual function and replace the faulty decision with a function called satisfies_both. Test both candidates. Then use m = [1, 1, 2, 2], target = [3, 6] and candidates [0, 3] and [1, 2]. Record the output you expect before running each call.
  3. Reproduce and interpret (10 min). Restart and run from the first cell. Label the deliberately faulty results separately from your corrected final results. Explain why two successful candidates disprove uniqueness for the dependent system, and why rejection of one candidate would not prove that no other candidate works. Keep your corrected code and explanation together.
Hint for Activity 3.36

Read or literally: how many of its two comparisons need to be true? For candidate [3, 1], the first equation works. Check the second before accepting the candidate.

Return to Activity 3.36

Discussion of Activity 3.36

The first matrix sends [1, 2] to [5, 2], so its residual is [0, 0]. It sends [3, 1] to [5, 1], giving [0, -1]. The faulty function returns True in both cases because the first residual entry is zero. This accepts a candidate that fails the second equation.

The condition must require both entries to vanish:

def satisfies_both(m, candidate, target):
    r = residual2(m, candidate, target)
    return r[0] == 0 and r[1] == 0

The corrected results are True and False. For the second matrix, both proposed candidates give output [3, 6] and residual [0, 0], so both return True. They are distinct solutions of the same system; therefore its solution is not unique. In fact, x+y=3 describes all its solutions and the second equation is redundant.

The repaired function checks membership in the solution set, not uniqueness and not the suitability of a physical model. A failed candidate supplies evidence only about that input. Restarting removes dependence on earlier notebook state; it does not turn these finite test cases into a proof about all possible programs or floating-point inputs.

Answer and reasoning for iCMA 01 Question 6

Answer A. The product is BA: its first row is (−1,−1) and its second is (0,1). The second alternative is AB; the third adds entries instead of composing the actions.

Which operation always preserves the solution set of a real linear system?

Answer and reasoning for iCMA 01 Question 7

Answer B. Adding three times another row is reversed by subtracting those three copies. Multiplying by zero discards an equation; replacing a nonzero right-hand side by zero changes the problem.

3.37 Unit 3 quick reference

30 minutes. Spend five minutes recalling the main rules without looking, ten comparing them with the table, ten reconstructing one method from its reasoning, and five choosing what to revisit. For reconstruction, you might explain why the rightmost matrix acts first, why a row operation is reversible, or why a zero determinant alone does not decide consistency.

Download the matching two-page reference PDF. Recall the central rules; understand the arguments that support them; consult the compact coding details when needed. The web table and PDF use the same reference entries. Conditions are part of each result.

Dimensions and columns
A=(abcd),A(xy)=(ax+bycx+dy)

m rows × n columns: n input entries, m output entries. Entry aᵢⱼ: row i, column j. Coordinate-vector images are the columns.

Composition
(BA)v=B(Av)

Rightmost acts first. Inner dimensions must match. AB and BA can differ. Identity I leaves every compatible vector unchanged.

Determinant and inverse
Δ=ad−bc,A−1=1Δ(d−b−ca)

Inverse formula requires Δ ≠ 0. Then every target has exactly one input. Check AA⁻¹ = A⁻¹A = I. With Δ = 0, inspect the target: no solution or infinitely many.

Solution-preserving operations
Ri↔Rj,Ri←kRi (k≠0)
Ri←Ri+kRj(i≠j)

Operate on whole equations, including right-hand sides. Keep the other row in a row replacement. Each operation is reversible.

Elimination and solution sets
0=c (c≠0)⇒no solution

0 = 0 adds no condition. Keep the remaining equations. Back-substitute when each unknown is fixed; otherwise describe allowed free parameters. Verify against the originals.

A complete parameter family
x+2y=6⟺(x,y)=(6−2t,t)

t ranges over all real numbers. Check both directions: every parameter gives a solution; every solution has such a parameter. Sample pairs alone do not prove completeness.

Area and orientation in the plane
image area=|Δ|original area

Same perpendicular, equal-scale coordinates. Positive Δ preserves orientation; negative reverses it; zero collapses area. Area scale does not specify length or angle changes.

r=Axcandidate−b

Every entry zero: candidate satisfies these equations. This does not prove uniqueness. A failed candidate does not prove inconsistency. Small residual need not mean small input error.

3.38 Looking back, then looking ahead

The street grid at the opening of this unit invited us to imagine a systematic change applied to a whole diagram. We can now describe such a change by giving the images of the coordinate vectors. Every other input is built from those vectors, so the columns of a matrix carry the rule. Applying one rule after another gives a matrix product whose order preserves the order of actions.

Working backwards exposed a different issue: information may have been lost. A nonzero two-by-two determinant means that the unit square has not collapsed and every target has one input. A zero determinant means that distinct inputs can share an output; the specified target then determines whether the equations have no solution or a family. Elimination makes that distinction visible through a contradiction or a redundant equation.

These ideas extend the habits of Units 1 and 2. A displacement needs ordered endpoints. A product needs the intended quantity and orientation. A system needs all its equations. In every case, the meaning of the inputs and the claim being checked determine what counts as a convincing answer. Neither a plausible picture nor a successful line of code removes that responsibility.
