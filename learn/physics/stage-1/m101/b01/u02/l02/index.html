M101 · Block 1 · Unit 2: Products, projections and orientation

Lesson 2
Signed projections and perpendicular parts

A displacement can carry you partly along a chosen route and partly away from it. The ordinary horizontal component answers “how much eastwards?” But a route need not run east. How can we ask the same question about any specified direction?

In Lesson 1, the scalar product combined a signed component with the length of a second vector. We will now remove that second length. The result will measure a contribution along a direction of our choosing. We will then turn this number into an arrow and find the part of the original vector that remains.

Allow about three hours: one hour for the explanations and examples, ninety minutes for the activities, and thirty minutes for feedback, corrections and recall. Keep paper beside you and sketch the directions before calculating. You will use vector subtraction, unit vectors and the scalar-product rules from the preceding lessons; no programming is required. Unless units are supplied, vectors are dimensionless. All components refer to the same perpendicular unit axes.

2.8 A component along a chosen direction

Start with a displacement a=⟨−3,4⟩m, on axes pointing east and north. Its eastward component is −3m. That is useful information: the displacement takes us three metres west, despite having a northward part as well. Its length is 5m, a different quantity. Neither number replaces the other.

Label the tail O and the tip A. Now imagine drawing a new axis through O, along a direction indicated by a nonzero vector b. Choose positive along b. From the tip of a, draw a perpendicular to the whole line through this axis, extending it backwards if necessary. Call the intersection F, the perpendicular foot. Reading F's signed coordinate on that axis gives the signed scalar projection of a along b.

The word “scalar” tells us that this answer is a number, with units when appropriate. The word “signed” matters just as much. A foot ahead of the origin gives a positive number; one behind gives a negative number. The distance OF is always nonnegative and is the absolute value of that number. The line continues beyond the end of the arrow used to label b: the arrow selects a direction, not a fence that restricts where the foot may lie.

If a is nonzero and θ is its tail-to-tail angle with b, the signed component rule from Unit 1 gives

s=|a|cosθ.

Here s denotes the signed scalar projection. For an acute angle, the usual right triangle gives a positive component. For an obtuse angle, the foot is on the negative side and cosine is negative. At 90° the foot is the origin and s=0. We do not take an absolute value unless the question asks for the projection's length.

Example 2.9 · A negative component is useful information

A displacement has magnitude 6 m and makes an angle of 120° with a marked positive direction. Find its signed scalar projection and the distance from the origin to its perpendicular foot.

Solution

The angle is obtuse, so we expect a negative component. Using cos120∘=−12,

s=(6m)(−12)=−3m.

The foot lies three metres on the negative side of the origin. Its distance from the origin is |s|=3m. The first answer records an orientation; the second measures a length. Saying only “the projection is 3 m” would leave unclear which question had been answered.

Activity 2.9 · Locate the foot

Allow about 10 minutes.

Draw a horizontal line with positive direction to the right. A vector of length 8 has tail at the origin.

  1. Sketch it at 60°, 90° and 150° to the positive direction. Mark each perpendicular foot and calculate its signed coordinate. Keep exact values where possible.
  2. State the three distances from the origin to the feet.
  3. The arrow marking the positive direction is drawn only two units long. Does this change any answer? Explain.
Discussion of Activity 2.9

The coordinates are 8cos60∘=4, 8cos90∘=0, and 8cos150∘=−43≈−6.93. The corresponding distances are 4, 0 and 43. The last foot is left of the origin. The two-unit arrow specifies orientation; the axis is an entire line. A foot beyond that arrow's tip is therefore allowed, and drawing a longer reference arrow would change none of these coordinates.

A sketch makes the sign visible when an angle is supplied. Often, however, we have components rather than an angle. The scalar product lets us find the same signed coordinate directly.

2.9 Let the direction have unit length

The magnitude of b played no part in locating the foot. We can remove it by replacing b with its unit vector,

b^=b|b|,b≠0.

The hat marks a vector of length one. Dividing every component by the same positive magnitude preserves direction. If b carries units, those units cancel in this ratio: b^ describes direction alone.

For nonzero a, the geometric dot-product rule now becomes a·b^=|a|cosθ, because |b^|=1. This is precisely the signed component we wanted. The algebraic form remains valid for a=0: a zero displacement has zero component along any specified nonzero direction, even though it has no angle of its own.

Definition 2.5 · Signed scalar projection

For any vector a and a nonzero reference vector b,

s=a·b^=a·b|b|.

This is the signed scalar projection of a along b. It has the units of a. If a is nonzero, it also equals |a|cosθ. Its sign records orientation along the chosen axis; its absolute value is a length or magnitude.

Notice which magnitude appears in the denominator. We divide by the magnitude of the reference vector, b. In Lesson 1, dividing by both magnitudes isolated the cosine of an angle. Here we want to retain the size of a's contribution, so we remove only the arbitrary length of the direction marker.

Example 2.10 · Measuring along a tilted axis

Find the signed component of a=⟨4,1⟩m along the direction b=⟨3,4⟩.

Solution

The reference vector has length 5, giving b^=⟨3/5,4/5⟩. Dotting the displacement with this unit direction gives

s=⟨4,1⟩·⟨35,45⟩m=(125+45)m=165m=3.2m.

Equivalently, calculate a·b=16m and divide by |b|=5. The positive result places the foot along the marked positive direction. It is not the eastward component 4 m, because we asked about a different axis. We did not need to find the angle first.

The original coordinate axes remain useful for arithmetic. The direction along which we measure need not be one of them. This is the gain from the dot product: it computes a component along a tilted axis without requiring us to redraw every vector in a new coordinate system.

Activity 2.10 · Which length should be removed?

Allow about 12 minutes.

Let a=⟨−2,5⟩m and b=⟨4,3⟩.

  1. Find b^ and the signed scalar projection of a along b.
  2. Find the signed scalar projection along the opposite direction −b. Interpret the difference.
  3. A learner divides a·b by |a| instead. What quantity have they computed, and why does it not answer the first question?
Discussion of Activity 2.10

Since |b|=5, its unit direction is ⟨4/5,3/5⟩. The signed component is [−2(4)+5(3)]/5=7/5m. Reversal gives −7/5m: the chosen positive direction changed, while the displacement stayed fixed. Dividing by |a|=29m would give 7/29, a dimensionless number here. By symmetry of the dot product, that is the signed scalar projection of b along a. The roles of vector being projected and reference direction have been exchanged.

2.10 Turning the number into a vector

A signed number tells us how far along an oriented axis to go. To write the corresponding movement in the original components, we must also supply that axis's direction. Multiply the number s by the unit direction b^:

p=sb^.

The result is the arrow from the original tail O to the perpendicular foot F. We call it the vector projection of a onto b, and denote it by projba. The subscript identifies the reference direction. In the rest of this lesson, p will be a shorter name for this vector.

For positive s, p points along b; for negative s, it points the other way along the same line. When s=0, p=0. Thus “parallel part” includes an opposite-directed part and the zero part; it does not promise an arrow pointing forwards. Its magnitude is |p|=|s|, because the unit vector has magnitude one.

Substituting both formulas from the previous section gives a calculation that does not require square roots:

p=(a·b|b|)b|b|=a·b|b|2b=a·bb·bb.

There are two divisions by |b| for two different reasons. The first extracts a signed component from the dot product. The second normalises the vector we multiply by. Forgetting the second division would multiply the correct component by a direction arrow of the wrong length.

Definition 2.6 · Vector projection

For b≠0, the vector projection is

p=projba=a·bb·bb.

It lies along the line of b, has magnitude equal to the absolute value of the signed scalar projection, and has the units of a. A scalar projection is a number; a vector projection has components and direction unless it is zero.

Example 2.11 · Follow the sign into the arrow

Find both projections of a=⟨−5,0⟩ along or onto b=⟨1,2⟩.

Solution

The dot product is −5 and |b|=5, so the signed scalar projection is s=−5/5=−5. For the vector projection, use b·b=5:

p=−55⟨1,2⟩=⟨−1,−2⟩.

This arrow points opposite to b, as the negative scalar projection predicted. Its magnitude is (−1)2+(−2)2=5, not −5. The coefficient multiplying b is −1; the signed scalar projection is −5. They are different numbers because b is not a unit vector.

For the tilted-axis displacement in Example 2.10, multiplying 16/5m by ⟨3/5,4/5⟩ gives p=⟨48/25,64/25⟩m. Both entries are needed to describe an arrow along that tilted line. A one-direction contribution need not have only one nonzero component on the original axes.

Activity 2.11 · Keep the three outputs separate

Allow about 14 minutes.

Take a=⟨1,−4⟩ and b=⟨2,1⟩.

  1. Predict whether the projection points along or against b, then calculate the signed scalar projection, vector projection and its magnitude.
  2. A proposed vector answer is −25⟨2,1⟩. Explain exactly what went wrong and repair it.
  3. Now project b onto a. Is the answer the same vector? Explain why symmetry of the dot product does not settle this question.
Hint for Activity 2.11

Write the reference vector underneath each projection symbol. The denominator and final vector factor must both belong to that reference vector.

Discussion of Activity 2.11

The dot product is 2−4=−2. Thus s=−2/5 and

p=−25⟨2,1⟩=⟨−45,−25⟩.

It points against b and has magnitude 2/5. The proposed answer multiplies the correct signed scalar by b instead of its unit vector; divide that vector factor by 5 as well. In reversed order, a·a=17, so

projab=−217⟨1,−4⟩=⟨−217,817⟩.

The dot-product numerator stays −2, but both the denominator and reference line change. Vector projection is not a symmetric operation.

2.11 What remains is perpendicular

The projected arrow accounts for the part along the chosen line. It usually does not reach the tip of a. The remaining arrow, from the foot F to that tip A, completes the journey. By the rule for subtracting position vectors, it is

q=a−p.

Consequently a=p+q. We have decomposed the original vector into two parts. The construction placed FA at right angles to the reference line. We should also be able to see that perpendicularity in the calculation.

Use distributivity to dot the remainder with the reference vector. Since p is a scalar multiple of b, its scalar factor can be taken outside the product:

q·b=(a−p)·b=a·b−p·b=a·b−a·bb·b(b·b)=0.

The cancellation is valid because b·b>0 for nonzero b. If q is nonzero, this zero dot product establishes that it is perpendicular to b. If q=0, no perpendicular movement remains; the equation is still true, but we do not assign an angle to the zero arrow.

Example 2.12 · Complete a decomposition in space

Separate a=⟨4,2,0⟩ into its projection onto b=⟨1,1,1⟩ and the perpendicular remainder. Check both parts.

Solution

First find the coefficient of the reference vector. The product a·b=6 and the squared reference magnitude b·b=3 give

p=63⟨1,1,1⟩=⟨2,2,2⟩.

Subtract this vector component by component:

q=⟨4,2,0⟩−⟨2,2,2⟩=⟨2,0,−2⟩.

The parts reconstruct the input: ⟨2,2,2⟩+⟨2,0,−2⟩=⟨4,2,0⟩. The remainder passes the independent direction check, q·b=2+0−2=0. The projection lies on the required line because it is 2b. Notice the third components: +2 and −2 cancel. An input with third component zero can still have a projection with a nonzero third component when the reference line is tilted out of the original plane.

Checking only that the two answers add to a is too weak. For any proposed p, defining q=a−p guarantees that sum, even if p points in the wrong direction. A complete check asks three things: does p lie along the reference line, does the sum reconstruct a, and does q·b=0?

Result 2.7 · Parallel part and perpendicular remainder

For a specified nonzero direction b,

p=a·bb·bb,
q=a−p.

The projection lies along the line of b, the two parts sum to a, and q·b=0. The zero remainder is allowed. These conditions determine the two parts uniquely.

To see why the last sentence holds, write any proposed parallel part as cb, where c is an unknown scalar. Requiring its remainder to satisfy (a−cb)·b=0 gives a·b−c(b·b)=0. Solving for c gives exactly the coefficient above. There is only one value because its denominator is positive. This supplies a second route to the projection formula, starting from the condition on the remainder.

Activity 2.12 · Check the decomposition, not just the sum

Allow about 14 minutes.

Take a=⟨3,−1,4⟩ and b=⟨2,0,1⟩.

  1. Find p and q. Verify the three checks described above.
  2. Another decomposition is p′=⟨3,0,0⟩, q′=⟨0,−1,4⟩. It has the correct sum, and p′·q′=0. Why is it still not the requested decomposition?
Discussion of Activity 2.12

The dot product is 10 and b·b=5, giving p=2b=⟨4,0,2⟩. The remainder is q=⟨−1,−1,2⟩. Their sum is ⟨3,−1,4⟩, the projection is explicitly a multiple of b, and q·b=−2+0+2=0. The alternative parts are perpendicular to each other, but p′ is not parallel to b: no single scalar multiplies ⟨2,0,1⟩ to give ⟨3,0,0⟩. Also, q′·b=4≠0. There are many ways to split a vector into perpendicular parts; the specified reference line selects the one required here.

2.12 Change the arrow, keep the line

A direction marker can be redrawn longer without moving its line. Reversing it also leaves the line in place, but exchanges which side is positive. These observations lead to different predictions for our two types of projection. The signed scalar component depends on the chosen positive direction; the projected arrow from O to F depends on the line and the original vector.

Example 2.13 · Two sign changes can preserve an arrow

For a=⟨2,3⟩ and b=⟨2,1⟩, compare the projections using b, 3b and −b as reference vectors.

Solution

With b, the product is 7 and the squared magnitude is 5. Hence s=7/5 and p=(7/5)⟨2,1⟩=⟨14/5,7/5⟩. For 3b, the product and magnitude both triple, giving s=21/(35)=7/5. Its vector projection is

2145⟨6,3⟩=⟨145,75⟩.

For −b, the product becomes −7 while the magnitude stays 5. Thus the signed scalar becomes −7/5. But the vector projection is

−75⟨−2,−1⟩=⟨145,75⟩.

Both the coefficient and the reference arrow reverse sign. Their product is the same projected arrow as before. The remainder also stays the same, because neither a nor p changed.

The result holds for any nonzero rescaling, not just these examples. Let the new reference be kb, with k≠0. Magnitude obeys |kb|=|k||b|, whereas the dot product changes by the signed factor k. Therefore the new scalar projection is

snew=k|k|s.

The ratio k/|k| is 1 for positive k and −1 for negative k. The vector calculation has another factor k from the final vector:

projkba=k(a·b)k2(b·b)(kb)=projba.

All factors of k cancel. We are projecting onto the same entire line, including its negative side. This is why the result is unchanged under negative as well as positive rescaling. Projection onto a forward ray or a finite segment would be a different question and is not what these formulas compute.

Activity 2.13 · Predict what changes

Allow about 14 minutes.

Use a=⟨−1,4⟩ and b=⟨1,2⟩.

  1. Calculate s, p and q for this pair.
  2. Predict their values when only the reference becomes −2b. Verify the projection vector by direct calculation.
  3. Return to the original reference and replace only the input by 2a. What happens to the three outputs?
  4. Explain why none of the rescaling conclusions permits setting k=0 for the reference.
Discussion of Activity 2.13

The product is 7, so s=7/5, p=⟨7/5,14/5⟩ and q=⟨−12/5,6/5⟩. With reference ⟨−2,−4⟩, the product is −14 and the squared magnitude is 20. Thus (−14/20)⟨−2,−4⟩=⟨7/5,14/5⟩: the vector and remainder stay fixed, while s changes to −7/5. Doubling the input instead doubles all three outputs: 14/5, ⟨14/5,28/5⟩ and ⟨−24/5,12/5⟩. That changes the vector being decomposed. A zero reference would have no direction and would make both projection formulas divide by zero.

2.13 Zero cases and useful bounds

Before using a general formula on complicated data, try cases whose geometry you already know. If a lies along the reference line, its foot is its own tip. The projection is all of a and the remainder is zero, whether a points along or against b. If the two nonzero inputs are perpendicular, the foot is the origin, so the projection is zero and the remainder is all of a.

Two zero-input cases must be separated. A zero vector being projected gives s=0, p=0 and q=0 for any nonzero reference. A zero reference is not permitted: it specifies no line. A zero numerator in a formula cannot rescue a zero denominator.

Example 2.14 · A zero that is an answer, and one that is not

For reference b=⟨1,−2⟩, compare inputs a=⟨2,1⟩ and c=⟨−3,6⟩. Then consider projecting b onto the zero vector.

Solution

For a, the dot product is 2−2=0, so its vector projection is zero and its remainder is ⟨2,1⟩. The two nonzero inputs are perpendicular. For c=−3b, the vector already lies on the reference line. Algebra confirms this: c·b=−15, b·b=5, so p=−3b=c and the remainder is zero. Its signed scalar projection is −15/5=−35, recording opposition. Finally, projection onto 0 is undefined. “Zero projection” and “projection not defined” describe different situations.

A further check concerns size. For nonzero a, the cosine formula gives |s|=|a||cosθ|≤|a|. A projected part cannot be longer than the vector from which it came. The zero input also satisfies the inequality directly. It applies to the whole magnitude, not separately to each coordinate: Example 2.12 already showed a projected third component larger than the input's zero third component.

The two parts also satisfy Pythagoras. Expanding the squared magnitude of their sum by the identity from Lesson 1 gives

|a|2=|p+q|2=|p|2+2p·q+|q|2=|p|2+|q|2.

The middle term vanishes because p is a multiple of b and q·b=0. This reasoning includes either part being zero. It provides a useful magnitude check, though it does not replace checking that the projection uses the correct reference line.

Result 2.8 · Conditions and magnitude checks

The reference must be nonzero. The input and either output may be zero. For the correctly calculated parts,

|p|=|s|≤|a|,
|a|2=|p|2+|q|2.

Use these as checks alongside the reference-line, reconstruction and dot-product checks. No single numerical check establishes every condition.

Activity 2.14 · Test the boundaries

Allow about 12 minutes.

  1. For reference ⟨1,0,0⟩, state the projections and remainders of ⟨−3,0,0⟩, ⟨0,2,−1⟩ and 0.
  2. Use the parts p=⟨2,2,2⟩, q=⟨2,0,−2⟩ from Example 2.12 to check Pythagoras numerically.
  3. Someone reports a projection of magnitude 7 for an input of magnitude 5. Another person says a zero reference gives a zero projection. Explain why each claim needs correction.
Discussion of Activity 2.14

The first input is entirely on the reference line: projection ⟨−3,0,0⟩, remainder zero, signed component −3. The second has no component along the line: projection zero, remainder ⟨0,2,−1⟩, signed component zero. The zero input gives zero for all three outputs. For Example 2.12, |a|2=20, |p|2=12 and |q|2=8, so 20=12+8. A magnitude of 7 exceeds the permitted input magnitude 5 and cannot be this perpendicular projection. A zero reference supplies no direction; the computation is undefined, not a defined zero result.

2.14 Read a projection as a measurement

Projection is a way of asking a precise question about a vector. “How far along the marked positive direction?” asks for a signed scalar. “What vector accounts for that part?” asks for p. “What must be added to it to recover the original?” asks for q. The arithmetic begins similarly, but the requested answers have different meanings.

Example 2.15 · Along a survey line and away from it

On a flat plan, east and north are the positive coordinate directions. A fictional survey displacement is d=⟨1,7⟩m. A reference line through its starting point is oriented along b=⟨4,3⟩. Find the signed advance along this direction, its vector part and the perpendicular remainder. How far is the finishing point from the reference line?

Solution

The direction vector has magnitude 5. The dot product is [1(4)+7(3)]m=25m, so the signed advance is s=5m. Its vector form is

p=2525⟨4,3⟩m=⟨4,3⟩m.

Subtracting gives q=⟨−3,4⟩m. It passes the direction check, since (−3)(4)+4(3)=0. Its magnitude is 5m. Thus the finishing point is five metres from the line, measured perpendicularly. The original displacement has magnitude 50m; this is neither the five-metre advance nor the five-metre separation.

These are parts of one displacement. They do not claim that a surveyor actually walked first along the line and then sideways. The same start and finish could be connected by a direct route or many indirect routes. Vector decomposition alone does not describe the path travelled.

Why does the perpendicular remainder give the distance to the line? Label the finishing point A. Let F be the perpendicular foot and choose another point G on that line. The segment FG runs along the line, while FA is perpendicular to it. Pythagoras gives AG2=AF2+FG2. The extra square cannot be negative and is zero only when G is F. Thus the perpendicular segment is the shortest connection. This argument concerns the line through the vector's tail, not an arbitrary parallel line elsewhere.

A wider view · optional context

Is a projection always a shadow?

The photograph offers a familiar image of something being projected onto a surface. But a shadow follows the direction of the light. That direction need not be perpendicular to the surface receiving it. In our vector construction, the guide from the tip must meet the reference line at a right angle.

To compare the two ideas, imagine the side view of a tilted rod above a level baseline. Drop perpendicular guides from its ends to obtain the projection studied here. Now let parallel light rays fall obliquely towards the baseline. Their intersection points generally give a different projected segment. Depending on the illumination, an ordinary shadow can be longer than the rod. That does not contradict our magnitude bound: a different geometrical operation has been used.

The lunar photograph also includes relief and perspective. We cannot infer a perpendicular component simply by measuring its visible shadow. The useful habit is to ask which line or surface receives the projection and in what direction its points are carried there.

Activity 2.15 · Explain the measurement

Allow about 14 minutes.

A displacement on an east–north plan is d=⟨−2,6⟩m. A reference line through the start has positive direction b=⟨3,4⟩.

  1. Calculate the signed advance, vector projection and remainder. Find the perpendicular distance from the finish to the line. Check the answer with both a dot product and squared magnitudes.
  2. An observer reverses the reference arrow. State exactly which answers change.
  3. A report calls the magnitude of d “the distance walked”. Explain what further information would make that claim valid. Another report replaces the negative eastward entry of the remainder by its absolute value. Does it still reconstruct the displacement?
Hint for Activity 2.15

Keep fractions until the checks are finished. A distance from a point to a line is the magnitude of the perpendicular remainder, whereas its components retain signs.

Discussion of Activity 2.15

The product is −6+24=18m and |b|=5. Therefore

s=185m,
p=⟨5425,7225⟩m,
q=⟨−10425,7825⟩m.

The perpendicular distance is

|q|=1042+78225m=265m.

The direction check gives [−104(3)+78(4)]/25=0. The magnitude check gives

|p|2+|q|2=324+67625m2=40m2.

This agrees with the input, whose squared magnitude is (4+36)m2=40m2. Reversing the reference changes only the signed advance, to −18/5m; the two vector parts and the distance stay fixed. The magnitude 40m is the straight-line separation of start and finish. It is the distance walked only if the path goes directly between them without detours or backtracking. Changing the remainder's first entry to +104/25 makes the reconstructed first entry 158/25, not −2. Taking a magnitude is not the same as taking absolute values of individual components.

Looking back and ahead

A unit direction turns the dot product into a signed component. Multiplying that component by the unit direction restores its vector form; subtracting that vector leaves a remainder with zero dot product against the reference. These are connected steps, each answering a different question about the same input.

Use the remaining feedback time to close the discussions and reconstruct those steps from a sketch. Explain why reversing the reference changes the signed scalar but preserves the vector projection. Then correct one uncertain activity and repeat its direction check without the solution in view.

So far we have selected a direction and separated a vector relative to it. In Lesson 3, two nonparallel vectors in space will instead determine a perpendicular direction together, using the cross product. Its orientation will require another convention. The substantial mixed exercise set and reference resources will follow at the end of Unit 2.
