Lesson 3
Cross products and orientation

A projection separates an arrow into a part along a line and a part perpendicular to it. Now place two arrows at the same starting point. Together they can mark out a flat patch. How can we describe both the size of that patch and the direction in which it faces?

The cross product answers this question with a vector. Its length measures a parallelogram area; its direction is perpendicular to the plane of the two arrows. There are two opposite perpendicular directions, so we need an agreed way to choose between them. Keeping track of that choice is as important as carrying out the arithmetic.

Allow about 3½ hours: 75 minutes for explanations and examples, 105 minutes for activity attempts, and 30 minutes for checking, correction and retrieval. The activity times are guides, not deadlines. You will use three-dimensional components, unit vectors, the dot product and perpendicular projections from the preceding lessons. No determinants or programming are assumed.

2.15 Choosing an orientation in space

Imagine placing a book flat on a table. An arrow along one edge and an arrow along an adjacent edge describe two directions in its surface. An arrow straight away from the cover describes something different: the direction of the surface itself. Such a perpendicular direction is called a normal. Here “normal” means perpendicular, not usual or average.

There is an equally good perpendicular arrow pointing down through the table. Geometry alone has not chosen between them. We need to distinguish the two sides of the plane. First we establish a convention for our coordinate axes; then we will use it consistently for any pair of vectors.

Take the positive x-axis to the right and the positive y-axis towards the top of the page. The positive z-axis points out of the page towards you. These axes form a right-handed coordinate system. To check the convention, curl the fingers of your right hand from positive x towards positive y through a quarter-turn. Your thumb points towards positive z. The drawing shows only a flat view of this arrangement: the third axis is perpendicular to the sheet.

A dot inside a circle represents the tip of an arrow coming towards you. A cross inside a circle represents its tail going away from you. These symbols say which way an arrow points relative to your view; neither symbol says that its length is one. We will label unit directions explicitly.

Rotating a right-handed set of axes in space does not change its handedness. You can turn your hand with the axes and preserve the same relationship. Reversing just one axis does change it. In particular, keeping x rightwards and y upwards while pointing positive z into the page would produce a left-handed arrangement. We will use right-handed axes throughout this lesson.

Example 2.16 · Read the viewing direction

A diagram is viewed from the positive z side towards the origin. Positive x points right and positive y points up. Which way does a right-hand curl from x to y point? What changes if you look at the same axes from the other side?

Solution

From the stated viewpoint, the turn from right to up is anticlockwise. The right thumb points towards the viewer, along positive z. Walking to the other side does not reverse the actual axes. Positive z now points away from the viewer, and the same turn looks clockwise. The change is in the view, not the coordinate convention.

This is why “clockwise” without a viewing direction is incomplete information in three dimensions. A marked normal or a specified viewpoint removes the ambiguity.

In the first drawing, i points right, j up and k out of the page. For the second drawing, the turn from right to down is clockwise as you look at the sheet. The right thumb therefore points into the page; use a circled cross for positive z.

Coordinate axes are chosen reference directions. They may be tilted, or attached to a flat drawing as here. The required relationship is their handedness and mutual perpendicularity, not their alignment with the room. A satisfactory explanation should specify all three positive directions and the viewing side.

2.16 A product that measures area

Put two nonzero, nonparallel vectors a and b tail to tail. Complete a parallelogram by copying each arrow at the tip of the other. Its base can be taken to have length |a|. Its height is the perpendicular distance from the tip of b to the line of a.

That height is already familiar. In Lesson 2 we separated a vector into its projection onto a line and the remainder. Here write b⟂ for the remainder after projecting b onto a. The height is |b⟂|, so the area is |a||b⟂|. A longer part along the base shears the parallelogram sideways, but does not add height.

Let θ be the angle between the two input vectors, with 0∘≤θ≤180∘. The right triangle formed by the height gives |b⟂|=|b|sinθ. For an obtuse angle, the perpendicular foot lies on the extension of the base behind the origin. The triangle then uses the acute angle 180∘−θ, whose sine equals sinθ. The same height formula applies. Sine is nonnegative throughout this range.

We now attach a direction to the area. Curl your right-hand fingers from the first vector a towards the second vector b through their angle, less than 180∘. Your thumb selects one of the two normals. The cross product, written a×b and read “a cross b”, points in that direction and has the area as its magnitude. The symbol between the vectors denotes this new vector operation; it is not an instruction to multiply matching components.

Key result 2.9 · The geometric cross product

For nonzero, nonparallel vectors in three-dimensional space, a×b is perpendicular to both inputs, with direction selected by a right-hand curl from a to b. Its magnitude is

|a×b|=|a||b|sinθ.

This is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the inputs when they represent side displacements. If the inputs are parallel, or either is zero, define a×b=0. The zero result has no direction.

Swapping the inputs preserves the same parallelogram, so its area does not change. But the curl reverses. Thus the two cross products have equal magnitudes and opposite directions:

b×a=−(a×b).

For example, with the axes in Figure 2.14, i×j=k, while j×i=−k. The length of each is one because the input unit vectors are perpendicular. A negative sign in front of a vector reverses its direction; it does not make its magnitude negative.

Example 2.17 · Length, direction and units

Two side displacements have lengths 4m and 3m, with an angle of 30∘ between them. They lie in the xy-plane. Viewed from positive z, the turn from the first to the second is anticlockwise. Find their cross product and the area when their order is reversed.

Solution

The product magnitude follows from base times height:

|a×b|=4×3×sin30∘=6m2.

The anticlockwise turn, viewed from positive z, gives a normal in the positive z direction. Therefore

a×b=(6m2)k.

Reversal gives b×a=−(6m2)k. The area is still 6m2. Each input supplies one factor of metres. A cross product is not necessarily an area in physical interpretation: in general its units are the product of the input units. Here both inputs are lengths, which is why area units arise.

  2. Describe what happens as the angle approaches 0∘ and as it approaches 180∘.
  3. At 90∘, reverse the order of the inputs. State the new cross product and its magnitude separately.
Discussion of Activity 2.17

The area is 10sinθcm2, giving 5, 10 and 5cm2. The 30∘ and 150∘ parallelograms have the same height of 1cm; their different sideways slants do not affect their areas. Both limiting angles flatten the patch onto a line, so the area tends to zero. At the exact endpoints the cross product is zero and no normal direction is selected by it.

At 90∘ the original product is (10cm2)k. Reversal gives −(10cm2)k, whose magnitude remains 10cm2. If you wrote a negative magnitude, distinguish the signed component along k from the length of the resulting vector.

2.17 Building from the three axis directions

The right-hand convention gives a small set of products from which we can calculate others. The unit vectors i, j and k point along the positive x, y and z axes. Following those directions in the cyclic order i, j, k, and back to i, gives

i×j=k,
j×k=i,
k×i=j.

Each pair is perpendicular, so each product has magnitude one. In each case the right-hand curl chooses the positive direction of the remaining axis. Reading any pair in the reverse order gives the negative of the remaining unit vector. Crossing an axis direction with itself gives zero, because the parallelogram has collapsed.

We also need to know how a cross product behaves when an input is assembled from parts. It distributes over vector addition, and scalar factors can be taken outside. These are properties of the cross product, just as distributivity was a property of the dot product. We use them here to obtain a coordinate rule; the rule then lets us check the properties by ordinary expansion. Crucially, distributing never permits us to reverse the order of the vector factors.

Key result 2.10 · Scaling and distributing

For vectors in the same right-handed frame and real scalars r,s,

(ra)×(sb)=rs(a×b),
(a+b)×c=a×c+b×c,
a×(b+c)=a×b+a×c.

A negative overall scalar reverses the product direction. Reversing both input vectors leaves the product unchanged. Input order must be preserved in every term.

Scaling also has a geometric explanation. Doubling one side doubles the parallelogram area. Reversing one side reverses the orientation. Reversing both sides changes the sign twice. This gives a useful way to check algebra without repeating it.

Example 2.18 · Expand a small product

Calculate (2i−k)×j.

Solution

Distribute while leaving j on the right in both terms:

(2i−k)×j=2(i×j)−(k×j)=2k−(−i)=i+2k.

In the second term, k×j=−i because the pair runs backwards through the cyclic order. The subtraction already outside that product then turns it into +i. Keeping these two sources of minus signs separate avoids losing one.

The result has no j component, as it must to be perpendicular to j. It is also perpendicular to 2i−k: the dot product is 2(1)+(−1)(2)=0. Its magnitude 5 equals the product of the input lengths 5 and 1, since these inputs are perpendicular.

Hint for Activity 2.18

Let u=i+2j and v=j−k. Part 3 asks for v×(−3u).

Discussion of Activity 2.18

The three short products are −k, j and 0. For part 2,

u×v=i×j−i×k+2j×j−2j×k=k+j+0−2i=−2i+j+k.

For part 3, reverse the order first or scale first; either route must account for both signs:

v×(−3u)=−3(v×u)=3(u×v)=−6i+3j+3k.

The factor is positive three relative to part 2. One sign came from swapping operands and one from the negative multiplier.

2.18 Calculating with components

Expanding into axis directions works for any pair, but writing every term each time is cumbersome. We can do the expansion once with general components and collect a reusable rule. Write

a=axi+ayj+azk,
b=bxi+byj+bzk.

The subscripts name the axes: ay is the y-component of the first vector, for example. Distributing gives nine terms. Three contain a basis vector crossed with itself, so they vanish. The remaining six come in pairs that point along the same axis.

For the x direction, only the products involving j and k survive. Their contributions are

aybz(j×k)+azby(k×j)

and therefore combine to (aybz−azby)i. For the y direction, the contributing pairs are k×i and i×k, giving (azbx−axbz)j. Finally, the i,j pairs give (axby−aybx)k. Each component uses the other two axes.

Key result 2.11 · Component rule in right-handed axes

If c=a×b, calculate the three components separately:

cx=aybz−azby,cy=azbx−axbz,cz=axby−aybx.

Then assemble c=⟨cx,cy,cz⟩. Both inputs must be expressed in the same right-handed, perpendicular unit-axis system.

Notice the order in the middle line: z,x, not x,z. All three lines follow the cycle x,y,z,x,y. If you instead write axbz−azbx in the middle, you get the negative of the required y-component. There is no extra minus sign to add to the formula as printed. Parentheses around negative input values help make each multiplication unambiguous.

Example 2.19 · Keep the three calculations separate

Find a×b for a=⟨2,−1,1⟩ and b=⟨1,2,2⟩. These vectors are dimensionless.

Solution

Label the first vector's components before substituting: ax=2, ay=−1, az=1. The second has bx=1, by=2, bz=2. Using the three lines of the rule,

cx=(−1)(2)−(1)(2)=−4,cy=(1)(1)−(2)(2)=−3,cz=(2)(2)−(−1)(1)=5.

Thus c=⟨−4,−3,5⟩. The final component is a subtraction of a negative number; it becomes 4+1, not 4−1. For its magnitude, square each component before adding; the squares of −4 and −3 are positive:

|c|=16+9+25=50=52.

We have a candidate normal with an area magnitude. Check it against both original vectors:

a·c=−8+3+5=0,
b·c=−4−6+10=0.

Both vanish. These are useful arithmetic checks, but they do not yet establish the sign of c: the opposite vector would pass too. The ordered component calculation supplies that sign.

Why do the perpendicularity checks work in general? Substitute the component formula into a·c. The expansion is

a·c=axaybz−axazby+ayazbx−ayaxbz+azaxby−azaybx.

The first and fourth terms cancel, as do the second and fifth and the third and sixth. These are products of real numbers, whose order may be changed. Hence the dot product is zero. The same cancellation occurs with b·c. When c is nonzero, this proves it is perpendicular to both inputs. When it is zero, the algebra remains valid but there is no angle to discuss.

The proposed answer has reversed the y-component. Its dot product with u is 4+10+6=20, so it fails. Its dot product with v is still zero because vy=0: checking only one input could have missed this particular error. Reversing the operands gives v×u=⟨−4,5,6⟩.

2.19 From an area vector to a unit normal

A cross product combines two pieces of information. Its magnitude describes the size of the spanned parallelogram, while its direction describes an orientation of the plane. Sometimes only the direction is wanted. As in Unit 1, we can divide a nonzero vector by its magnitude to obtain a unit vector:

n^=a×b|a×b|.

The hat marks a unit vector. Both n^ and −n^ are unit normals to the plane. The formula selects the one associated with the order a,b. If a question specifies “the normal with positive z-component”, you may need to choose the opposite one after calculating. If it asks specifically for a×b, you may not change the sign to suit a preference.

Consider an ideal flat patch on a panel such as the arrays in Figure 2.17. Two nonparallel edge displacements determine its plane. Multiplying their cross product by a nonzero scalar preserves its perpendicularity to that plane; a negative scalar reverses the selected side. Normalising it sets its length to one. This distinction lets us use a unit normal to describe a facing direction without also assigning the panel an area of one.

Example 2.20 · Describe a tilted patch

An ideal parallelogram patch has adjacent edge displacements

a=⟨2,0,0⟩m,
b=⟨1,2,2⟩m.

Find its area and the unit normal with positive z-component.

Solution

The first edge runs along x, so any component of the second edge along x adds no height. The remaining part is ⟨0,2,2⟩m, of magnitude 22m. This gives an independent geometric prediction: the area should be 2(22)=42m2.

Now calculate the cross product, using the numerical components in metres. Each output component will have units of square metres:

cx=0(2)−0(2)=0,cy=0(1)−2(2)=−4,cz=2(2)−0(1)=4.

Thus c=⟨0,−4,4⟩m2, with magnitude 32m2=42m2, agreeing with base times height. Its z-component is positive already. Dividing by the magnitude gives

n^=⟨0,−12,12⟩.

The units cancel in the division. Check that |n^|2=0+1/2+1/2=1. The negative y-component and positive z-component describe a tilt; the normal is not simply “up” because the patch itself is tilted.

Using base times perpendicular height is valuable when the orientation is difficult to picture. For Example 2.19, whose vectors have no physical units, Lesson 2 gives the projection coefficient of b onto a as (a·b)/(a·a)=2/6=1/3. Hence

b⟂=b−13a=⟨13,73,53⟩.

Its squared magnitude is (1+49+25)/9=25/3. Multiplying its magnitude by |a|=6 gives 6(5/3)=52, exactly the cross-product magnitude already found. The second method checks the size without repeating the three component subtractions. It does not check the chosen orientation.

  1. Calculate p×q and find the patch's area.
  2. Find both unit normals, then select the one with positive x-component.

Discussion of Activity 2.20

The cross product has components −3, 0, 3, so

p×q=⟨−3,0,3⟩m2.

The area is 32m2. This also equals the product of the edge lengths 2m and 3m, since p·q=0. The two unit normals are

⟨−12,0,12⟩

and

⟨12,0,−12⟩.

The second has positive x-component. Choosing that unit normal is allowed by part 2, but the proposed cross product in part 3 reverses the orientation set by the stated order. It is q×p. Neither perpendicularity nor magnitude alone distinguishes the two signs.

2.20 Plane vectors still need a third direction

Many area questions begin with only two coordinates. To use the three-dimensional cross product, place their plane in the xy-plane and append a zero z-component. This does not change their lengths or their angle. It records explicitly which three-dimensional plane contains them.

For a=⟨ax,ay,0⟩ and b=⟨bx,by,0⟩, the x and y components of their product vanish. Only one component remains:

a×b=⟨0,0,axby−aybx⟩.

The number axby−aybx is a signed area component. Its absolute value is the parallelogram area. Its sign tells us whether the normal points along positive or negative z. Viewed from positive z, a positive value corresponds to an anticlockwise turn from the first vector to the second through an angle less than 180∘; a negative value corresponds to a clockwise turn. The cross product itself is the whole three-component vector, not that single number.

Example 2.21 · Form edges from three positions

The vertices of a triangle are P=(1,1), Q=(5,2) and R=(2,4), with coordinates measured in metres. Find its area and the orientation of PQ→×PR→.

Solution

The coordinates describe positions, not adjacent edge vectors. Subtract the common starting point P to form the two sides:

PQ→=⟨5−1,2−1,0⟩=⟨4,1,0⟩m,
PR→=⟨2−1,4−1,0⟩=⟨1,3,0⟩m.

The only nonzero product component is 4(3)−1(1)=11, giving

PQ→×PR→=⟨0,0,11⟩m2.

It points out of the xy-plane towards positive z. The corresponding parallelogram has area 11m2. Its diagonal divides it into two equal triangles, so the requested triangle area is 11/2=5.5m2. Using the position vectors of Q and R without subtracting P would describe a different triangle, based at the coordinate origin.

Reversing the order of the two edges in this example would give a z-component of −11m2. It would describe the other orientation of the same triangle; it would not produce a negative geometric area. Translating all three vertices by the same displacement would leave both edge differences unchanged and therefore leave the area unchanged.

A triangular sheet has vertices A=(2,−1), B=(5,1) and C=(1,3) in centimetres.

  2. Repeat the orientation statement for AC→×AB→. Does the area change?
  3. Every vertex is shifted by ⟨10,−4⟩cm. Write the new coordinates and show that the edge vectors are unchanged.
  4. Explain why the triangle formula contains a factor of 1/2 and an absolute value when written using its signed z-component.
Hint for Activity 2.21

Subtract the coordinates of the chosen common vertex before multiplying. A product of position vectors from the origin would answer a different question.

Discussion of Activity 2.21

The side vectors are ⟨3,2,0⟩cm and ⟨−1,4,0⟩cm. Thus

cz=3(4)−2(−1)=14cm2.

The ordered product is ⟨0,0,14⟩cm2, along positive z. The triangle area is 7cm2. Reversing the sides gives negative z, with the same area.

The translated points are A′=(12,−5), B′=(15,−3) and C′=(11,−1). Subtracting A′ gives ⟨3,2⟩ and ⟨−1,4⟩ again. The common displacement cancels from each subtraction.

A triangle is half the parallelogram formed from its two adjacent sides, which accounts for 1/2. The absolute value removes the orientation sign while preserving the positive size. Both choices of order describe the same sheet.

2.21 What a zero product tells us

A cross product can vanish even when neither input does. Imagine sliding the two arrows into the same line. Their parallelogram becomes flat, whether they point in the same or in opposite directions. Algebraically, if b=λa, then

a×b=λ(a×a)=0.

For two nonzero inputs the converse also holds: zero cross product means the vectors are parallel. Their lengths are positive, so the magnitude formula can vanish only when sinθ=0. In the allowed angle range this means 0∘ or 180∘.

A zero input needs separate wording. It has no direction, so it has no angle with the other vector. The component rule still gives zero because every term includes a zero component from that input. We do not need to assign an angle to make the calculation work.

Key result 2.12 · Conditions at zero

For nonzero vectors, a×b=0 if and only if the vectors are parallel, including opposite directions. If either input is zero, the product is also zero, but the angle between the inputs is undefined.

A unit normal obtained by dividing the cross product by its magnitude exists only when the product is nonzero. Parallel inputs do not pick out a unique plane and its normal.

Two parallel arrows can lie in many different planes: rotate a sheet around their common line and it still contains them. The failure to normalise the zero result reflects this geometric ambiguity. It is not a request to invent a direction for zero. You may be able to choose some perpendicular vector by other information, but the two parallel inputs have not selected one.

Example 2.22 · Two different routes to zero

Compare a=⟨2,−1,3⟩, b=⟨−4,2,−6⟩ and o=0. What do a×b and a×o tell us?

Solution

Since b=−2a, the first pair is nonzero and points in opposite directions. Its angle is 180∘ and its cross product is zero. The component calculation confirms this:

cx=(−1)(−6)−3(2)=0,cy=3(−4)−2(−6)=0,cz=2(2)−(−1)(−4)=0.

The second cross product is also zero, but the angle between a and o is undefined. Neither pair yields a unit normal by normalising its cross product. The common numerical answer does not erase the difference between opposite nonzero arrows and an input with no direction.

These cases also help distinguish the two products taught in this unit. For two nonzero perpendicular vectors, the dot product is zero and the cross-product magnitude is as large as possible for the given lengths. For two nonzero parallel vectors, the cross product is zero and the dot product has its largest possible absolute value. The products answer different questions about the same pair.

Activity 2.22 · Diagnose the geometry before calculating

Allow about 15 minutes, followed by correction time.

  1. For a=⟨1,−2,2⟩ and b=⟨t,−4,4⟩, find the value of t for which the cross product is zero. State whether the nonzero vectors then point in the same or opposite directions.
  2. Compare i·j with i×j. Explain why zero in the first calculation does not imply zero in the second.
  3. A student obtains a zero cross product and immediately writes “the angle is 0∘”. Give two distinct reasons why this conclusion may fail.
  4. Return to one of your earlier nonzero products. Check its units, its dot products with both inputs, and the effect of reversing input order. Explain which of these checks, if any, would reveal an overall sign error.
Discussion of Activity 2.22

The y and z components of b are twice those of a. To be parallel, its x-component must also be twice as large, so t=2 and b=2a: the directions agree. Directly,

a×b=⟨0,2t−4,2t−4⟩,

which vanishes exactly when t=2. Next, i·j=0, whereas i×j=k. Their right angle makes cosine zero and sine one.

A zero cross product could come from opposite nonzero directions, giving 180∘, or from a zero input, giving no defined angle. These are the two missing possibilities in the student's claim.

For the final task, units and zero dot products cannot detect an overall sign reversal. Swapping the input order must reverse a correct product, but even a consistently sign-reversed rule can pass that test. To settle the sign, check the original ordered component formula or a right-hand orientation in a clear view. A useful check tests a particular feature; no single check establishes everything.

The cross product now gives us a way to describe a plane with an oriented normal and to recover an area from edge vectors. The connection to perpendicular projection explains its magnitude; the right-handed convention fixes its sign. In the next lesson we will choose between scalar products, projections and cross products according to what a question actually asks, paying attention to units and the type of answer required.

Before moving on, use the remaining feedback time to revisit a sign error or an uncertain sketch. Be able to explain why a nonzero cross product is perpendicular to both inputs, why swapping the inputs changes its sign, and why a zero result cannot be normalised. The cumulative exercise set and unit reference will follow at the end of Unit 2.
