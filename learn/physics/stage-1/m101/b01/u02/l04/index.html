Lesson 4
Choosing a product and explaining its meaning

Two vectors do not tell us which calculation to perform. We might want their angle, one vector's contribution along a line, or a normal to the plane they span. The same component data can answer all three questions, but the answers describe different things.

In this lesson, the main decision comes before the arithmetic. We will identify the quantity requested, choose a suitable operation, and explain what the result means. Two physical examples—work and torque—will make the distinction especially clear. Their definitions will be supplied here; you do not need to have studied the associated laws of motion.

This lesson has a 3½-hour allocation. Use about 60 minutes for explanations and examples, 90 minutes for the activities here, and 30 minutes for feedback and correction. Reserve the remaining 30 minutes of practice for the cumulative exercise set at the end of Unit 2. That reserved time is part of these hours, not extra work. No programming is required here.

2.22 Begin with the quantity being asked for

Suppose that a displacement is described by a and a reference direction by a nonzero vector b. “How much of a lies along b?” sounds like a simple question, but it needs one more distinction. Do we want a signed number on an oriented line, or the projected arrow in the original coordinate system?

The signed scalar component is a·b/|b|. It can be negative: that means the component points against the positive direction chosen by b. The vector projection is obtained by multiplying that number by the unit vector b/|b|. The number and the arrow describe related information, but neither can simply be substituted for the other.

A useful first sentence in a solution is therefore “The requested answer is a …”. Complete it with signed scalar, length, vector along a line, angle, or oriented normal. Then identify the reference direction or operand order. This short statement can prevent several lines of irrelevant arithmetic.

Different questions about the same vectors
What is required?A suitable starting point
An angle between two nonzero vectorsUse the dot product divided by the product of their magnitudes to obtain the cosine.
A signed component along a nonzero directionDot with a unit vector in that direction.
The projected vector along a lineMultiply the signed component by the unit direction, or use the projection formula.
An oriented normal in three dimensionsUse the ordered cross product. Normalise only if it is nonzero and a unit normal is wanted.
An area from adjacent side vectorsTake the cross-product magnitude; halve it for a triangle.

This is a guide to choosing a representation, not a list of interchangeable recipes. A length or area is nonnegative. A signed scalar may be negative. A nonzero vector needs a direction as well as a magnitude. An angle needs a range and, when obtained from a dot product, two nonzero inputs. These requirements come from the question rather than from the number of coordinates supplied.

Example 2.23 · Three answers from one pair

Let a=⟨−2,3,0⟩m and let the dimensionless direction vector be b=⟨2,0,0⟩. Find the dot product, the signed component of a along b, and its vector projection onto that line.

Solution

The direction vector has magnitude 2, so its unit vector is b^=⟨1,0,0⟩. The requested quantities are

a·b=(−4+0)m=−4m,
s=a·b^=−2m,
p=sb^=⟨−2,0,0⟩m.

Here the dot product and signed component both have units of metres, because b is dimensionless. Their values differ: the raw dot product includes the reference vector's length of two. The projected arrow points left, and its magnitude is 2m. Writing “the projection is −2” without saying whether a scalar component or a vector is meant leaves the answer incomplete.

There is another useful question to ask: should the answer change if we replace the reference arrow by a longer arrow pointing along the same line? A geometric projection onto the line should not. The raw dot product would change. This gives a way of detecting a wrong method even when its units look plausible.

Activity 2.23 · Name the answer before finding it

Allow about 12 minutes.

Take u=⟨3,−4,0⟩cm and the dimensionless direction v=⟨0,2,0⟩. For each part, first state whether your answer is a scalar or a vector.

  1. Find the signed component of u along v, its vector projection, and the magnitude of that projection.
  2. Find u·v. Explain why this number is not the signed component in part 1.
  3. Replace v by 3v. Predict which answers change before checking them.
Discussion of Activity 2.23

The unit direction is ⟨0,1,0⟩. The signed scalar component is −4cm, the projected vector is ⟨0,−4,0⟩cm, and its magnitude is 4cm. The dot product is −8cm: its second input has magnitude two, not one.

Tripling the reference vector triples the raw dot product to −24cm. It leaves the unit direction unchanged, so the signed component, vector projection and projection magnitude remain the same. Unit consistency alone would not have distinguished the two scalar answers; the reference-rescaling check does.

2.23 Keep the sign and test the zero

Taking a magnitude is a mathematical operation with a cost: it discards some information. The magnitude of a vector discards its direction. The absolute value of a signed scalar discards its sign. Either is appropriate when the question asks for a size, but neither is a harmless finishing step for every calculation.

For two nonzero vectors, the dot product distinguishes acute from obtuse angles. A negative value means that the angle exceeds 90∘. Replacing it by its absolute value makes an obtuse relationship look acute. By contrast, the cross-product magnitude cannot distinguish θ from 180∘−θ, because those angles have the same sine. The two operations preserve different information.

Example 2.24 · Recover the angle without losing its sign

Let a=⟨1,0,0⟩ and b=⟨−1,1,0⟩, both dimensionless. A student finds an angle of 45∘ using |a·b|/(|a||b|). Diagnose the error.

Solution

The dot product is −1, while the magnitudes are 1 and 2. We must retain the negative numerator:

cosθ=−12,θ=135∘.

The student's absolute value changed the cosine to a positive number. The sketch also contradicts an acute answer: b points partly against a. Meanwhile,

a×b=⟨0,0,1⟩.

Its magnitude gives sinθ=1/2, which agrees with both 45∘ and 135∘. Sine alone does not resolve this ambiguity. The positive z direction tells us the orientation of the turn, not whether its angle is acute.

Zero results need the same care. With nonzero inputs, a zero dot product means perpendicularity; a zero cross product means parallel directions, including opposite directions. A zero projection of a onto nonzero b means either that a is perpendicular to b or that a itself is zero. If the reference vector is zero, the projection formula is undefined, even though the dot and cross products with zero can still be evaluated.

Key result 2.13 · Check the requested information

Before calculating, identify the type of answer and its reference direction or order. Before interpreting a zero, check which inputs are nonzero. Take a magnitude or absolute value only when a size is requested; preserve signs and vector directions when they are part of the answer.

Activity 2.24 · Repair three explanations

Allow about 14 minutes. Give a counterexample or a corrected statement for each claim.

  1. “The dot product is negative, so its magnitude is negative.”
  2. “The cross product is zero, so the vectors are perpendicular.” Use the specific pair ⟨2,1,0⟩ and ⟨−6,−3,0⟩ in your response.
  3. “If a·b=0, the projection of a onto b is always defined and equals zero.” Include the case b=0.
  4. For a=⟨2,0,0⟩ and b=⟨−1,2,0⟩, calculate both products. State what each says about the angle and orientation without finding an inverse trigonometric value.
Discussion of Activity 2.24

A dot product is a signed scalar. Its absolute value is nonnegative; a negative product is not a negative length. The pair in part 2 consists of opposite parallel vectors, since the second is −3 times the first. Their cross product is zero, but their angle is 180∘, not 90∘.

In part 3, the projection requires b≠0. Under that condition the zero dot product does give the zero projected vector. With a zero reference, division by its squared magnitude is impossible: there is no line direction onto which to project.

For the final pair, a·b=−2 and a×b=⟨0,0,4⟩. Both inputs are nonzero, so the negative dot product means an obtuse angle. The positive z component means an anticlockwise turn from a to b, viewed from positive z. These conclusions describe different aspects of the pair.

2.24 Work: a force acting through a displacement

We now supply a physical meaning for one dot product. A force is a directed push or pull, measured in newtons, written N. For this lesson, take a force F that remains constant in both magnitude and direction while its point of application undergoes a displacement d. The work done by that force is defined, for this constant-force setting, by

W=F·d.

Work is a signed scalar associated with energy transfer by the force. Its unit is the joule, J, with 1J=1Nm. We will use the definition to interpret vector calculations; the wider energy laws belong to the physics sequence. In particular, this calculation concerns the specified force, not automatically the combined effect of every force on an object.

If the displacement is nonzero, let d^=d/|d|. Then

W=(F·d^)|d|.

The bracket is the signed component of the force along the displacement. Multiplying it by the displacement length gives the work. A perpendicular force component contributes zero because its dot product with the displacement is zero. A component against the displacement contributes negative work; taking an absolute value would change the physical answer.

Key result 2.14 · Constant-force work

For a force constant in magnitude and direction over the displacement of its point of application,

W=F·d.

For nonzero inputs this is |F||d|cosθ. Positive and negative work distinguish the force contribution along and against the displacement. If either vector is zero, W=0 without assigning an angle. Do not apply the single-force formula using one sampled force when the force varies along the motion.

Example 2.25 · A force can do negative work

A specified constant force is F=⟨−3,4,0⟩N. Its application point moves by d=⟨2,0,0⟩m. Find the work done by this force and explain the contribution of each force component.

Solution

The requested quantity is a signed scalar, so use the dot product. Multiplying the numerical components and retaining the force–length units gives

W=(−3×2+4×0)Nm=−6J.

The x-component of the force points left while the displacement points right. Its work is therefore negative. The y-component is perpendicular to the displacement and contributes zero. The force's vector projection along the displacement is ⟨−3,0,0⟩N; its signed component is −3N.

The full force has magnitude 5N. Multiplying this by 2m would give 10J, which ignores the angle. Taking the absolute value of the correct result would instead give 6J, which preserves the size but loses the required sign. Neither is the work done by the stated force.

A person can exert a force and become tired while holding an object still. The work defined here can nevertheless be zero if the force's application point has no displacement. “Work” in this equation is a precise physical quantity, not a measure of effort or fatigue. Equally, zero work by one force does not mean that no other force does work.

Activity 2.25 · Keep the force and the journey separate

Allow about 16 minutes.

A particle is subject to a specified constant force F=⟨6,−2,0⟩N. It moves from P=(1,2,0) to Q=(4,3,0), with positions in metres.

  1. Find the displacement and calculate the work by this force. Explain why using the position of Q itself would be wrong.
  2. Suppose it travels first to R=(4,2,0) and then to Q, with the same constant force throughout. Calculate the work on each part and compare the sum with part 1.
  3. If the force reverses for the second part only, find the new total. Explain why the original constant-force calculation over the whole journey is no longer applicable.
  4. For the original force, find a nonzero displacement that gives zero work, and check your choice.
Hint for Activity 2.25

Each part has its own displacement: subtract its starting position from its finishing position. Add the signed work values, not their absolute values.

Discussion of Activity 2.25

The total displacement is ⟨3,1,0⟩m, giving W=(18−2)J=16J. Position depends on the chosen origin; displacement describes the journey. Using Q would include the unrelated displacement from the origin to P.

The first part has displacement ⟨3,0,0⟩m and work 18J. The second has displacement ⟨0,1,0⟩m and work −2J. Their sum is 16J, as expected from distributivity of the dot product with the same force in both terms.

When the force reverses on the second part, its work becomes +2J, so the total is 20J. The force is constant on each part, but not across both; calculate those parts separately. One possible zero-work displacement is ⟨1,3,0⟩m, because 6(1)−2(3)=0. There are many correct nonzero perpendicular displacements.

2.25 Torque: a force acting at a position

A force can also have a turning effect about a chosen point. To describe that effect we need to know where the force acts, not how far its application point has moved. Choose a reference point O, let the force act at P, and define the position vector r=OP→. The torque of the force about O is defined by

τO=r×F.

The Greek letter τ is read “tau”. Torque is a vector. Its direction records the sense of the turning effect using the right-hand rule, and its unit is the newton metre, Nm. The order is position crossed with force. Reversing that order would describe the opposite torque.

For a plane example, put O at the origin, positive x rightwards, positive y upwards, and positive z towards you. An anticlockwise turning effect has a positive z torque component; a clockwise effect has a negative one. The torque vector points along the axis normal to the diagram. It is not an extra force pushing the object out of the page.

We are calculating the contribution of a specified force about a specified point at an instant. We are not deducing the resulting motion: other forces, constraints and the body's properties also matter. In particular, a nonzero torque from one force can exist while an object is held stationary. The wider rotational laws are not needed to calculate this vector.

Key result 2.15 · Torque about a stated point

With r measured from the reference point to the force's application point,

τO=r×F.

For nonzero r and F, its magnitude is |r||F|sinθ, where θ is their tail-to-tail angle. Always give the reference point and retain the vector direction or the signed component along a specified axis. A torque magnitude is never negative.

Example 2.26 · A downward force away from the origin

A force F=⟨0,−20,0⟩N acts at P=(0.40,0.30,0) in metres. Find its torque about the origin.

Solution

The position vector is r=⟨0.40,0.30,0⟩m. Both inputs are in the xy-plane, so only the z-component of their cross product can be nonzero:

τz=(rxFy−ryFx)=(−8.0−0)Nm=−8.0Nm.

Thus τO=⟨0,0,−8.0⟩Nm. Viewed from positive z, the downward force to the right of O has a clockwise turning effect. Its magnitude is 8.0Nm, without a minus sign.

The distance OP is 0.50m, but multiplying 0.50 by 20 would overestimate the torque. The force is not perpendicular to OP. We need the perpendicular distance from O to the force's line of action.

For a nonzero force, the line of action is the straight line through the application point parallel to the force. In Figure 2.23 it is the vertical line x=0.40m. Its perpendicular distance from O is ℓ=0.40m. This distance, called the perpendicular lever arm, gives a second expression for the torque magnitude:

|τO|=|F|ℓ.

To connect this with the cross product, form a triangle from O, P and the perpendicular foot on the line of action. Its perpendicular side has length ℓ=|r|sinθ. The product |F|ℓ is therefore |r||F|sinθ. For an obtuse angle the supplementary angle gives the same perpendicular distance, as in the area construction of Lesson 3.

The force diagram draws F at P, while the cross-product angle is defined with inputs tail to tail. You may translate a copy of the force arrow to O to read that angle; this does not move the actual force's application point. Keep those two uses of an arrow distinct. In this example the line-of-action method avoids calculating an angle altogether.

Example 2.27 · Change the reference point

Keep the force and application point from Example 2.26, but find the torque about Q=(0.10,0,0) metres.

Solution

Replace OP→ by QP→. The new position vector is

rQ=⟨0.30,0.30,0⟩m.

Its cross product with the same force has z-component −6.0Nm: the two component products are −6.0Nm and zero. The torque is ⟨0,0,−6.0⟩Nm, still clockwise in the stated view. The line of action has not moved, but its perpendicular distance from the new reference point is 0.30m rather than 0.40m.

Changing the reference point changes the question. It is different from merely relabelling coordinates while continuing to calculate about the same physical point.

If the line of action passes through the reference point, the lever arm is zero. The force may be nonzero, but its torque about that point vanishes. If the force acts at the reference point itself, r=0 and the component formula gives the same conclusion without defining an angle.

Activity 2.26 · Check a torque two ways

Allow about 16 minutes.

A force F=⟨−12,0,0⟩N acts at P=(0.30,0.40,0) metres. Use the same right-handed axes as in Figure 2.23.

  1. Find the torque about O=(0,0,0) and state its turning sense viewed from positive z.
  2. Identify the line of action and perpendicular lever arm. Use them to check the magnitude.
  3. Move the application point to P′=(0.70,0.40,0), keeping the same force. Predict and verify the torque about O.
  4. Return to P, but change the reference point to Q=(0,0.20,0). Find the new torque. Explain why parts 3 and 4 need not have the same effect.
Discussion of Activity 2.26

About O, the z-component is

τz=[0.30(0)−0.40(−12)]Nm=4.8Nm.

The torque points along positive z, corresponding to an anticlockwise turning effect. The line of action is y=0.40m, so ℓ=0.40m and the magnitude is (12N)(0.40m)=4.8Nm.

At P′ the line of action is unchanged. The changed x-coordinate multiplies Fy=0, leaving the same torque. This says that this force has the same torque about O when moved along its line of action; it does not claim that every other physical effect is unchanged.

About Q, the position vector to P is ⟨0.30,0.20,0⟩m, giving ⟨0,0,2.4⟩Nm. The perpendicular distance from the reference point to the line has halved. Part 3 kept that distance fixed, whereas part 4 changed it.

2.26 Same units do not mean the same quantity

Work and torque both involve a force and a length, so their units have the same dimensions. This does not make them interchangeable. Work uses a displacement and produces a scalar; torque uses a position relative to a stated point and produces a vector. We conventionally write work in joules and torque in newton metres to keep their meanings visible.

The distinction is not merely that one formula contains a dot and the other a cross. The length vectors have different roles. A force can act far from a reference point while its application point does not move. That can give a nonzero torque and zero work. Conversely, a force acting along a line through the reference point has zero torque about that point, but can do nonzero work if its application point moves along the force.

A real powered tool involves several interacting parts. Our next example deliberately uses a simpler model: one specified force, one application point and one reference point. It isolates the mathematical distinction without requiring a model of the motor, the fastener or the astronaut.

Example 2.28 · A position is not a journey

A constant force F=⟨0,15,0⟩N acts at a point initially at P=(0.20,0,0) metres. Find its torque about the origin. Compare its work in two trials: first the application point is held stationary; then, in a separate trial, it moves upwards by 0.040m while the specified force stays constant.

Solution

The initial torque uses r=⟨0.20,0,0⟩m:

τO=⟨0,0,3.0⟩Nm.

In the stationary trial, d=0, so W=0. This statement concerns the work by the specified force over that interval; the nonzero torque does not imply that the fixture lets the point move.

In the moving trial, the displacement is d=⟨0,0.040,0⟩m. Thus

W=(15N)(0.040m)=0.60J.

The application point's x-coordinate stays 0.20m, so this vertical force has the same line of action and the same torque about O throughout the trial. The work differs because the displacement differs. No contradiction arises: the two products answer different questions.

Key result 2.16 · Units are a check, not a definition

A proposed answer must have the correct units, but correct units alone do not establish the correct quantity. State what each input vector represents, the output type, and any reference point or direction. In these models, W=F·d and τO=r×F use different length vectors.

Activity 2.27 · Zero work with a changing torque

Allow about 16 minutes.

A force F=⟨0,10,0⟩N remains constant while its application point is moved horizontally from A=(0.30,0,0) to B=(0.50,0,0) metres. A separate mechanism controls the motion; you are asked only about the specified force.

  1. Calculate its work over the displacement from A to B.
  2. Calculate its torque about the origin at A and at B. State the directions and magnitudes.
  3. A student multiplies 10N by 0.50m and reports “5J of work”. Explain both the geometric and quantity errors.
  4. In another trial the force is ⟨10,0,0⟩N, with the same motion along the x-axis. Find its work and torque about the origin.
Discussion of Activity 2.27

The displacement is ⟨0.20,0,0⟩m, perpendicular to the first force. Its work is zero. The torque at A is ⟨0,0,3.0⟩Nm; at B it is ⟨0,0,5.0⟩Nm. Both point along positive z, with magnitudes 3.0 and 5.0Nm.

The student used the final distance from the origin instead of the displacement, and ignored the perpendicular angle relevant to work. The number 5.0 does occur as the final torque magnitude, but it describes a turning effect about O, not work. Giving it joule units does not repair the choice of quantity.

For the horizontal force in part 4, W=(10N)(0.20m)=2.0J. Its line of action passes through O, so its torque about O is zero at every point of this motion. Nonzero work and zero torque are perfectly compatible.

2.27 Make a defensible choice

In a mixed problem, choosing a method is part of the reasoning that should appear in the solution. Start by naming the requested quantity and writing a symbolic expression before substituting numbers. If an expression divides by a magnitude, check that the corresponding vector is nonzero. If it uses a cross product, state its order and the right-handed frame. If it describes a physical quantity, identify the model assumptions that make the expression applicable.

Next make a qualitative prediction. Should the answer point along a reference line, or perpendicular to a plane? Should a signed scalar be positive, negative or zero? Such a prediction is most useful before arithmetic gives you an answer to defend. After calculating, choose a check that tests something different from the steps you just performed.

Example 2.29 · Two tasks with the same data

A displacement is a=⟨2,1,2⟩m. A dimensionless vector b=⟨1,2,0⟩ specifies a reference line. Find the vector part of a perpendicular to this line. Then find a unit normal to the plane of a and b, with the orientation selected by a×b.

Solution

The first answer must complete a decomposition of a, so begin with a projection and subtract it. The dot products are a·b=4m and b·b=5. Consequently

p=⟨45,85,0⟩m,
q=a−p=⟨65,−35,2⟩m.

Check that p+q=a, and that the numerical dot product of q with b is 6/5−6/5=0. These checks test both the reconstruction and the perpendicularity.

The second task asks for a vector perpendicular to the entire plane. Calculate the ordered cross product:

c=a×b=⟨−4,2,3⟩m.

Its magnitude is 29m, so the requested dimensionless unit normal is

n^=129⟨−4,2,3⟩.

The dot products of ⟨−4,2,3⟩ with the numerical input components are −8+2+6=0 and −4+4=0. The squared magnitude of the unit normal is (16+4+9)/29=1.

Both q and n^ are perpendicular to b, but they are not interchangeable. The remainder q lies in the plane of a and b, because it is a minus a multiple of b. The normal is perpendicular to that plane. In three dimensions, “perpendicular to this line” does not by itself select one direction.

Likewise, a zero dot product is a good check on perpendicularity but cannot establish a vector's scale or orientation. An answer with a sign error can pass it. Correct units can expose a missing length factor, but cannot distinguish work from torque. Effective checking means knowing what a check can reveal and what it leaves undecided.

Activity 2.28 · Select, calculate and justify

Allow about 16 minutes. Write a method sentence before each calculation.

Let a=⟨1,−2,2⟩m and let b=⟨2,1,0⟩ be dimensionless.

  1. Find the angle between them. State why the formula is allowed.
  2. Find the projection of a onto the line of b and the perpendicular remainder. Predict these before calculating.
  3. Find the unit normal selected by a×b. Check its direction is perpendicular to both inputs and its magnitude is one.
  4. A classmate says the remainder in part 2 must equal the cross product in part 3 before normalisation, because both are perpendicular to b. Explain the error.
  5. Would replacing b by −b change the angle, the projected vector or the selected unit normal? Explain each answer.
Hint for Activity 2.28

Calculate the dot product first. A zero value can simplify the angle and projection questions, but it does not make the cross product zero.

Discussion of Activity 2.28

Both inputs are nonzero and a·b=(2−2)m=0, so their angle is 90∘. The vector projection is 0 and the perpendicular remainder is all of a. This follows geometrically before using the projection formula.

For the normal, preserve the input order:

a×b=⟨−2,4,5⟩m.

Its magnitude is 45m=35m. Therefore

n^=135⟨−2,4,5⟩.

The dot products of its numerator with the input components are −2−8+10=0 and −4+4=0. Dividing its squared magnitude 45 by (35)2=45 gives one. The remainder lies in the input plane; the cross product is perpendicular to it. Perpendicularity to a single line does not make those vectors equal.

Reversing b leaves the angle at 90∘ in this particular case, and the projected vector remains zero on the same line. It reverses the selected unit normal. For a general non-right angle, reversing one vector would change the angle to its supplement; do not generalise the unchanged angle from this special pair.

You can now connect a calculation to a question: a dot product measures a signed parallel contribution, a projection returns a component along a chosen line, and a cross product supplies an oriented perpendicular. The physical examples add another responsibility: identify the displacement used for work and the reference point used for torque. Units support those choices, but cannot make them for us.

Use the feedback time to revisit a choice that was initially uncertain and explain it without looking at the formula. Keep the reserved 30 minutes of written practice for that unit-ending set.
