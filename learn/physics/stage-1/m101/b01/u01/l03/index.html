Lesson 3
Magnitude and unit vectors

A displacement tells us how a position changes. Its components keep track of those changes along separate axes, but a question such as “How far apart are the starting and finishing points?” asks for a single length. We need to extract that length without confusing it with the route travelled.

In Lesson 2 we joined and scaled vectors. We could say that doubling an arrow doubles its length, but we did not yet have a method for calculating that length from components. We will build one from Pythagoras’ theorem, first in a plane and then in three dimensions. We will then turn the calculation around: if we remove a vector’s length, what information remains, and how can we use it to construct a new vector?

The answer leads to a useful separation between how much and which way. By the end of the lesson, you will be able to express a nonzero vector as a magnitude multiplied by a unit vector giving its direction. This prepares the ground for Lesson 4, where angles provide another way of specifying that direction.

Allow about four hours for the reading, worked examples, diagrams, activities and feedback. Keep squared paper and a pencil beside you; a calculator is useful for checking decimal approximations, but retain exact square roots in your working. There is no programming. Try each activity before opening its discussion, and use the checks in the solutions to locate errors rather than simply replace your answer.

1.15 From components to length

Consider a displacement d = ⟨−4, 3⟩ m. From its starting point, the finishing point is four metres to the left and three metres in the positive y-direction. The two signed components describe perpendicular changes. Neither component alone gives the length of the displacement arrow.

Draw the arrow from O and complete a right-angled triangle with sides parallel to the axes. The horizontal side has length 4 m and the vertical side has length 3 m. The minus sign in the horizontal component tells us which way to draw that side; it does not make the side’s length negative. The displacement arrow forms the hypotenuse—the side opposite the right angle.

Recall Pythagoras’ theorem: if the perpendicular sides of a right triangle have lengths a and b, and its hypotenuse has length h, then h2 = a2 + b2. Here this gives h2 = (4 m)2 + (3 m)2 = 25 m2, so h = 5 m. We take the nonnegative square root because we are finding a length.

The magnitude of a vector is the length represented by its arrow. We write it as |d|, read “the magnitude of d”. Thus d = ⟨−4, 3⟩ m, whereas |d| = 5 m. The first is a vector with direction as well as size; the second is a nonnegative scalar. The vertical bars also denote the absolute value of an ordinary number, as in |−4| = 4. The quantity inside them tells you which use is intended.

Key idea 1.13 · Magnitude in a plane

For perpendicular Cartesian axes with the same length scale,

if v = ⟨vx, vy⟩, then
|v| = √(vx2 + vy2).

The square root covers the whole sum. Use compatible units for the components; the magnitude has the same physical unit as the vector.

Why does this work for negative components? The triangle’s side lengths are |vx| and |vy|, and squaring either absolute value gives the same result as squaring the component itself. If one component is zero, the arrow lies along an axis and the formula reduces to the absolute value of the other component. If both are zero, its magnitude is zero.

1.15.1 Exact answers and useful approximations

Example 1.14 · A length that is not a whole number

A displacement has components ⟨−2, 5⟩ cm. Find its magnitude exactly, and then to two decimal places.

Solution

Square the signed numerical components, add, and take the square root:

|d| = √((−2)2 + 52) cm
= √(4 + 25) cm = √29 cm ≈ 5.39 cm.

The notation √29 is already an exact answer. The decimal 5.39 is an approximation, so it follows an ≈ sign. Since 25 < 29 < 36, the answer must lie between 5 and 6 cm. This gives a quick check before trusting a calculator.

Notice the parentheses in (−2)2: we square the entire component. The expression −22, without parentheses, conventionally means −(22) = −4. That would give the wrong calculation here.

Do not add the component lengths instead. For Figure 1.17, 4 + 3 = 7 m is the length of a route along the two perpendicular legs, not the diagonal. Nor may we take separate square roots and then add them: √(16 + 9) = 5, whereas √16 + √9 = 7. The location of the square-root sign matters.

Discussion of Activity 1.17

Historical perspective · optional reading

A diagonal written in clay

The Old Babylonian tablet YBC 7289 contains a square, its diagonals and a remarkably accurate numerical approximation to √2. It belongs to a mathematical tradition more than a millennium earlier than Pythagoras. The Institute for the Study of the Ancient World’s account explains how the diagonal calculation is recorded.

In our notation, a unit square’s diagonal has length √(12 + 12) = √2. The exact expression describes the length without rounding; a numerical approximation makes it convenient to calculate with. Those two roles remain distinct whether the numbers are written in clay or entered into a calculator. The modern numerical labels in the photograph are explanatory additions, not ancient markings. The group 1, 24, 51, 10 encodes 1 + 24/60 + 51/60² + 10/60³, approximately 1.414213. Compare it with √2 on a calculator if you are curious; interpreting this base-60 notation is optional.

Our right triangle has so far fitted in a plane. To measure an arrow that also changes height, we can use the same theorem twice.

1.16 Length in three dimensions

A robot moving across a floor can often be modelled with two position coordinates. A free-flying robot inside a spacecraft needs a third. Two locations may look close in a photograph while being separated in depth; a plane drawing cannot preserve every length and angle of the original scene.

We will use an idealised cabin with three mutually perpendicular axes. This is a mathematical setting inspired by the photograph, not a reconstruction of the photographed experiment. A displacement d = ⟨−4, 3, 12⟩ m changes all three coordinates. How can we find its length without trying to measure a perspective sketch?

First ignore the change in z and consider the displacement’s shadow on the xy-plane. This has components ⟨−4, 3, 0⟩ m and length r = √(16 + 9) m = 5 m. Here “shadow” means the mathematical projection found by setting the third component to zero; no light source is involved.

Now consider a second triangle. One leg is the 5 m segment in the xy-plane. The other is the 12 m change parallel to z. These legs are perpendicular because the z-axis is perpendicular to the whole xy-plane. The full displacement joins the start of the first leg to the end of the second, forming the hypotenuse. Its length is therefore √(52 + 122) m = 13 m.

The same reasoning gives the general result. If v = ⟨vx, vy, vz⟩, the squared length of the projection is r2 = vx2 + vy2. Applying Pythagoras in the second plane gives |v|2 = r2 + vz2. Substituting for r2 removes the intermediate quantity.

Key idea 1.14 · Magnitude in three dimensions

|v| = √(vx2 + vy2 + vz2).

The axes must be mutually perpendicular, and all components must use a consistent unit scale. Setting one component to zero recovers the two-dimensional formula.

Negative components cause no new difficulty. The two triangles use absolute lengths; squaring removes the component signs. If the horizontal projection is zero, the arrow lies along z and the answer is |vz|. The formula includes this case even though the first triangle has collapsed to a point.

Example 1.15 · Separate the endpoint calculation from the length

A tracked point moves from P = (1, −2, 4) cm to Q = (−1, 1, −2) cm. Find its displacement and the separation of the endpoints.

Solution

First find the signed changes; then calculate their combined length.

d = ⟨−1 − 1, 1 − (−2), −2 − 4⟩ = ⟨−2, 3, −6⟩ cm. Thus |d| = √(4 + 9 + 36) cm = √49 cm = 7 cm.

As a check on the subtraction, adding d to P gives (−1, 1, −2) cm, which is Q. As a separate length check, 7 cm is at least as large as every component’s absolute value, including the 6 cm change in z. A claimed length of 5 cm would be impossible even before we repeated the arithmetic.

The magnitudes of the position vectors of P and Q would describe distances from O. Subtracting those distances would not generally give the separation of P and Q. The displacement must be formed first.

1.17 What scaling does to magnitude

Lesson 2 showed geometrically that a positive scalar stretches or shrinks a vector without reversing it, while a negative scalar also reverses its direction. The magnitude formula now lets us verify that statement algebraically. This is a useful habit: when a new method becomes available, use it to check a result you already understand in another way.

Take v = ⟨−4, 3⟩, which has magnitude 5. Multiplying by −2 gives −2v = ⟨8, −6⟩, with magnitude √(64 + 36) = 10. The length has doubled, not become −10. The minus sign is expressed by the reversed direction of the vector.

For a general scalar k, each component of kv contains the same factor. Squaring introduces k2 in every term, so in a plane,

|kv|2 = (kvx)2 + (kvy)2
= k2(vx2 + vy2) = k2|v|2.

Taking a nonnegative square root gives |k| |v|. The absolute value appears because √(k2) = |k|, not necessarily k. The third component in a three-dimensional vector contributes another term with the same factor, so the reasoning is unchanged.

Key idea 1.15 · A multiplier’s sign affects direction, not the sign of length

|kv| = |k| |v|.

In particular, |−v| = |v|. Reversing a vector preserves its magnitude. Multiplying by zero gives magnitude zero.

1.17.1 Why magnitudes do not add like vectors

A magnitude gives one number where a vector gives several components. Information has been lost, so we should be cautious about trying to recover a vector calculation from magnitudes alone. For example, two nonzero displacements of equal magnitude can cancel if they point in opposite directions. Their magnitudes do not cancel: both are positive lengths.

Example 1.16 · A sum first, or magnitudes first?

Let a = ⟨3, 0⟩ m and b = ⟨0, 4⟩ m. Compare |a + b| with |a| + |b|.

Solution

The sum is a + b = ⟨3, 4⟩ m, so |a + b| = 5 m. Taking the magnitudes separately gives |a| + |b| = 3 m + 4 m = 7 m.

The first expression measures the straight-line separation after both stages. The second measures the distance along those two straight stages. Their difference is geometric, not a rounding error. If the second stage instead pointed along the same positive axis as the first, the two lengths would add to the length of the resultant.

The same warning applies to subtraction. To calculate |a − b|, form the difference vector and then find its magnitude. The quantity |a| − |b| answers a different question: how much larger one magnitude is than the other. It can even be negative. A vector magnitude cannot.

  2. Find a vector of magnitude 5 m that is neither v nor −v. Explain why equality of magnitudes is insufficient to prove vector equality.

Discussion of Activity 1.19

(a) The magnitudes are 2.5 m, 15 m and 0 m. For example, −3v = ⟨12, −9⟩ m, giving √(144 + 81) = 15 m. (b) One answer is ⟨5, 0⟩ m. It has the required length but points along a different direction. Many other answers are possible.

(c) v − w = ⟨−8, 0⟩ m has magnitude 8 m. But |v| − |w| = 5 m − 5 m = 0 m. Equal distances from a common origin do not mean coincident endpoints. (d) We need 5|k| = 12, so k = 12/5 or −12/5. The positive value preserves direction; the negative one reverses it.

Magnitude alone cannot specify a direction. We now look for a vector that retains direction while replacing the original magnitude by a standard value.

1.18 Keeping direction, removing scale

Imagine describing a straight route to someone who needs to take a shorter step along it. The displacement ⟨4, 3⟩ m fixes both a direction and a length of 5 m. For a one-metre step in that direction, every component must be one fifth as large. For any other step length, it would be helpful to have a direction description independent of metres.

Divide the displacement by its magnitude, including the units:

u = d/|d| = (⟨4, 3⟩ m)/(5 m)
= ⟨4/5, 3/5⟩.

The metres cancel. The resulting vector is dimensionless and has magnitude √((4/5)2 + (3/5)2) = √(16/25 + 9/25) = 1. It has the same direction as d because both components were divided by the same positive quantity.

A vector with magnitude 1 is called a unit vector. Here “unit” means magnitude one, not a physical measurement unit such as a metre. In this lesson our direction unit vectors are dimensionless. Multiplying u by 1 m produces a displacement of length one metre; multiplying it by 5 m reconstructs d.

1.18.1 Why the construction works

Let v be any nonzero dimensionless vector. Its magnitude is positive, so multiplying by 1/|v| preserves direction. From the scaling rule, the magnitude of the result is (1/|v|)|v| = 1. Both requirements—direction and length—follow from using the same positive multiplier on the whole vector. For a physical vector, the matching units cancel in the ratio, just as they did above.

Key idea 1.16 · Normalising a nonzero vector

u = v/|v|,   provided v ≠ 0.

Divide every component by the magnitude of the entire vector. The result has magnitude 1 and the same direction as v. This process is called normalisation. The unit vector in the opposite direction is −u.

Example 1.17 · A direction with negative components

Find a unit vector in the direction of v = ⟨−2, 1, 2⟩, and another in the opposite direction.

Solution

The magnitude is √(4 + 1 + 4) = 3. Dividing all three components by 3 gives u = ⟨−2/3, 1/3, 2/3⟩. Its squared magnitude is 4/9 + 1/9 + 4/9 = 1, so its magnitude is 1.

The multiplier 1/3 is positive, confirming the direction. For the opposite direction, reverse every component: −u = ⟨2/3, −1/3, −2/3⟩. This also has magnitude 1. A negative component is perfectly compatible with being a unit vector.

Normalisation is not always a shrinking operation. For example, ⟨0.3, 0.4⟩ has magnitude 0.5. Dividing by 0.5 doubles its components, giving ⟨0.6, 0.8⟩ with magnitude 1. For dimensionless vectors, a vector longer than one is shortened, a nonzero vector shorter than one is lengthened, and a unit vector is unchanged.

A unit vector need not have a component equal to 1. In Example 1.17 none does. Nor do its components usually add to 1. What must equal 1 is the sum of their squares. The components of ⟨1, 1⟩ add to 2 and its magnitude is √2; replacing each component of ⟨4, 3⟩ by 1 would change both length and direction.

Example 1.18 · Keep the square root until the check is complete

Normalise a = ⟨1, −3⟩.

Solution

Since |a| = √10, the exact unit vector is u = ⟨1/√10, −3/√10⟩. Check its squared magnitude before rounding: 1/10 + 9/10 = 1.

For a numerical sketch we could use u ≈ ⟨0.316, −0.949⟩. The squared magnitude of these rounded components is 0.3162 + 0.9492 = 1.000457, slightly above 1. That small difference comes from rounding, not from a failure of the exact method. Keep the square-root expression if the unit vector will be used in a later calculation.

1.18.2 The zero vector is a genuine exception

The rule cannot be applied to 0, because its magnitude is zero. Dividing its components by zero is undefined. There is also a geometric reason: an arrow with coincident tail and head gives no direction to preserve. Multiplying it by any finite scalar still gives 0, never a vector of magnitude one.

This differs from a vector that merely has a zero component. The vector ⟨0, −6⟩ has magnitude 6 and a definite direction down the y-axis; its unit vector is ⟨0, −1⟩. Check whether the whole vector is zero, not whether one entry happens to be zero.

Discussion of Activity 1.20

(a) The original magnitudes are 10, 6 and 3. The unit vectors are ⟨−4/5, 3/5⟩, ⟨0, −1⟩ and ⟨2/3, −1/3, 2/3⟩. Their squared magnitudes are 16/25 + 9/25 = 1, 0 + 1 = 1, and 4/9 + 1/9 + 4/9 = 1. (b) The squared magnitudes are ½, 1 and 1. Only the second and third are unit vectors.

(c) The common positive factor 1/7 preserves direction, but the magnitude is 5/7, not 1. The denominator 7 adds the original components; the required denominator is their vector’s magnitude, 5. (e) Normalisation is undefined: the vector has magnitude zero and no specified direction. Do not return the zero vector as a unit vector.

1.19 The coordinate unit vectors

There are infinitely many possible unit-vector directions. Three are especially convenient when we use Cartesian coordinates: the positive directions of the coordinate axes themselves. Giving these vectors short names provides another way to write a component description.

In a plane, define i = ⟨1, 0⟩ and j = ⟨0, 1⟩. Each has magnitude 1. The vector i points along positive x, and j along positive y. They are the standard coordinate unit vectors for the chosen axes. The bold letters are names for vectors; they are not extra coordinates or physical units.

For example, ⟨−3, 2⟩ = ⟨−3, 0⟩ + ⟨0, 2⟩ = −3i + 2j. This is the same component addition used in Lesson 2, with names attached to the axis directions. The signed coefficient −3 specifies a contribution along negative x; it does not turn i itself into a negative-axis vector. Its definition remains fixed.

Key idea 1.17 · Two notations for the same vector

⟨vx, vy⟩ = vxi + vyj.

In three dimensions, i = ⟨1, 0, 0⟩, j = ⟨0, 1, 0⟩ and k = ⟨0, 0, 1⟩, so

⟨vx, vy, vz⟩ = vxi + vyj + vzk.

These unit vectors are dimensionless. For a physical vector, its coefficients carry the physical units.

Thus a displacement ⟨−3, 2⟩ m can be written (−3 m)i + (2 m)j, or (−3i + 2j) m. The parentheses in the latter form make clear that metres apply to the whole combination. Do not write the magnitude as −3 + 2: the two terms belong to different perpendicular directions.

In the previous lesson, an ordinary italic k was used for a scalar multiplier. Here bold k is the standard vector along z. Typography distinguishes the roles. In your handwritten work, put an arrow over a vector letter if bold writing is inconvenient.

Example 1.19 · Read the notation, then normalise

A displacement is d = (2i − j + 2k) cm. Find its magnitude and its direction unit vector.

Solution

The coefficient of j is −1, so the component form is ⟨2, −1, 2⟩ cm. Its magnitude is √(4 + 1 + 4) cm = 3 cm. Dividing by 3 cm gives u = (2/3)i − (1/3)j + (2/3)k.

The unit vector carries no centimetres. Multiplying it by 3 cm gives back every term of d. The coordinate notation has not introduced a new operation; it has made the separate directional contributions visible.

Activity 1.21 · Translate between representations

  1. Write ⟨−2, 5, 0⟩ m using coordinate unit vectors. Write (4j − 3k) cm as a component triple.
  2. Find the magnitude and direction unit vector of (4j − 3k) cm.
  3. Calculate i + j. Is the sum a unit vector? Find a unit vector in its direction.

Discussion of Activity 1.21

(a) (−2i + 5j) m, with zero k-term; and ⟨0, 4, −3⟩ cm. (b) The magnitude is 5 cm and the unit vector is (4/5)j − (3/5)k. (c) The sum is ⟨1, 1⟩, with magnitude √2. Its unit vector is ⟨1/√2, 1/√2⟩, or (i + j)/√2. Adding two unit vectors does not generally produce another unit vector.

(d) The vector expression is correct, but 2 and −1 multiply different directions. Their scalar sum is not the vector’s length. Its magnitude is √(4 + 1) = √5.

1.20 Building a vector of a chosen length

Normalisation removes scale. Multiplication by a chosen magnitude puts it back. If u is a direction unit vector and L is a positive length, then d = Lu is a displacement of magnitude L in that direction. This gives a practical procedure when a problem specifies “this far, in that direction”.

Suppose the direction is given by ⟨−4, 3⟩, but the required displacement has length 2 m. Multiplying that vector directly by 2 m would give ⟨−8, 6⟩ m, whose length is 10 m. The direction vector’s own magnitude, 5, would still be present. First replace it by ⟨−4/5, 3/5⟩; then multiply by 2 m to obtain ⟨−8/5, 6/5⟩ m.

Key idea 1.18 · Normalise, then apply the required magnitude

For nonzero v, a vector of magnitude L > 0 in its direction is

d = L(v/|v|).

Use the negative of this vector for the opposite direction. More generally, every nonzero vector can be written as its magnitude multiplied by its direction unit vector.

The factor L carries the desired physical unit if we are constructing a physical quantity. The ratio v/|v| is dimensionless, whether the starting direction was supplied as a dimensionless triple or extracted from a physical displacement. If L = 0, the result is the zero vector; it no longer has the chosen direction as an intrinsic property.

Example 1.20 · Choose a step along a line

A tracked point is at P = (1, −2, 0) m. It should move 6 m in the direction of the dimensionless vector v = ⟨−2, 1, 2⟩. Find its displacement and new position.

Solution

From |v| = 3, its direction unit vector is u = ⟨−2/3, 1/3, 2/3⟩. The required displacement is d = (6 m)u = ⟨−4, 2, 4⟩ m.

Add the displacement to P: the new position is (1 − 4, −2 + 2, 0 + 4) = (−3, 0, 4) m. The magnitude check gives √(16 + 4 + 16) = 6 m. The direction check is separate: d is a positive multiple, 2 m, of the given dimensionless vector. Both requirements hold.

If the instruction instead said “6 m in the opposite direction”, the displacement would be ⟨4, −2, −4⟩ m and the endpoint (5, −4, −4) m. The magnitude would still be 6 m.

1.20.1 Direction information must be sufficient

An instruction to “move 6 m” leaves infinitely many possible endpoints. An instruction to move 6 m parallel to a line usually leaves two, one in each direction along it. An arrow or a direction unit vector selects one of those directions. This is why “parallel to” and “in the same direction as” are not interchangeable in a reconstruction question.

Even when a proposed direction vector has been supplied, check whether it has magnitude 1 before multiplying by the target length. For instance, ⟨0.6, 0.8⟩ is a unit vector because 0.36 + 0.64 = 1, but ⟨0.6, 0.6⟩ is not: the sum of squares is 0.72. Similar-looking components do not guarantee the same length.

Activity 1.22 · Reconstruct and verify

  1. Find a displacement of magnitude 10 cm in the direction of ⟨3, −4⟩. Check its length and direction separately.
  2. Find a displacement of magnitude 2 m in the direction opposite to ⟨1, −1, 1⟩. Give exact components and verify the sum of their squares.
  3. A point starts at (−2, 1) m and moves 2.5 m in the direction of ⟨−4, 3⟩. Find the endpoint.
  4. A proposed displacement is (8 m)⟨0.6, 0.6⟩. Does it have magnitude 8 m? Replace it with a displacement of magnitude 8 m in the same direction.
Hint for Activity 1.22

Check the magnitude of the supplied direction vector before using it. In (d), equal positive components select a diagonal direction, but do not make the vector a unit vector.

Discussion of Activity 1.22

(a) The given vector has magnitude 5, so the displacement is (10 cm)⟨3/5, −4/5⟩ = ⟨6, −8⟩ cm. Its magnitude is 10 cm and the multiplier 2 cm is positive. (b) The direction vector has magnitude √3. The answer is ⟨−2/√3, 2/√3, −2/√3⟩ m. Its squared magnitude is (4/3 + 4/3 + 4/3) m2 = 4 m2, giving 2 m; the negative multiplier reverses direction.

(c) The direction unit vector is ⟨−4/5, 3/5⟩. Multiplying by 2.5 m gives ⟨−2, 1.5⟩ m. Adding to the start gives (−4, 2.5) m. (d) The proposed magnitude is 8√0.72 m, approximately 6.79 m. Normalising the equal positive components gives ⟨1/√2, 1/√2⟩. The required replacement is ⟨8/√2, 8/√2⟩ m, whose squared magnitude is 64 m2.

1.21 One displacement, two descriptions

We can now move between two descriptions of the same nonzero vector. A component description tells us the signed change along each axis. A magnitude and a direction unit vector tell us how much change occurs and which way it points. Neither description has priority in every problem: component form makes addition convenient, while magnitude and unit direction make a prescribed-length step convenient.

There is a useful order to follow when several tasks appear together. First determine the displacement from the given positions or vector operations. Then calculate its magnitude. Only then normalise it or construct a new vector of a required length. This keeps position, displacement, magnitude and unit direction from becoming mixed in the same line of working.

Activity 1.23 · Approach a target, then step back

In an idealised three-dimensional test space, a tracked point starts at P = (2, −1, 1) m. A target is at Q = (−2, 1, 5) m. Use only the results developed in this lesson and the two preceding lessons.

  1. Find the displacement from P to Q, its magnitude, and its direction unit vector.
  2. Starting at P, move 1.5 m directly towards Q. Find the new point R. Explain why this reaches one quarter of the way along the original displacement.
  3. Find the remaining displacement from R to Q and its magnitude. Check that the completed and remaining displacements add to P→Q.
  4. From R, make a displacement of 0.75 m directly away from Q. Find the new position S and its distance from Q.
  5. Suppose the starting point had already been Q. Which part of the instruction “move towards Q” would then be undefined?
Hint for Activity 1.23

Keep the same direction unit vector while moving along the line, and use a negative sign for the step away from Q. Form the remaining displacement from its endpoints to check the result.

Discussion of Activity 1.23

(a) P→Q = ⟨−4, 2, 4⟩ m, with magnitude √36 = 6 m and unit direction u = ⟨−2/3, 1/3, 2/3⟩. (b) The 1.5 m step is ⟨−1, 0.5, 1⟩ m, giving R = (1, −0.5, 2) m. The fraction is 1.5/6 = ¼, and each displacement component is one quarter of the original.

(c) R→Q = ⟨−3, 1.5, 3⟩ m, with magnitude √(9 + 2.25 + 9) = 4.5 m. Adding it to ⟨−1, 0.5, 1⟩ m gives ⟨−4, 2, 4⟩ m. Here the lengths also add, 1.5 + 4.5 = 6 m, because these consecutive stages point in the same direction along one line.

(d) The step is −(0.75 m)u = ⟨0.5, −0.25, −0.5⟩ m. Thus S = (1.5, −0.75, 1.5) m. From S to Q the displacement is ⟨−3.5, 1.75, 3.5⟩ m, whose magnitude is 5.25 m. This also agrees with 4.5 + 0.75 m: the retreat increased the separation. (e) At Q the displacement towards the target would be 0. Its magnitude is well defined, but there is no nonzero arrow to normalise and hence no direction selected by that instruction.

Return to the opening question. The magnitude supplies the straight-line length represented by a displacement, while normalisation supplies a direction independent of its physical scale. The exceptional zero case is part of the method: it tells us when the question “which way?” has not been answered by the data.

Before moving on, check that you can explain why component signs disappear from a magnitude calculation but remain in a unit vector. You should also be able to check a proposed unit vector by summing the squares of its components, and reconstruct a physical displacement with the correct units.

In Lesson 4 we will specify a plane direction using an angle. Trigonometry will connect that angle to the components of its unit vector, so that “length and angle” can be translated into the component language already established here. The full-unit exercise set will then mix these representations and require you to choose a method.
