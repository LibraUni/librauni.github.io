Lesson 2
Adding and scaling vectors

A route can be described one stage at a time: move to the first marker, then to the next, then return to the equipment left behind. Each stage has its own displacement. But if we want to know where the route finishes, we need a way of combining those directed changes.

In Lesson 1, we found a displacement by comparing two positions. Here we start with the changes themselves. We will join them, reverse them and make them larger or smaller. Diagrams will show what each operation means; calculations with components will let us carry it out precisely.

Allow about four hours, including drawing, activities and checking your work. You will need paper, preferably squared, and a pencil. There is no programming. The interactive later in the lesson can also be explored using the accompanying table and sketches. Work through the examples with your own diagrams: recognising an arrow construction is easier than reproducing it, and both are useful.

1.8 The same vector in a different place

Imagine a small survey robot moving across a level test area. Its instructions refer to fixed directions marked on the floor: positive x to the right and positive y towards the far wall. An instruction to change position by ⟨3, 1⟩ m means three metres in the positive x-direction and one metre in the positive y-direction overall. It need not begin at the origin.

If the robot starts at A = (−2, −1) m, that instruction takes it to B = (1, 0) m. Starting instead at C = (1, 2) m would take it to D = (4, 3) m. The endpoints differ, but the changes agree. In each case the final horizontal coordinate is three greater and the final vertical coordinate is one greater than the starting coordinate.

This gives us a useful freedom when drawing vectors. An arrow represents a directed change, rather than a particular patch of paper. We may move a copy of it to a convenient place, provided its length and direction are preserved. Vectors treated in this way are sometimes called free vectors.

There is an important connection with the previous lesson. A position vector is a vector with a specified role: it locates a point from the chosen origin. Moving its arrow elsewhere preserves the directed change, but the moved arrow is no longer drawn as that point’s position vector. We have changed the representation’s location, not the vector’s components.

Key idea 1.7 · Equality of vectors

In the same coordinate system and units, two vectors are equal when their corresponding components are equal. On an equally scaled diagram, nonzero equal vectors have the same length and direction; their arrows need not start at the same point. The zero vector has zero components and no unique direction.

Example 1.7 · Comparing changes rather than endpoints

Let P = (−3, 2), Q = (1, 1), R = (0, −2) and S = (4, −3), with coordinates in metres. Do P→Q and R→S represent the same vector?

Solution

Use final minus initial for each arrow. The first gives ⟨1 − (−3), 1 − 2⟩ = ⟨4, −1⟩ m. The second gives ⟨4 − 0, −3 − (−2)⟩ = ⟨4, −1⟩ m. The vectors are equal even though none of the corresponding endpoints coincide.

Both changes are four metres right and one metre towards negative y. Looking only at Q and S would compare final positions instead of the changes we were asked about.

Activity 1.8 · Moving an arrow

Draw axes with a one-metre scale. Let A = (−2, 1), B = (1, 3) and C = (2, −2).

  1. Draw A→B and calculate its components.
  2. Draw an equal vector with its tail at C. Find its head D, and check by subtracting C from D.
  3. Draw B→A. Explain why this is not equal to A→B, even though the two arrows have the same length.
Discussion of Activity 1.8

(a) The changes are ⟨1 − (−2), 3 − 1⟩ = ⟨3, 2⟩ m. (b) Add these changes to C: D = (2 + 3, −2 + 2) = (5, 0) m. Checking D minus C recovers ⟨3, 2⟩ m. The two arrows should be parallel and equally long. (c) B→A has components ⟨−3, −2⟩ m. Its direction is reversed. Equal length alone is insufficient to establish equality.

The freedom to move a copy of an arrow is what makes the next construction possible. We can arrange two changes in the order in which they are to happen, without altering either change.

1.9 Joining directed changes

Suppose our robot first makes the displacement a = ⟨3, 1⟩ m and then b = ⟨−1, 2⟩ m. Starting at O, the first instruction finishes at (3, 1). The second instruction starts there, moves one metre left and two metres farther along positive y, and finishes at (2, 3).

Draw the first arrow. Then place a copy of the second arrow with its tail at the head of the first. An arrow from the original starting point to the final finishing point describes the combined change. We call this the sum a + b, also called the resultant. Here its components are ⟨2, 3⟩ m.

This construction is called head-to-tail addition, or the triangle law. The tail of the second arrow must meet the head of the first. Putting two tails together and joining the heads produces a different arrow, which we will examine when discussing subtraction.

1.9.1 Does the order matter?

Try the same two instructions in the opposite order. From O, b finishes at (−1, 2). Following a from there finishes at (2, 3), exactly as before. The intermediate stopping point differs, but the final change agrees.

We can put both routes on one diagram. Copies of a form two opposite sides of a parallelogram, and copies of b form the other two. The diagonal from the shared starting point is the resultant. This is the parallelogram construction for addition. It is especially useful when two arrows have already been drawn from a common tail.

Key idea 1.8 · The sum describes the combined change

Place arrows head to tail and join the first tail to the last head. Reordering two vector displacements does not change their sum: a + b = b + a. This property is called commutativity.

There is a qualification when applying this to a real route. The two paths might pass through different obstacles. Also, our instructions specify changes along fixed axes; they are not commands such as “turn left, then drive forward”, whose meaning depends on the robot’s current orientation. Equal vector sums do not imply that two sequences of physical actions are interchangeable in every respect.

Example 1.8 · A third stage

After making a and b above, the robot makes c = ⟨−2, −3⟩ m. Find the resultant of all three stages.

Solution

The first two stages take the robot from O to (2, 3). Starting there, c takes it to (2 − 2, 3 − 3) = (0, 0). The final head meets the original tail, so the resultant is 0.

The robot has travelled a route; it has not remained still. A zero resultant tells us that the route closes. It says nothing about how long the journey took or how much distance was covered.

Discussion of Activity 1.9

(a) The intermediate point is (2, 3) m; the final point is (2 + 3, 3 − 1) = (5, 2) m. The resultant is ⟨5, 2⟩ m. (b) The reversed order passes through (3, −1) m and ends at (3 + 2, −1 + 3) = (5, 2) m. The intermediate points and path segments differ, while the diagonal agrees. (c) The return must change (5, 2) to (0, 0), so it is ⟨−5, −2⟩ m. Its tail belongs at (5, 2), not at O.

A wider view · optional reading

The route still matters

A vector sum is an economical description: it retains the overall change and discards the intermediate route. That is useful for locating an endpoint, but insufficient for deciding whether a route crosses safe ground. The photograph is a reminder that a mathematical description answers a particular question. Our level test-area diagrams are idealised examples, not a reconstruction of this Martian drive.

We have found sums by following arrows. The coordinates of each stopping point suggest a shorter calculation: add all the horizontal changes together, and do the same for the vertical changes.

1.10 Adding components

Write a = ⟨ax, ay⟩ and b = ⟨bx, by⟩. The subscript identifies the direction, as it did for position vectors in Lesson 1. Suppose the starting horizontal coordinate is x0. After the first change it is x0 + ax; after the second it is x0 + ax + bx. Subtracting the starting coordinate leaves the total horizontal change ax + bx.

The vertical calculation is identical in form. Notice that the starting position has dropped out. That is why the addition rule works wherever the first arrow is drawn.

Key idea 1.9 · Add corresponding components

a + b = ⟨ax + bx, ay + by⟩.

For three-dimensional vectors, include az + bz as the third component. The components must refer to the same axes, and physical quantities must have compatible units.

The last condition prevents a common kind of meaningless calculation. A displacement in metres cannot be added to a time in seconds. Even two displacements must be expressed consistently: convert centimetres and metres before adding their numerical components. Nor can a two-component arrow in a plane simply be paired entry by entry with a three-component vector. The representation and the quantity being described must both be clear.

As an endpoint check, start at (4, 1, −2) m. The successive positions are (2, 4, −1), (7, 3, −1) and (6, 1, 3) m. Final minus initial gives ⟨6 − 4, 1 − 1, 3 − (−2)⟩ = ⟨2, 0, 5⟩ m.

1.10.1 Grouping a longer sum

You could first combine p and q, obtaining ⟨3, 2, 1⟩ m, then add r. Alternatively, combine q and r to get ⟨4, −3, 4⟩ m, then add p. Both give ⟨2, 0, 5⟩ m. Grouping changes the bookkeeping, not the result.

In symbols, (p + q) + r = p + (q + r). This is the associative property. It follows because ordinary addition has that property in every component. Together with commutativity, it lets us rearrange a long sum to combine convenient pairs first. It does not let us forget minus signs when some terms are negative vectors.

Activity 1.10 · Choosing a useful order

  1. Calculate ⟨−4, 2⟩ + ⟨1, −5⟩ + ⟨3, 1⟩. Show the horizontal and vertical sums separately and sketch a head-to-tail check.
  2. Find ⟨2, −1, 3⟩ + ⟨−5, 4, −2⟩ + ⟨3, −3, 0⟩. Identify any cancellations before calculating.
  3. A mechanism makes ⟨150, −50⟩ cm followed by ⟨−1, 2⟩ m. Express the resultant in metres. Explain why ⟨149, −48⟩ m is not an acceptable answer.
  4. For part (a), repeat the endpoint check starting at (2, 1), using the same coordinate scale. Which final position do you obtain, and how does it differ from the resultant vector?
Discussion of Activity 1.10

(a) The sums are −4 + 1 + 3 = 0 and 2 − 5 + 1 = −2, giving ⟨0, −2⟩. (b) The x- and y-components cancel: 2 − 5 + 3 = 0 and −1 + 4 − 3 = 0. The remaining component is 3 − 2 + 0 = 1, so the result is ⟨0, 0, 1⟩. A vector can have two zero components without being the zero vector.

(c) First convert: ⟨150, −50⟩ cm = ⟨1.5, −0.5⟩ m. Addition gives ⟨0.5, 1.5⟩ m. The proposed answer mixes numerical readings from different unit scales. (d) The positions are (−2, 3), (−1, −2), then (2, −1). The final point is (2, −1); the change from the starting point is ⟨0, −2⟩. The starting position must be included when locating the endpoint.

1.11 Opposites and differences

Suppose you have followed an arrow and want to undo that change. The return arrow must have the same length and point the other way. Every rightward change becomes leftward; every upward change becomes downward. The vector that reverses b is written −b, and is called its negative or opposite.

For example, if b = ⟨−1, 2⟩ m, then −b = ⟨1, −2⟩ m. These arrows cancel when placed head to tail: b + (−b) = 0. Negating a vector changes the sign of every component, including components that are already negative. A zero component remains zero. The zero vector is its own negative.

Key idea 1.10 · Subtract by adding the opposite

a − b = a + (−b) = ⟨ax − bx, ay − by⟩.

The same rule applies to the third component in three dimensions. Keep the order: components of a minus the corresponding components of b.

There are two helpful ways to draw a difference. One is to reverse b, attach it to the head of a, then draw the resultant. The other begins with a and b at a common tail. In that picture, a − b runs from the head of b to the head of a. Why this direction? Travelling along b and then along the difference must bring you to the same place as travelling along a alone.

Example 1.10 · Which way does the difference point?

For a = ⟨3, 1⟩ m and b = ⟨−1, 2⟩ m, find both differences.

Solution

a − b = ⟨3 − (−1), 1 − 2⟩ = ⟨4, −1⟩ m. From the head of b, at (−1, 2), we move four units right and one down to reach (3, 1), the head of a.

Reversing the subtraction gives b − a = ⟨−1 − 3, 2 − 1⟩ = ⟨−4, 1⟩ m. It joins the same points in the opposite direction. Thus b − a = −(a − b). Subtraction is not commutative.

This is the displacement rule from Lesson 1 in a more general form. If rP and rQ are position vectors, then the displacement from P to Q is rQ − rP. The two position arrows share O as a tail, and the difference arrow runs from P to Q. Nothing new has happened to the points: we now have a name and an algebraic rule for the arrow construction.

Hint for Activity 1.11

Write a pair of parentheses around each component being subtracted. For (d), consider what happens when the two original vectors agree.

Discussion of Activity 1.11

(a) −v = ⟨−4, 1⟩; u − v = ⟨−6, 4⟩; v − u = ⟨6, −4⟩. The difference arrows run respectively from (4, −1) to (−2, 3), and back again. (b) ⟨−2 − (−4), 3 − 1⟩ = ⟨2, 2⟩. Reversing the opposite of v restores v, so subtracting −v is adding v.

(c) ⟨1 − (−2), −3 − 1, 2 − (−4)⟩ = ⟨3, −4, 6⟩. Adding ⟨−2, 1, −4⟩ returns ⟨1, −3, 2⟩. (d) If u = v, both differences are 0. Conversely, equality of the opposite differences would make every component of either difference equal its own negative, hence zero; the original vectors must agree.

1.12 Scaling a vector

Two identical displacements in succession give a + a, which we abbreviate to 2a. Three give 3a. In each case every component is multiplied by the same number. This suggests a way to extend multiplication beyond whole numbers: half the change has half of each component, and one and a half times the change has one and a half times each component.

The multiplier is a scalar: a number, rather than another vector. For a positive multiplier, the arrow keeps its direction. A negative multiplier also reverses it. Multiplying by zero produces the zero vector, which has no direction. In this lesson the multipliers have no units, so a displacement measured in metres remains a displacement measured in metres.

Key idea 1.11 · Multiply every component by the scalar

ka = ⟨kax, kay⟩.

Include kaz in three dimensions. For a nonzero vector and nonzero k, the length is multiplied by |k|. The direction stays the same when k is positive and reverses when k is negative.

The bars in |k| mean the absolute value of the number: for example, |−2| = 2. A negative multiplier does not produce a negative length. It gives an arrow pointing the other way, with an ordinary nonnegative length. We will calculate vector lengths systematically in Lesson 3; here the relative lengths are enough.

Example 1.11 · A negative fractional multiplier

Let u = ⟨−4, 2⟩ m. Find −1.5u and ½u, and describe each geometrically.

Solution

−1.5u = ⟨(−1.5)(−4), (−1.5)(2)⟩ = ⟨6, −3⟩ m. The arrow is one and a half times as long as u, in the opposite direction. The original arrow points left and up; this one points right and down.

½u = ⟨−2, 1⟩ m. This arrow has half the length of u and the same direction. In both calculations the multiplier applies to every component.

1.12.1 Scaling is different from moving an arrow

When we moved an arrow to a new starting point in Section 1.8, its components did not change. Scaling usually changes them. If the tail stays at O, doubling ⟨2, 1⟩ moves its head from (2, 1) to (4, 2). Translating the original arrow so that its tail is (2, 1) instead gives an arrow from (2, 1) to (4, 2), still representing ⟨2, 1⟩. The endpoints happen to agree, but the arrows represent different changes. Always inspect both the tail and the head.

Activity 1.12 · Recognising a scalar multiple

  1. Calculate −2⟨3, −1⟩, ¼⟨−8, 4⟩ and −½⟨2, −6, 4⟩. Describe the direction and relative length of each result compared with its original vector.
  2. Is ⟨6, −3⟩ a scalar multiple of ⟨−4, 2⟩? Is ⟨6, 3⟩? Give a reason that checks both components.
  3. Find k if k⟨0, 3⟩ = ⟨0, −6⟩. Why does the first component alone not determine k?
  4. Explain the difference between translating an arrow and multiplying it by zero.
Discussion of Activity 1.12

(a) The results are ⟨−6, 2⟩, ⟨−2, 1⟩ and ⟨−1, 3, −2⟩. Their lengths are respectively twice, one quarter and one half the originals. The first and third reverse direction; the second keeps it. (b) The first is −1.5 times the original, as both components confirm. For ⟨6, 3⟩ the first component would require −1.5, but that gives a second component of −3, not 3. There is no single multiplier.

(c) The second component gives 3k = −6, hence k = −2. The first says only 0k = 0, true for every k. (d) Translation keeps the change represented by the arrow. Multiplication by zero removes every component of that change, giving 0.

1.13 Combining operations

A route need not use each displacement exactly once. It might repeat one stage, reverse another, and shorten a third. Expressions such as 2a − b describe these combinations compactly. The numbers multiplying the vectors are called coefficients. Work out each scalar multiple before combining the components, just as you do multiplication before addition in ordinary arithmetic.

Example 1.12 · Two routes to the same calculation

For a = ⟨2, −1⟩ and b = ⟨−1, 3⟩, calculate 2a − b.

Solution

First 2a = ⟨4, −2⟩. Then 2a − b = ⟨4 − (−1), −2 − 3⟩ = ⟨5, −5⟩.

An independent route is to add a, a and −b: ⟨2, −1⟩ + ⟨2, −1⟩ + ⟨1, −3⟩ = ⟨5, −5⟩. On a diagram beginning at O, the successive endpoints are (2, −1), (4, −2) and (5, −5). This checks both the factor of two and the reversal of b.

1.13.1 Brackets and like terms

Compare 2(a + b) with 2a + 2b, using the same vectors. In the first expression the sum is ⟨1, 2⟩, so doubling it gives ⟨2, 4⟩. In the second, adding ⟨4, −2⟩ and ⟨−2, 6⟩ gives the same answer. Scaling a combined change is equivalent to scaling each contributing change.

This is not a coincidence of our numbers. In the horizontal component, k(ax + bx) = kax + kbx, by ordinary arithmetic. The vertical component obeys the same rule. Consequently k(a + b) = ka + kb. This is the distributive property.

Key idea 1.12 · Keep the coefficient with the whole vector

You may distribute a scalar over a sum, and collect multiples of the same vector:

k(a + b) = ka + kb
ka + ma = (k + m)a.

A minus sign before brackets changes every term inside them. For example, −(a − b) = −a + b.

These rules let you simplify before substituting components. For example, 3a − (a + 2b) becomes 3a − a − 2b = 2a − 2b. Do not combine this into “0a” just because the numerical coefficients add to zero: a and b are generally different vectors. Like terms must contain the same vector.

Discussion of Activity 1.14

(a) The simplified form is 2a − 2b = ⟨2, −4⟩ − ⟨6, 2⟩ = ⟨−4, −6⟩. Directly, 3a = ⟨3, −6⟩ and a + 2b = ⟨7, 0⟩; subtracting again gives ⟨−4, −6⟩. (b) First a − b = ⟨−2, −3⟩, so multiplying by −2 gives ⟨4, 6⟩. Expanding gives −2a + 2b = ⟨−2, 4⟩ + ⟨6, 2⟩ = ⟨4, 6⟩.

(c) The left-hand side is 2⟨4, −1⟩ = ⟨8, −2⟩. The proposed right-hand side is ⟨2, −4⟩ + ⟨3, 1⟩ = ⟨5, −3⟩. It omits a second copy of b. (d) 3p = ⟨6, 0, −3⟩ and ½q = ⟨−½, 2, 1⟩. Their difference is ⟨6½, −2, −4⟩.

1.13.2 Finding an unknown change

Sometimes the endpoint is prescribed and a stage of the route is unknown. An equation lets us recover it. In x + a = b, subtracting a from both sides gives x = b − a. The same component operations justify the familiar algebraic step.

Example 1.13 · An unknown repeated stage

Find x if 2x + a = b, where a = ⟨−2, 4⟩ m and b = ⟨6, −2⟩ m.

Solution

Subtract a to obtain 2x = b − a = ⟨8, −6⟩ m. Multiplying both sides by ½ gives x = ⟨4, −3⟩ m.

Substitution checks the answer in the original equation: 2⟨4, −3⟩ + ⟨−2, 4⟩ = ⟨8, −6⟩ + ⟨−2, 4⟩ = ⟨6, −2⟩ m, as required. We divided components by the nonzero scalar 2; we did not divide one vector by another.

Activity 1.15 · Recover a missing vector

  1. Solve 3x + ⟨−1, 2⟩ = ⟨5, −7⟩. Check by substitution.
  2. Solve ⟨2, −1, 4⟩ − y = ⟨−1, 3, 2⟩. Explain why simply subtracting the first given vector from the second would have the wrong sign.
Hint for Activity 1.15

For (b), add y to both sides first. This avoids losing the minus sign attached to the unknown.

Discussion of Activity 1.15

(a) 3x = ⟨5, −7⟩ − ⟨−1, 2⟩ = ⟨6, −9⟩, so x = ⟨2, −3⟩. Checking gives ⟨6, −9⟩ + ⟨−1, 2⟩ = ⟨5, −7⟩. (b) Rearranging gives y = ⟨2, −1, 4⟩ − ⟨−1, 3, 2⟩ = ⟨3, −4, 2⟩. Checking: ⟨2, −1, 4⟩ − ⟨3, −4, 2⟩ = ⟨−1, 3, 2⟩. Subtracting in the other order would give −y, not y.

1.14 Returning to the route

We began by asking how several directed changes combine. The arrow picture and the component calculation now give two ways to answer. Joining arrows retains the route; adding their components finds its overall effect. Scalar multiplication lets us repeat, shorten or reverse a stage, while subtraction identifies the change needed to get from one endpoint to another.

Use the following activity to bring these ideas together. Try it without looking back at the rules. If your drawing and calculation disagree, use the starting point, intermediate endpoints and final-minus-initial check to locate the disagreement.

Activity 1.16 · Complete and reverse a route

A robot starts at P = (−1, 2) m on a level test area with fixed axes. Let a = ⟨2, 1⟩ m and b = ⟨−1, 2⟩ m. It makes 2a, then −b, then an unknown displacement c. Its required final position is Q = (4, 0) m.

  1. Sketch the first two stages and label their endpoints. Find their combined displacement.
  2. Find c, then check that all three changes take P to Q.
  3. Find the single displacement that returns directly from Q to P. Distinguish it from the coordinates of P.
  4. Would interchanging the first two stages change c? Explain what would change about the diagram.
Hint for Activity 1.16

First find the required total Q minus P. The missing displacement is that total minus the sum of the known stages.

Discussion of Activity 1.16

(a) 2a = ⟨4, 2⟩ m takes P to (3, 4) m. Then −b = ⟨1, −2⟩ m takes it to (4, 2) m. These stages combine to ⟨5, 0⟩ m. (b) The required overall displacement is ⟨4 − (−1), 0 − 2⟩ = ⟨5, −2⟩ m. Thus c = ⟨5, −2⟩ − ⟨5, 0⟩ = ⟨0, −2⟩ m. From (4, 2), this reaches (4, 0). Equivalently, adding all three changes to P gives (−1 + 4 + 1 + 0, 2 + 2 − 2 − 2) = (4, 0).

(c) The return displacement is ⟨−5, 2⟩ m. It changes Q into P; it is not P’s position vector ⟨−1, 2⟩ m. (d) The intermediate point would become P − b = (0, 0) m, followed by (4, 2) m. The known resultant remains ⟨5, 0⟩ m, so the required c is unchanged. The route to (4, 2) differs.

Before leaving the lesson, explain aloud why a minus sign reverses an arrow, why a sum does not depend on where its first arrow is drawn, and why a final position must not be confused with a resultant. If any explanation feels uncertain, return to the corresponding diagram and trace a concrete route.

There is still a question we have deliberately left open: given components such as ⟨3, 4⟩ m, how long is the arrow? Component addition tells us how changes combine, but does not by itself tell us their lengths. Lesson 3 connects components with magnitude, then separates a vector’s length from its direction using a unit vector. The cumulative exercises at the end of Unit 1 will return to these operations alongside that later material.
