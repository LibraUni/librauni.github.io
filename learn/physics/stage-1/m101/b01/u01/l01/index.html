M101 · Mathematics for physics I

Mathematics and the
description of the world

A photograph of a moving object records where it was when the shutter opened. A second photograph, taken a little later, gives us something more: the beginnings of a description of its motion. With a scale and a clock, we can start to ask how far the object has moved, how quickly its position is changing, and whether that change is steady.

These questions are simple to state, but answering them takes us a surprisingly long way into mathematics. We need numbers to record measurements, a way of specifying position, and a means of comparing what happens at different times. If the motion changes, we must also decide what we mean by its speed at a particular instant. The mathematical ideas that emerge from these questions are useful far beyond moving objects. They help us describe the heating of a material, the flow of an electric current and the changing brightness of a star.

This module introduces some of those ideas. You will learn to work with quantities that have direction, investigate how one quantity changes in relation to another, and calculate totals built up from many small contributions. Along the way, you will use equations, diagrams and short computer calculations to look at the same problem from different points of view.

From an observation to a mathematical question

Imagine filming a bead as it moves along a straight track. A ruler beside the track allows you to estimate its position in each frame. You could list those positions in a table, draw them on a graph, or try to find an equation that describes how position depends on time. Each representation brings out something different. A table preserves individual readings; a graph makes a pattern visible; an equation may allow you to calculate a position at a time when no photograph was taken.

That last step requires care. An equation inferred from a few measurements need not describe everything the bead will do. Perhaps friction changes along the track, or the bead eventually reaches an end stop. We therefore have to distinguish between the object itself and the mathematical description we have chosen for it. Such a description is called a model. A useful model includes the features needed for the question we are asking, while leaving out others.

In Figure M1, the bead changes position by 2 cm during the first second and by 6 cm during the next. We can already compare these changes using arithmetic. Later, you will learn how to describe a rate of change over successively shorter intervals. This leads to the derivative, one of the central ideas of calculus. We will also investigate the reverse kind of question: if we know how quickly a quantity is changing, how can we find the change that has accumulated? That question leads to integration.

You are not expected to know calculus before beginning. The purpose of this introduction is to show where the work is heading. The definitions, calculations and qualifications will be developed when they are needed.

A wider view · optional reading

An address in space

The James Webb Space Telescope operates in an orbit around the Sun near the Sun–Earth L2 point, about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth. It does not remain motionless at that point: it follows an orbit around the L2 region. NASA explains that this arrangement helps keep the Sun, Earth and Moon on the same side of the telescope, where its sunshield can block their light and heat. [1]

Even this short description raises mathematical questions. Where is a position measured from? In which direction does the telescope lie? How does that position change with time? A distance alone cannot answer all three.

Calculating such an orbit requires physics and mathematics beyond this module. But the first task—turning a description of position into something we can calculate with—is exactly where we will begin.

The route through M101

The first block, Vectors and linear relationships, begins with position and direction. You will describe these using coordinates and vectors, then learn how vectors can be combined and compared. Matrices will give us a compact way of representing transformations and related equations. A rotation or reflection can then be studied both as a change in a diagram and as a calculation with numbers.

The second block, Change and local behaviour, develops differentiation. We will begin with the behaviour of functions and work towards methods for finding rates of change. These methods help us investigate where a quantity increases, where it decreases and where it may have a maximum or minimum. Their usefulness depends on understanding the question being asked, rather than simply recognising a formula to apply.

In the third block, Accumulation and integral models, the emphasis turns to totals: how small contributions combine to produce an area, an average or an accumulated change. You will see how integration and differentiation are connected. You will also compare exact calculations with numerical estimates, which are especially useful when a convenient exact calculation is unavailable.

The final block, Approximation, validation and mathematical judgement, brings these methods together. We will ask what makes an answer trustworthy. Does it have the right units? Does it behave sensibly in a simple case? Does a numerical calculation support the reasoning, and what does that support actually establish? These questions will recur throughout the module, becoming more substantial as your mathematical tools develop.

Working with the material

Mathematics is often understood in two stages. A worked solution can seem clear while you are reading it; only when you try a related question do you discover which parts you can reproduce and which remain uncertain. For that reason, the activities form part of the teaching. Keep paper beside you, draw the diagrams, and write enough working to make your reasoning visible. An unfinished attempt is often a better starting point for a tutorial than an answer copied from a solution.

When you read a worked example, attend to the decisions as well as the arithmetic. Why was that representation chosen? What does the symbol refer to? Why is the next step allowed? When you check your own work against a discussion, look for the first place where the reasoning differs. Sometimes the problem will be a sign or a calculation; sometimes it will be the interpretation of the question.

Green boxes collect important definitions and results after we have developed them. Sepia boxes contain worked examples, including the decisions behind the calculation. Numbered activities invite you to pause and try an idea; a substantial exercise section closes each unit. Short notes beside the text clarify notation or recall an earlier idea. Blue boxes marked A wider view or Historical perspective offer applications and context; you can leave these aside on a first pass without losing a prerequisite. Essential results will be identified separately. The reference sheet at the end of each completed unit will collect the notation, methods and conditions you need to return to.

M101 is studied alongside P101, Physics, space & scientific thinking. P101 introduces the shared Python tools. We will use them in M101 once that foundation is in place, for such tasks as checking a vector calculation or exploring a numerical approximation. The opening unit requires no programming. Familiarity with signed numbers, elementary algebra, graphs and trigonometry will be useful across the module; short reminders will appear where they are needed.

The mathematical and physical strands will meet repeatedly, but they have different responsibilities. Here we will take time over the mathematical reasoning. When a physical situation is used before its theory has been taught, the assumptions and relationships needed for the calculation will be supplied. You should not have to guess an unfamiliar law of physics in order to learn a mathematical method.

Block 1 · Vectors and linear relationships

Unit 1
Coordinates, vectors
and components

Introduction

If you arrange to meet someone “two kilometres from the station”, you have given useful information, but not enough to locate the meeting place. There are many possible destinations at that distance. A direction, or some other way of identifying a position, is still needed.

The same difficulty appears in physics. To describe where an object is, we need a reference and a way of distinguishing one direction from another. To describe a change in position, we must compare a starting point with a finishing point. These requirements are familiar from maps and everyday directions. In this unit we will turn them into mathematical descriptions that can be drawn, calculated and checked.

The street pattern in Figure 1.1 offers a useful starting point. Two directions can organise a description of place without either direction having to point towards the top of the page. A mathematical coordinate system makes the reference point, directions and measurement scale explicit.

We begin with coordinates. A coordinate system supplies a reference point and a set of directions, allowing a position to be described by numbers. At first we will work along a line and then in a plane, such as a map or a plan of a room. A third coordinate will allow us to extend the same reasoning to space.

A pair of coordinates describes a point’s position relative to the chosen system. But we will also want to describe a directed change from one point to another. This leads to a vector. Vectors allow us to keep track of direction as well as size, so that a movement to the left is distinguished from a movement of the same size to the right. The arrows used in our diagrams will make this distinction visible.

Once these descriptions are established, we will learn to combine vectors, find their lengths and express them in terms of perpendicular components. Trigonometry will connect a description using length and angle with a description using components. You will meet several representations of the same vector; an important part of the unit is learning to choose between them and recognise what each makes easier to see.

Many of the early calculations are short. Their purpose is to help you make precise distinctions: a point and a vector, a position and a displacement, a signed component and a length. These distinctions become useful when several quantities appear in the same problem. They will also prepare you to resolve quantities into components in P101.

As you work, keep the diagrams close to the algebra. If a component is negative, be ready to point to the direction it represents. If two answers look different, consider whether they use different reference points or describe different quantities. We will begin with the simplest setting, where a single number is enough to locate a point.

Coordinates, position and displacement

1.1 Describing a position

1.1.1 Position along a line

Consider a straight passage with a doorway somewhere along it. We can use the doorway as a reference point and measure positions along the passage in metres. If we agree that positions to the right are positive and positions to the left are negative, a single number locates any point on the line.

The reference point is called the origin, and its coordinate is zero. In Figure 1.2, A has coordinate −3 m because it is three metres to the left of the origin. B has coordinate 2 m because it is two metres to the right. The minus sign in −3 does not mean that a length is negative. It distinguishes the side of the origin on which A lies.

This convention is a choice. We could place the origin at another point or choose the opposite direction as positive. The physical passage would remain the same, but the numbers used to describe positions would change. For now, keep the origin and positive direction fixed so that everyone reading the diagram is using the same description.

Key idea 1.1 · A coordinate needs a convention

To specify a position along a line, choose an origin, a positive direction and a unit. The sign of the coordinate identifies the side of the origin; it does not make the distance from the origin negative.

Suppose we move from A to B. Our coordinate changes from −3 m to 2 m. To find the change, we ask what number must be added to −3 to give 2. The answer is 5, since

−3 + 5 = 2.
Note 1.1 · Position or change?
A coordinate describes a position. A change compares two positions. Both may be measured in metres, so units alone do not distinguish them.

The signed change in coordinate is therefore +5 m. We can obtain it by subtracting the starting coordinate from the finishing coordinate:

change = 2 − (−3) = 5 m.

The brackets show that the number being subtracted is −3. In the reverse direction, from B to A, the change is −3 − 2 = −5 m. The numerical size is the same, but the sign records the opposite direction. This is a useful example of why signs are part of a mathematical description, rather than merely marks to manage during arithmetic.

Example 1.1 · Finding a finishing position

A trolley starts at coordinate 4 m on an axis whose positive direction is to the right. It moves 7 m to the left. Find its finishing coordinate and check the direction of its motion.

Solution

First express the movement with a sign. A movement to the left is negative in this coordinate system, so the change is −7 m.

finishing coordinate = 4 + (−7) = −3 m.

The trolley finishes three metres to the left of the origin. As a check, the change computed from the two positions is −3 − 4 = −7 m, which agrees with the stated movement. The distance moved is 7 m; the signed change is −7 m.

Activity 1.1 · Positions and changes

Use the coordinate convention in Figure 1.2.

  1. A point C is four metres to the left of the doorway. Write its coordinate.
  2. Find the signed change from C to a point D with coordinate 1 m.
  3. A point starts at coordinate −2 m and its coordinate changes by −3 m. Where does it finish?
Discussion

(a) C has coordinate −4 m. (b) The change is 1 − (−4) = +5 m. Crossing the origin does not change the rule: subtract the starting value from the finishing value. (c) The finishing coordinate is −2 + (−3) = −5 m. The point finishes five metres to the left of O. Notice that −3 m is the change, whereas −5 m is the final position.

1.1.2 Coordinates in a plane

A single coordinate is enough along a line because there is only one direction in which position can vary, together with its reverse. On a flat surface, a point can also lie to either side of that line. A second coordinate is needed to distinguish these possibilities.

We introduce two perpendicular lines, called coordinate axes. Their intersection is the origin, O. In the usual drawing, the horizontal axis is labelled x and the vertical axis y. Arrowheads indicate the positive directions. Figure 1.3 uses positive x to the right and positive y upwards, with both coordinates measured in metres.

To specify P, we write its x-coordinate first and its y-coordinate second, enclosed in brackets and separated by a comma:

P = (2, 3).

We have already stated that the coordinates are in metres. The pair therefore identifies a position two metres in the positive x-direction from O and three metres in the positive y-direction. You can think of these as instructions for locating the point, but the point itself does not depend on which of the two moves you imagine making first.

Note 1.2 · Letter O, number 0
O labels the origin; 0 is the number zero. In a plane, O has coordinates (0, 0).

The order in which the coordinates are written does matter. The pair (3, 2) names a different point: three metres to the right and two metres up. For this reason, a pair of coordinates is called an ordered pair. The first entry belongs to the horizontal axis and the second to the vertical axis in the convention used here.

The same reading rule applies when a coordinate is negative. Q lies left of the vertical axis, so its first coordinate is negative. It lies above the horizontal axis, so its second coordinate is positive. Its coordinates are (−3, 1). There is no need for a new rule on the other side of an axis; the sign already supplies the required distinction.

Historical perspective · optional reading

When geometry acquired an algebraic language

The adjective Cartesian comes from the Latin form of René Descartes’ name. His La Géométrie, published in 1637, developed the use of algebra in geometrical problems. It appeared as one of three accompanying essays to his Discourse on the Method; the others concerned optics and meteorology. Mathematics was being presented alongside an attempt to understand the natural world. [2]

Descartes was not working in isolation. Pierre de Fermat also developed methods connecting equations and geometrical curves. This connection between equations and curves was developed through more than one line of investigation. [3]

The enduring idea is that a geometrical question can be translated into algebra. Later you will use that connection in both directions: an equation can describe a shape, while a picture can help explain what an equation means.

This system is called a rectangular, or Cartesian, coordinate system. In the present unit the axes will be perpendicular. We will use other mathematical descriptions later in the programme, but there is already considerable freedom within this one: we choose the origin, the directions of the axes and the unit of length. These choices need to be clear before the numbers can be interpreted.

Key idea 1.2 · Reading rectangular coordinates

In our diagrams, write a point as (x, y): horizontal coordinate first, vertical coordinate second. Read the scale on each axis before assigning the numbers. A point on the horizontal axis has y = 0; a point on the vertical axis has x = 0.

Example 1.2 · Reading a scale

On a coordinate diagram, successive ticks on the horizontal axis are labelled 0, 2, 4 and 6 metres. Successive ticks on the vertical axis are labelled 0, 1, 2 and 3 metres. A point R lies at the second tick to the right of zero and the third tick above zero. Find its coordinates.

Solution

The horizontal ticks are two metres apart, so the second tick to the right of zero represents 4 m. The vertical ticks are one metre apart, so the third tick above zero represents 3 m. Hence

R = (4, 3),

with coordinates in metres. Counting “two across and three up” without reading the scales would have given (2, 3), which is incorrect. A grid square is a feature of the drawing; its physical size is determined by the scale.

Different drawing scales can be convenient when quantities span different ranges. They do not alter how coordinates are read. They do, however, affect the appearance of the picture: a length or angle measured on a distorted drawing need not represent the corresponding physical length or angle. In our geometrical diagrams we will normally use the same scale on both axes.

Activity 1.2 · Reading an ordered pair

Draw perpendicular axes, using one square to represent one metre in each direction. Plot A = (−2, 3), B = (3, −2), C = (0, −3) and D = (4, 0).

  1. Explain why A and B are different points, even though the same two numbers appear in their coordinates.
  2. Which point lies on the horizontal axis? Which lies on the vertical axis?
  3. Give the coordinates of the origin. Explain the role of zero in your answers to this part and the previous part.
Discussion

A is two metres left and three metres up; B is three metres right and two metres down. Their first entries specify different horizontal positions, and their second entries specify different vertical positions.

D lies on the horizontal axis because its vertical coordinate is zero. C lies on the vertical axis because its horizontal coordinate is zero. The origin has coordinates (0, 0): it lies on both axes. A zero coordinate is useful information about a position; it does not mean that the point is missing or that its other coordinate must also be zero.

Looking back and ahead

Our starting difficulty was that a distance alone does not locate a place. Coordinates resolve that difficulty by making the reference point and directions explicit. Their signs and order carry meaning, and the scale tells us how to interpret the numbers. These conventions are worth checking before beginning a calculation.

We can now locate a point using a pair of numbers and recover its position from those numbers. The next step is to attach a directed arrow to that description. An arrow from O to P will represent P’s position vector; an arrow from one point to another will allow us to describe a displacement. The coordinate system we have established gives us the means to calculate with both.

Sample exercises

Opening selection · These exercises cover Section 1.1. These demonstrate exercise presentation only. Actual units will end with exercises covering the entire unit.

Try the questions on paper before opening the solutions. Include a sketch when it helps you explain your answer. If you use a hint, return to the question and finish the reasoning yourself before checking the worked solution.

Exercise 1.1

A sensor moves on a line measured in centimetres, with positive to the right. It starts at −6 cm and finishes at 3 cm. Find its signed change, the distance it moved if it travelled directly, and the signed change for the return trip.

Hint for Exercise 1.1

Subtract the starting coordinate from the finishing coordinate.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.1

The outward change is 3 − (−6) = +9 cm. For a direct journey the distance is 9 cm. The return change is −6 − 3 = −9 cm. The opposite signs describe opposite directions; the two distances are equal.

Return to Exercise 1.1

Exercise 1.2

On perpendicular axes with the same scale, plot A = (−3, 2), B = (2, −3), C = (0, 2) and D = (−3, 0). Coordinates are in metres. Describe each position in words, and identify the points on an axis.

Hint for Exercise 1.2

Read the horizontal coordinate first. One coordinate is zero for a point on an axis.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.2

A is 3 m left and 2 m above O; B is 2 m right and 3 m below O. C is 2 m above O on the vertical axis. D is 3 m left of O on the horizontal axis. A and B are off both axes; exchanging the entries changes the point.

Return to Exercise 1.2

Exercise 1.3

A diagram uses 5 m between successive horizontal ticks and 2 m between successive vertical ticks. A point lies two ticks left of the origin and three ticks above it. Write its coordinates. Explain the error in the answer (−2, 3).

Hint for Exercise 1.3

Tick counts become coordinates only after multiplying by the corresponding scale.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.3

The horizontal coordinate is −2 × 5 = −10 m and the vertical coordinate is 3 × 2 = 6 m. The ordered pair is (−10, 6), in metres. The proposed answer records tick counts, not the distances specified by the axes.

Return to Exercise 1.3

Exercise 1.4

Two observers describe the same straight passage using metres and positive to the right. Observer A places the origin at a doorway. Observer B places it 4 m to the right of that doorway. A marker has coordinate 1 m for A. Find its coordinate for B. Has the marker moved?

Hint for Exercise 1.4

Sketch both origins and the marker on one line before assigning the second coordinate.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.4

The marker is 1 m right of A’s origin, whereas B’s origin is 4 m right of it. Relative to B it lies 3 m to the left, so its coordinate is −3 m. The marker has not moved: the reference point changed. This is why a coordinate needs its convention.

Return to Exercise 1.4

Exercise 1.5

A learner writes: “The point (0, −4) lies on the horizontal axis because its first coordinate is zero.” Explain the mistake and give the correct location. Then state a rule that identifies all points on the horizontal axis.

Hint for Exercise 1.5

Which coordinate measures displacement above or below the horizontal axis?

Worked solution to Exercise 1.5

The first coordinate is horizontal, so zero puts the point on the vertical axis. Its second coordinate places it four units below O. Points on the horizontal axis have second coordinate zero, so their form is (x, 0). A zero coordinate refers to one direction, not to the absence of a point.

Return to Exercise 1.5
