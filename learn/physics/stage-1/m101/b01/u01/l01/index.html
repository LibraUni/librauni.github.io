Lesson 1
Coordinates, position
and displacement

A marker on a map answers “where?”. An arrow between two markers answers a different question: “what change in position takes us from here to there?”. The numbers used in these two descriptions can look very similar. Learning to tell them apart will make the calculations in this unit much easier to interpret.

We begin along a line, where a sign distinguishes the two directions. We then extend the same reasoning to a plane and to three-dimensional space. By the end of the lesson, you should be able to read a position, describe it by a vector from an origin, calculate a displacement between two positions and explain what changes when the origin is moved.

Keep paper beside you. Draw the short sketches and attempt the activities before opening their discussions. Signed numbers and simple equations are the only algebra needed here; the first section gives a brief reminder. The lesson’s three-hour allocation includes the Unit 1 introduction, time to think, the activities and correction. It is quite reasonable to pause after the two-dimensional work and return to the rest later.

1.1 Positions and changes along a line

Imagine a straight passage with a doorway somewhere along it. Choose the doorway as a reference point and measure positions along the passage in metres. If we agree that positions to the right are positive and positions to the left are negative, a single number locates any point on the line. A location three metres to the left of the doorway has coordinate −3 m. A location two metres to its right has coordinate 2 m.

The reference point is called the origin, and its coordinate is zero. The minus sign in −3 tells us which side of O contains A; it does not make the distance from O negative. Choosing the opposite positive direction would change the signs of the coordinates without changing the passage or moving any point.

Key idea 1.1 · Specifying a coordinate

A coordinate along a line needs an origin, a positive direction and a unit. State these before interpreting the number.

1.1.1 From a starting position to a finishing position

Suppose we move from A to B in Figure 1.2. Our coordinate changes from −3 m to 2 m. What number must be added to −3 to obtain 2? Since −3 + 5 = 2, the signed change is +5 m. Equivalently, subtract the starting coordinate from the finishing coordinate:

change = 2 − (−3) = 5 m.

The brackets are useful: the number being subtracted is the negative number −3. The relation behind the calculation is start + change = finish. Checking this relation is often more informative than repeating the subtraction. On the return journey, from B to A, the change is −3 − 2 = −5 m. The sign reverses because the direction reverses.

A coordinate and a change in coordinate can both be measured in metres. The unit alone therefore does not tell us which is meant. “At −3 m” describes a position relative to O; “a change of −3 m” describes a movement in the negative direction from whichever starting point has been specified.

Note 1.1
Keep the words “starting”, “finishing” and “change” beside your numbers while learning the method. They prevent a correct calculation from answering the wrong question.

Example 1.1 · A missing starting position

A trolley moves 7 m to the left and finishes at coordinate −3 m. The positive direction is to the right. Find its starting coordinate.

Solution

First express the movement with a sign. Then use start + change = finish.

The change is −7 m. If the starting coordinate is s, then

s + (−7) = −3.

Here all coordinate values are in metres. Adding 7 to both sides gives s = 4, so the trolley started at 4 m. As a check, starting at 4 m and moving 7 m left takes it through the origin to −3 m. The negative finishing coordinate does not imply that it started on the negative side.

Activity 1.1 · Recovering the change

  1. Evaluate 4 − (−2). Explain what the inner minus sign belongs to.
  2. A point moves directly from −4 m to 1 m. Give the signed change and describe its direction.
  3. A point starts at −2 m and its coordinate changes by −3 m. Find its finishing coordinate and check your answer using final minus initial.
Discussion of Activity 1.1

(a) 4 − (−2) = 6. The inner minus sign is part of the number −2 being subtracted. (b) The change is 1 − (−4) = +5 m, towards the positive direction. The check is −4 + 5 = 1. (c) The finish is −2 + (−3) = −5 m; the reverse check is −5 − (−2) = −3 m.

If subtraction across zero is troublesome, draw the line and try this replacement item: start at −1 m and finish at 3 m. Count the movement, then write −1 + change = 3. The change is +4 m. Use that connection between the picture and the equation before returning to part (b). There is no score to achieve here; a difficulty identifies something to revisit.

1.2 Locating points in a plane

Along a line, there is only one direction in which position can vary, together with its reverse. On a flat surface, a point can also lie to either side of that line. A second coordinate is needed. A floor plan provides a familiar example: knowing how far a chair is from one wall does not usually locate it without a second measurement.

Introduce two perpendicular lines, called coordinate axes. Their intersection is the origin, O. We label the horizontal axis x and the vertical axis y, with positive x to the right and positive y upwards. These are directions in the drawing. If the drawing is a floor plan, “upwards” on the page need not mean upwards towards the ceiling.

1.2.1 The order of the coordinates

For P, read the horizontal coordinate first and the vertical coordinate second. Write the two numbers in brackets, separated by a comma: P = (2, 3), with coordinates in metres. Starting at O, move two metres in the positive x-direction and three metres in the positive y-direction to locate P. You would reach the same place if you imagined those two moves in the opposite order.

The order in which the numbers are written does matter. The pair (3, 2) describes a different place, three metres right and two metres up. For this reason the coordinates form an ordered pair. Q is left of the vertical axis and above the horizontal axis, so its coordinates are (−3, 1). The same rule works on either side of either axis.

A point on the vertical axis has horizontal coordinate zero. Its vertical coordinate need not be zero: (0, −3), for example, is three metres below O. A point on the horizontal axis has vertical coordinate zero. Only the origin lies on both axes.

Note 1.2
The letter O names the origin; the number 0 is zero. The coordinates of O are (0, 0).

The axes divide the plane into four regions, called quadrants. We can distinguish them by coordinate signs: (+, +), (−, +), (−, −) and (+, −). Points on the axes form the boundaries rather than belonging to any of these four regions. For now, the signs are more useful than memorising quadrant names.

Key idea 1.2 · Reading rectangular coordinates

For the convention used here, write (x, y): horizontal first, vertical second. Read each axis scale. Zero in one coordinate places the point on the other coordinate axis.

1.2.2 Read the scale, not just the squares

Example 1.2 · Unequal drawing scales

The horizontal axis is marked 0, 2, 4, 6 metres at successive ticks. The vertical axis is marked 0, 1, 2, 3 metres. R lies at the second horizontal tick to the right of zero and the third vertical tick above zero. Find its coordinates.

Solution

The horizontal ticks are two metres apart, so the second tick represents 4 m. The vertical ticks are one metre apart, so the third represents 3 m. Hence R = (4, 3), with coordinates in metres. The tempting answer (2, 3) counts ticks without interpreting their scale.

Conversely, to plot S = (−4, 2) on these same axes, go two ticks left and two ticks up. A drawing can stretch one direction more than another. Coordinate reading still works, but angles or lengths measured directly on such a drawing can be misleading.

Activity 1.2 · Plotting and interpreting points

Draw perpendicular axes, with one square representing one metre on each axis.

  1. Plot A = (−2, 3), B = (3, −2), C = (0, −3), D = (4, 0), E = (−1, −2) and F = (2, 1). Which points are on axes? Which have both coordinates negative?
  2. Explain why A and B differ although their coordinate pairs contain the same two numbers.
  3. On a separate diagram with 5 m per horizontal tick and 2 m per vertical tick, a point is two ticks left and three ticks above O. Find its coordinates.
Discussion of Activity 1.2

(a) A is two squares left and three up; B is three right and two down. C lies on the vertical axis, three below O; D lies on the horizontal axis, four right. E is one left and two down, so both its coordinates are negative. F is two right and one up. These four off-axis points sample all four sign combinations. (b) A’s first coordinate describes a leftward position, whereas B’s first coordinate describes a rightward position; their vertical positions also differ. (c) Multiply each tick count by its own scale: x = −2 × 5 = −10 m and y = 3 × 2 = 6 m, giving (−10, 6).

Historical perspective · optional reading

When geometry acquired an algebraic language

The adjective Cartesian comes from the Latin form of René Descartes’ name. His La Géométrie, published in 1637, developed the use of algebra in geometrical problems. It appeared as one of three accompanying essays to his Discourse on the Method; the others concerned optics and meteorology. Mathematics was being presented alongside an attempt to understand the natural world. MacTutor: Descartes

Descartes was not working in isolation. Pierre de Fermat also developed methods connecting equations and geometrical curves. This connection between equations and curves was developed through more than one line of investigation. MacTutor: Fermat

The enduring idea is that a geometrical question can be translated into algebra. Later you will use that connection in both directions: an equation can describe a shape, while a picture can help explain what an equation means.

We call this a rectangular, or Cartesian, coordinate system. Coordinates turn a location into something we can calculate with. The next step is to connect that location to a directed arrow.

1.3 A point and its position vector

A dot marks a location but does not, by itself, indicate a direction. Now draw an arrow from O to P in Figure 1.3. The arrow begins at O, its tail, and ends at P, its head. It represents the directed change needed to reach P from the origin. We call it the position vector of P relative to O.

A vector describes a quantity with magnitude and direction. Here, the magnitude is the size of the directed change, represented by the arrow’s length on an equally scaled diagram. We will learn how to calculate lengths in Lesson 3. For this lesson, the signed changes along the axes are sufficient to specify our vectors. They are called the vector’s components.

1.3.1 Connecting the arrow and the numbers

The arrow from O to P changes the horizontal coordinate from 0 to 2 m and the vertical coordinate from 0 to 3 m. We write its position vector as

rP = ⟨2, 3⟩ m.

The bold letter r denotes a vector; the subscript P identifies the point being located. Angle brackets distinguish a list of vector components from a point’s coordinate pair. The unit outside the brackets applies to every entry. Thus the horizontal component is 2 m and the vertical component is 3 m. We may label these rP,x and rP,y: P identifies the point and the final letter identifies the coordinate direction.

You will also encounter an arrow over a letter as vector notation. In handwriting, an arrow over r, or an underline, is easier to distinguish than a bold letter. The essential habit is to make clear whether a symbol represents a whole vector or one of its numerical components.

The coordinates of P and the components of rP contain the same numbers because the vector starts at the zero point of this particular system. That numerical agreement does not make the two descriptions interchangeable. P answers where the endpoint is; rP describes the directed change from the chosen origin to that endpoint.

Example 1.3 · The head is not the whole arrow

For P = (2, 3) and T = (−2, 0), with coordinates in metres, identify the components of the arrows from O to P and from T to P.

Solution

From O to P, the changes are 2 m right and 3 m up: ⟨2, 3⟩ m. Starting at T requires 4 m right to reach P’s horizontal coordinate and 3 m up to reach its vertical coordinate: ⟨4, 3⟩ m.

Both arrows end at P, but their horizontal changes differ. Reading only the head’s coordinates gives the components of an arrow only when its tail is at O. This is the reason to mark both ends before calculating.

1.3.2 What happens at the origin?

If P coincides with O, its position vector has components ⟨0, 0⟩ m. There is no change in either coordinate. This is the zero vector, written 0. Its magnitude is zero, and it has no unique direction: there is no nonzero arrow to point one way rather than another. On a diagram we mark the coincident tail and head with a labelled point.

Key idea 1.3 · A position vector starts at the origin

If P has coordinates (x, y), its position vector relative to O has components ⟨x, y⟩, in the same units. The zero vector is the case in which both components vanish; it has no unique direction.

Activity 1.3 · Reading the role of an arrow

Let R = (−3, 2), S = (1, 2) and O = (0, 0), with coordinates in metres.

  1. Sketch the position vector of R. Label its tail and head and write its components.
  2. Draw an arrow from S to R. Why is this not R’s position vector relative to O? Describe its horizontal and vertical changes.
  3. Write the position vector of O and explain why drawing an arbitrary arrowhead at O would be misleading.
Discussion of Activity 1.3

(a) The tail is O, the head is R and the components are ⟨−3, 2⟩ m. (b) The tail is S rather than O. Moving from S to R requires 4 m left and no vertical change, so this arrow has components ⟨−4, 0⟩ m. (c) O has position vector ⟨0, 0⟩ m. An arrowhead suggesting a particular direction would attach information the zero vector does not have.

We have described a few arrows by counting changes on a grid. A systematic calculation will let us handle endpoints that are less convenient to draw.

1.4 Displacement from one position to another

Suppose a small inspection carriage starts at A on a level surface and ends at B. We know each position relative to O, but the movement begins at A. The carriage’s displacement is the directed change from its initial position to its final position. Its arrow therefore has its tail at A and its head at B.

Use the same axes, origin and units for both endpoints. If A has coordinates (xA, yA) and B has coordinates (xB, yB), the horizontal change must satisfy

xA + Δx = xB.

The Greek capital letter Δ, pronounced “delta”, means a change here. It is part of the symbol Δx, not a number multiplying x. Subtracting xA from both sides gives Δx = xB − xA. Applying the same reasoning vertically gives Δy = yB − yA.

Key idea 1.4 · Final position minus initial position

Δr = rB − rA
= ⟨xB − xA, yB − yA⟩.

Subtract corresponding components in the same coordinate system. Include their units. The arrow runs from A to B.

This is our first use of vector subtraction. For displacement, it means taking the final value minus the initial value in each coordinate direction. Lesson 2 will develop vector operations more generally. At this stage, the start-plus-change relation explains every subtraction we need.

Example 1.4 · Two endpoints away from the origin

The inspection carriage moves from A = (−2, 1) to B = (3, −1), with coordinates in metres. Calculate and interpret its displacement.

Solution

Before calculating, compare the endpoints. B lies to the right of A and below it, so we expect a positive horizontal component and a negative vertical component.

Δx = 3 − (−2) = 5 m,
Δy = −1 − 1 = −2 m.

Therefore Δr = ⟨5, −2⟩ m. The final position is five metres farther in the positive x-direction and two metres farther in the negative y-direction than the starting position.

Check by reconstructing B: −2 + 5 = 3 and 1 + (−2) = −1. Both final coordinates are recovered. The result ⟨3, −1⟩ m would instead be B’s position vector from O; it would ignore where the carriage started.

1.4.1 Reversing the journey and returning to the start

For the return from B to A, the final and initial labels swap. The horizontal change becomes −2 − 3 = −5 m, and the vertical change becomes 1 − (−1) = 2 m. The return displacement is ⟨−5, 2⟩ m. Each component has changed sign, matching the reversal of the arrow.

If the start and finish are the same point, subtraction gives zero in both components. The displacement is then the zero vector, even if an object travelled a long way in between. Leaving a room, walking around the building and returning to the same spot produces no net change in position.

1.4.2 An endpoint description is not a route description

The arrow A→B need not be the carriage’s actual path. It might travel along a straight line, turn around an obstacle or make a detour. All such journeys have the same displacement if their initial and final positions agree. They need not have the same distance travelled, because distance accumulates along the actual route.

We will calculate vector magnitudes later. For now, keep the distinction verbal and geometric: displacement asks for the directed change between endpoints; distance travelled asks how much ground the actual journey covers.

Activity 1.4 · Signs, endpoints and checks

  1. C = (4, −3) and D = (−1, 2), in metres. Begin by writing Δx = −1 − 4; supply the vertical calculation and give the displacement C→D. Check both final coordinates.
  2. For E = (−3, −2) and F = (2, −4), calculate E→F and F→E without a supplied first step. Explain the signs.
  3. An object leaves E and returns to E by an unknown route. What is its displacement? What can you conclude about its distance travelled?
  4. A learner gives ⟨−1, 2⟩ m as the answer to part (a). Identify which quantity they have calculated.
Discussion of Activity 1.4

(a) Δy = 2 − (−3) = 5 m, so the displacement is ⟨−5, 5⟩ m. Check: 4 − 5 = −1 and −3 + 5 = 2. (b) E→F is ⟨2 − (−3), −4 − (−2)⟩ m = ⟨5, −2⟩ m: rightwards and downwards. F→E is ⟨−5, 2⟩ m, the opposite change. (c) Displacement is ⟨0, 0⟩ m. If it left and returned, it travelled a positive distance, but the endpoints do not give that distance. (d) ⟨−1, 2⟩ m is the position vector of D relative to O, not the displacement from C.

If your signs reverse both correct signs, inspect which endpoint you subtracted. If only one sign is wrong, keep parentheses around the negative coordinate and check that component by reconstructing the finish.

1.5 The same reasoning in three dimensions

A floor plan cannot distinguish two objects directly above one another. They share horizontal location, but differ in height. To describe the space in a room, add a third coordinate axis, z, perpendicular to both x and y. We now use an ordered triple (x, y, z).

In the room model here, x and y run along two perpendicular floor directions and positive z points upwards. A point with coordinates (2, 1, 3) m is located by moving 2 m along positive x, 1 m along positive y and 3 m upwards. As before, this is a way to locate the point, not a statement about the route of an object.

Reading a three-dimensional sketch requires attention to labels rather than screen directions. Both a floor axis and the vertical axis may slope upwards on the page even though only one represents height. In Figure 1.8, follow the guide labelled 2 m parallel to the x-axis, then the guide labelled 1 m parallel to y, then the 3 m vertical guide. The triple provides the precise information if the picture is difficult to interpret.

1.5.1 Three components, one displacement

A position vector from O to P now has three components, rP = ⟨2, 1, 3⟩ m. For a displacement between two positions, subtract the corresponding coordinates exactly as before, adding the third calculation Δz = zB − zA. No new rule is needed.

Example 1.5 · A sensor above a work surface

Model a sensor as a point. Relative to fixed laboratory axes its initial position is A = (−1, 2, 0) m and its final position is B = (2, 2, 4) m. Find its displacement.

Solution

Hence Δr = ⟨3, 0, 4⟩ m. Its horizontal x-coordinate increases by 3 m, its y-coordinate has no net change, and its height increases by 4 m. Reconstructing B gives −1 + 3 = 2, 2 + 0 = 2 and 0 + 4 = 4.

A zero y-component does not mean that the whole displacement is zero. Nor does it prove that the sensor’s y-coordinate stayed constant throughout the journey: it could have moved in that direction and returned before reaching B.

Key idea 1.5 · Zero in one component

A zero displacement component means that the initial and final coordinates agree in that direction. The entire displacement is zero only when all components are zero. Endpoint information alone does not describe the intervening motion.

Discussion of Activity 1.5

(a) The changes are ⟨−3, 2, 0⟩ m. Adding them back coordinate by coordinate gives (1 − 3, −2 + 2, 3 + 0) = (−2, 0, 3). (b) D minus C gives ⟨1 − (−2), −3 − 1, 2 − (−1)⟩ m = ⟨3, −4, 3⟩ m: a net increase of 3 m in x, decrease of 4 m in y and increase of 3 m in z. (c) Subtracting E from F gives ⟨−1 − 0, 4 − 4, −2 − 2⟩ m = ⟨−1, 0, −4⟩ m. The y-coordinate agrees at the endpoints, but motion away and back is possible. In particular, zero net change is not the same assertion as no motion at any time.

1.6 Choosing a different origin

Return to the inspection carriage. Its start and finish are fixed marks on the floor. We placed the origin at O, but another observer might choose a convenient corner of the room instead. Would that choice alter the displacement we calculated?

Moving the origin changes the numbers used to locate each mark. It does not move the marks themselves. To see exactly what changes, keep the directions of the axes and the metre scale the same. Only their zero point will move. This restriction matters: rotating the axes would change which directions the components describe.

1.6.1 Relabelling the same points

Let the new origin O′ be at (1, −2) in the old coordinates. The mark ′ is read “prime”: O′ means “O prime”. It distinguishes the new origin from the old one; here the prime is simply a label for the new system. We will likewise use x′ and y′ for coordinates measured from O′.

The new zero is one metre to the right and two metres below the old zero. To locate a point from there, we must remove those offsets from its old coordinates. Thus

x′ = x − 1,
y′ = y − (−2) = y + 2.

The second line is worth thinking through. Because the new origin is lower down, a fixed point has a larger vertical coordinate when measured from it. The plus sign follows from the geometry as well as from the subtraction rule.

Example 1.6 · New coordinates, same displacement

The carriage starts at A = (−2, 1) and finishes at B = (3, −1), in metres. Find the coordinates relative to O′ = (1, −2), then calculate the displacement using those new coordinates.

Solution

Apply the same change of origin to both points:

Two descriptions of the same floor marks · metres
PointOld (x, y)New (x′, y′)
A(−2, 1)(−3, 3)
B(3, −1)(2, 1)
O′(1, −2)(0, 0)

For example, A’s new coordinates are (−2 − 1, 1 − (−2)) = (−3, 3). For the displacement, take final minus initial in the new system:

Δx′ = 2 − (−3) = 5 m,
Δy′ = 1 − 3 = −2 m.

We recover ⟨5, −2⟩ m. A’s and B’s position vectors have changed because they now begin at O′, but the arrow from A to B has not changed at all.

1.6.2 Why the offset cancels

This agreement is not a coincidence caused by our choice of numbers. Suppose the new origin has old coordinates (cx, cy). The letter c simply labels the fixed offsets; its subscripts identify their directions. Then x′ = x − cx for every point. The new horizontal displacement is

Δx′ = (xB − cx) − (xA − cx)
= xB − cx − xA + cx
= xB − xA = Δx.

The two occurrences of cx cancel because the same offset was subtracted from both endpoint coordinates. The vertical calculation works in the same way; in three dimensions, so does the calculation for z. Changing the zero point therefore changes positions while preserving their differences.

Key idea 1.6 · Keep the coordinate system consistent

A fixed shift of origin leaves displacement components unchanged when axis directions and units are preserved. Both endpoints must be expressed in the same system before they are subtracted. This result does not say that components are unchanged by a rotation of the axes.

Activity 1.6 · Moving the zero point

A different pair of floor marks has coordinates A = (−1, 3) and B = (2, −2), in metres. Choose O′ at (−2, 1) in the old system, preserving axis directions and units.

  1. Find the new coordinates of A and B. Start by writing the rule for each coordinate.
  2. Calculate the displacement from A to B in both systems. What agrees, and what changes?
  3. A student subtracts A’s old coordinates from B’s new coordinates. Explain why this calculation cannot give the required displacement.
Hint for Activity 1.6

The new origin is two metres left and one metre above O. A fixed point therefore has a horizontal coordinate two greater and a vertical coordinate one smaller in the new system.

Discussion of Activity 1.6

(a) The rules are x′ = x − (−2) = x + 2 and y′ = y − 1. Thus A becomes (1, 2), and B becomes (4, −3). (b) In the old system, the changes are 2 − (−1) = 3 m and −2 − 3 = −5 m. In the new system, they are 4 − 1 = 3 m and −3 − 2 = −5 m. Both give ⟨3, −5⟩ m, although the position vectors have different components. (c) The mixed calculation gives ⟨4 − (−1), −3 − 3⟩ = ⟨5, −6⟩ m. The offsets cannot cancel when only one endpoint has been relabelled. Convert both endpoints to one system first.

The freedom to choose an origin is useful. We often put it at a convenient starting point, reducing one position vector to zero. What matters is recording the choice and using it consistently.

1.7 Bringing the descriptions together

We began with signed positions along a passage. Adding a second, and then a third, coordinate allowed us to locate points on a surface and in a room. An arrow supplied another piece of information: the place from which a directed change begins. From that distinction came both position vectors and displacement.

Before any calculation, ask what you are trying to describe. The following three questions keep the roles separate.

Three related descriptions
QuestionDescriptionWhat fixes it?
Where is B?A point with coordinates (xB, yB)The chosen origin, axes and units
How is B located from O?Position vector rB = ⟨xB, yB⟩An arrow from O to B
What is the change from A to B?Displacement Δr = rB − rAAn arrow from A to B, independent of the route taken

If A happens to be the origin, its position vector is zero and the displacement to B equals B’s position vector. This useful special case can hide the distinction when we first learn about arrows. With a starting point elsewhere, the initial coordinates must be subtracted.

(c) First subtract the new origin’s old coordinates from each point. Only then form the difference between the new endpoint coordinates.

Discussion of Activity 1.7

(a) B’s position vector is ⟨1, −1⟩ m. The forward displacement is ⟨1 − (−4), −1 − 2⟩ = ⟨5, −3⟩ m: five metres right and three down. Adding these changes to A gives (−4 + 5, 2 − 3) = (1, −1), as required. Reversing the endpoints gives ⟨−5, 3⟩ m. If you used B’s coordinates for both answers, revisit Section 1.3 and mark the two different tails on your sketch.

(b) Subtract C from D, coordinate by coordinate: ⟨−1 − 2, −1 − (−1), 0 − 4⟩ = ⟨−3, 0, −4⟩ m. The y-coordinate is the same at the endpoints. It could have changed during the journey and returned to −1 m; the endpoints alone cannot tell us. See Section 1.5 if zero components still seem puzzling.

(c) The new coordinates are A: (−4 − 2, 2 − 1) = (−6, 1), and B: (1 − 2, −1 − 1) = (−1, −2). Their difference is ⟨−1 − (−6), −2 − 1⟩ = ⟨5, −3⟩ m, just as before. A detour cannot change the displacement for these fixed endpoints, although it can change the distance travelled. If your result changed with the origin, use Section 1.6 to check whether both points were converted to the same system.

Before moving on

You should now be able to plot a point, distinguish its position vector from an arrow beginning elsewhere, and calculate a displacement in two or three dimensions. A sign check and a reconstruction of the final position give two practical ways of catching errors. You should also be able to explain why moving the zero point leaves a displacement unchanged under the conditions we used.

If one of those steps is uncertain, return to the relevant activity and make a fresh sketch before rereading its discussion. Fluency develops by moving between the picture, the numbers and their meaning; it does not require remembering each example’s answer.

In Lesson 2 we will combine directed changes and investigate what happens when a vector is multiplied by a number. Two successive displacements will provide a natural starting point. Later, the unit’s exercise set will bring together the methods from all five lessons, giving you more practice choosing an approach for yourself.
