Lesson 4
Resolving and reconstructing components

Imagine giving directions across an open field. You could specify how far to move east and how far to move north, or you could point in a direction and give a distance. Both instructions can lead to the same place. What changes is the information made explicit: the first separates the movement along two axes; the second separates its length from its direction.

Lesson 3 supplied part of the translation. From components we can calculate a magnitude, and by normalising we can retain direction in a unit vector. Here we give a direction in a plane another description: an angle. Trigonometry will connect that angle to the unit vector’s components. We can then turn a length and an angle into components, combine vectors in that form, and interpret the result as a length and direction again.

The calculations themselves are often short. The decisions around them deserve more attention. Where does an angle begin? In which sense is it measured? Which component should be negative? Does a calculator’s inverse tangent point towards our destination or in exactly the opposite direction? We will use sketches and simple checks to answer these questions before trusting a numerical result.

Allow about four hours, including the activities, worked examples and checking your attempts. You need paper, a pencil and a calculator with trigonometric functions. We will retrieve the necessary right-triangle ideas and angle units; there is no programming. The interactive figure is an aid to exploration, with a paper alternative carrying the same learning. The full-unit exercise set follows in Lesson 5.

1.22 Giving an angle meaning

“Move five metres at thirty degrees” is an incomplete instruction. An angle measures a turn from one direction to another, so we need to know the starting direction and the sense of the turn. In this lesson our default is the standard direction angle: begin at the positive x-axis and turn anticlockwise to the vector. Imagine the arrow’s tail at the origin while making this measurement. Translating the arrow without rotating it does not alter its direction angle.

One complete turn is 360°. Consequently an angle of 120° and one of 480° leave an arrow pointing the same way. A negative angle records a clockwise turn: −240° also ends at the direction shown in Figure 1.25. These angles describe different amounts of rotation but the same final direction. When reporting a direction here, we normally choose one representative angle from 0° inclusive to 360° exclusive. That keeps the positive x-direction at 0°, rather than giving it both 0° and 360°.

Key idea 1.19 · State the angle convention

Unless another convention is stated, a direction angle is measured anticlockwise from the positive x-axis. Report it in the range 0∘≤θ<360∘. Coterminal angles differ by whole turns and have the same final direction.

An angle without a reference direction and a sense of measurement cannot unambiguously specify a vector’s direction.

1.22.1 Degrees and radians

Degrees divide a turn into 360 equal parts. There is another useful way to measure a turn, built from the geometry of a circle. Draw a circular arc of length s on a circle of radius r. Its angle in radians is the ratio of those two lengths:

θrad=sr.

The lengths must use the same unit, so their units cancel. A larger circle gives a proportionately longer arc for the same turn, leaving the ratio unchanged. A complete circumference has length 2πr, so one complete turn is 2π radians. In particular, a half-turn is π radians or 180°. The symbol π denotes the circle constant, approximately 3.14159.

To change the numerical measure from degrees to radians, multiply by π/180; to go the other way, multiply by 180/π. The turn has not changed, only the scale used to describe it. Radians will become especially useful when we study changing quantities later in the module. For now, the essential habit is to match the calculator’s angle mode to the units of the input.

Example 1.21 · One direction in two angle units

Express 150° in radians. Then find a standard direction angle equivalent to −π/3 radians.

Solution

A half-turn is 180° or π radians, so 150° is five-sixths of a half-turn:

150×π180=5π6.

For the second angle, −π/3 radians means a clockwise turn of 60°. Add a complete turn to select the anticlockwise representative:

−π3+2π=5π3.

This is 300°. A sketch places it below the positive x-axis, exactly where the clockwise 60° turn ended.

Check your calculator before beginning: in degree mode, sin30∘=0.5; in radian mode, entering π/6 into sine should also give 0.5. Typing 30 in radian mode asks about a different turn. The resulting number may look plausible, so a sensible-looking answer alone does not establish that the mode was correct. In this lesson degree inputs carry the ° symbol; radian angles are identified in the surrounding text.

We now have a precise language for direction. The next task is to connect a turn to the signed changes along the coordinate axes.

1.23 From a triangle to signed components

Begin with a vector pointing up and to the right, making an acute angle θ with the positive x-axis. An acute angle lies between 0° and 90°. Drop a perpendicular from the arrow’s head to the x-axis. The arrow becomes the hypotenuse of a right triangle; the horizontal side is adjacent to the angle, and the vertical side is opposite it.

Recall the right-triangle ratios:

cosθ=adjacenthypotenuse,
sinθ=oppositehypotenuse.

For a fixed acute angle, every such triangle has the same shape. Enlarging it multiplies each side length by the same factor, leaving these ratios unchanged. This is why knowing the angle and the hypotenuse is enough to determine the other two lengths.

Let the vector’s magnitude be L. In this first-quadrant triangle the components are positive, so their values coincide with the side lengths. Multiplying each ratio by the hypotenuse gives

vx=Lcosθ,vy=Lsinθ.

The trigonometric ratios are dimensionless. If L is a length in centimetres, both components are in centimetres too. Resolving the vector means expressing it as the sum of these axis-directed contributions; it does not mean that the object must actually follow the two triangle legs.

Example 1.22 · A length and an acute angle

Find the components of a displacement of magnitude 10 cm at a standard direction angle of 30°. Give an exact answer and a useful decimal approximation.

Solution

Both components must be positive. Since the arrow lies nearer to the positive x-axis than to the positive y-axis, the horizontal component should be larger. Using cos30∘=3/2 and sin30∘=1/2,

dx=1032cm=53cm,
dy=1012cm=5cm.

Thus d=⟨53,5⟩cm, approximately ⟨8.66,5.00⟩cm. Reconstructing the magnitude from the exact components gives

|d|=(53)2+52cm
=75+25cm=10cm.

This verifies the length. The positive signs and the larger horizontal component provide additional checks on the direction; a magnitude check alone would not detect a sign reversal.

1.23.1 Extending the idea beyond an acute angle

A vector can point left or down. The side lengths in a right triangle are still nonnegative, but components can now be negative. To avoid forcing an obtuse angle into an acute-angle triangle, imagine a unit vector turning around the origin. Its head moves on the unit circle, the circle of radius one.

For any direction angle θ, cosine is the horizontal coordinate of that unit-circle point and sine is its vertical coordinate. In the first quadrant this agrees with the triangle ratios: the hypotenuse is one. Elsewhere it extends them while retaining the coordinate signs. In the upper-left quadrant cosine is negative and sine positive; in the lower-left both are negative; in the lower-right cosine is positive and sine negative.

Our direction unit vector is therefore u=⟨cosθ,sinθ⟩. Lesson 3 showed how to reconstruct a vector by multiplying its unit direction by its magnitude. The same component formula consequently works in every quadrant, not just the one in Figure 1.26.

Key idea 1.20 · Resolving a plane vector

For magnitude L≥0 and an angle measured anticlockwise from positive x,

v=L⟨cosθ,sinθ⟩.

The components inherit the signs of cosine and sine. If L is zero, the result is the zero vector for every angle; the zero vector itself has no direction.

There is also a useful identity hiding in the unit-circle picture. Applying Pythagoras to a unit vector gives cos2θ+sin2θ=1, where cos2θ means (cosθ)2. It follows that the reconstructed vector has magnitude L:

L2cos2θ+L2sin2θ=L2=L.

The last equality uses the fact that a magnitude is nonnegative. This is the general reason the check in Example 1.22 worked.

Example 1.23 · Resolving into the upper-left quadrant

A displacement has magnitude 6 m and direction angle 135°. Find its exact components.

Solution

The arrow points left and up, so we expect a negative x-component and a positive y-component. Its direction lies 45° above the negative x-axis. The associated right triangle therefore has equal legs. A right triangle with hypotenuse one and equal legs has leg length 1/2=2/2, by Pythagoras. Reflecting the first-quadrant 45° direction into the upper-left changes only the horizontal sign. Hence

cos135∘=−22,
sin135∘=22,
d=⟨−32,32⟩m.

Each component has magnitude approximately 4.24 m. The arrow is 6 m long, not 62 m: its two components must be combined using the magnitude formula, not added as positive lengths.

1.24 Turning a vector through a circle

Keep a vector’s magnitude fixed and turn it slowly anticlockwise. Its head travels around a circle, but its two components do not stay fixed. Starting along positive x, the horizontal component equals the full magnitude and the vertical component is zero. As the arrow turns towards positive y, its horizontal reach decreases while its vertical reach increases. At 90° their roles have exchanged.

Continuing the turn brings the head into the upper-left quadrant. The vertical component remains positive, but the horizontal component becomes negative. Crossing an axis does not make a component jump: it passes smoothly through zero. The sign records which side of the relevant axis the head occupies.

The components can both change while the magnitude stays the same. This is a useful distinction to make before we meet physical quantities that vary with time: variation in components can reflect a changing direction, not just a changing size. Here the control changes a direction you choose; it is not simulating a moving object or imposing a physical law.

Key idea 1.21 · Checks while resolving

Predict the signs from the quadrant. Check that neither component’s absolute value exceeds the magnitude. Finally, check the sum of the squared components:

vx2+vy2=L2.

These checks catch different mistakes. A correct squared sum does not establish the signs or distinguish swapped components.

1.25 Recovering direction from components

We now reverse the problem. Suppose you know that a displacement goes 4 m left and 7 m up. Its components tell us immediately that its direction is in the upper-left quadrant. Its magnitude follows from Lesson 3. To find a direction angle, complete the right triangle and first calculate its small, acute angle with the nearest horizontal direction.

The tangent ratio for an acute angle is opposite divided by adjacent. If neither component is zero, let α be the acute angle between the arrow and the horizontal axis on its own side of the origin. Then

tanα=|vy||vx|.

We use absolute values here because the triangle’s side lengths are positive. The angle itself is found with the inverse tangent, written arctan or tan−1. It answers the question “which angle has this tangent?” In this notation the superscript −1 means an inverse function, not a reciprocal. The reciprocal of tanα would instead be 1/tanα.

Example 1.24 · Components to a length and direction

Find the magnitude and standard direction angle of d=⟨−4,7⟩m. Give the angle to one decimal place.

Solution

The magnitude is

|d|=(−4)2+72m=65m.

The arrow points left and up. Its acute angle with the negative x-direction is

α=arctan(74)≈60.255∘.

The standard direction angle stops short of a half-turn by this acute angle, so

θ=180∘−α≈119.7∘.

The angle is between 90° and 180°, as the signs require. Its magnitude is approximately 8.06 m, larger than either component’s absolute value. If we reconstruct using the unrounded angle and 65, we recover −4 m and 7 m. Small discrepancies after rounding are expected; keep extra digits during the calculation.

1.25.1 Why the calculator needs your sketch

It is tempting to enter the signed ratio vy/vx directly into inverse tangent and accept whatever comes back. Most calculators return an inverse-tangent angle between −90° and 90°. For the vector in Example 1.24 they return approximately −60.3°. That is a direction in the lower-right quadrant, exactly opposite to the required one.

There is no calculator fault here. Reversing both component signs leaves their ratio unchanged, so tangent cannot distinguish opposite directions. The calculator must choose one answer from the many angles sharing that ratio. We must use the original signs to recover the direction in the question. Finding a positive acute reference angle first makes that decision visible.

Key idea 1.22 · Recover a direction with its quadrant

When both components are nonzero, calculate the acute angle

α=arctan(|vy||vx|).

Read axis directions directly: 0°, 90°, 180° or 270°. The zero vector has no direction angle.

The table is a compact record of four sketches, not a substitute for understanding them. In the upper-left quadrant you approach the negative x-axis from above; in the lower-left you pass that axis before reaching the arrow. In the lower-right you stop short of a complete turn. The acute reference angle is always the angle to the horizontal, not sometimes to the vertical.

If vx=0, the tangent ratio would require division by zero, but the geometry is simpler: a nonzero vector is straight up or down. If vy=0, it is straight right or left. If both are zero, no amount of calculator manipulation supplies a direction. Some software provides a two-input function called atan2 to use both component signs; its argument order and angle range must still be checked. We do not need it here, and it does not give the zero vector an intrinsic direction.

1.26 The same direction, another convention

The component formulas are attached to an angle convention. They do not promise that every number called an angle goes beside cosine for the x-component. Suppose an angle is measured from north instead of east. The triangle side adjacent to that angle is now the northward side, so the roles of sine and cosine change.

For a local map in this section, take positive x east and positive y north. A bearing β is measured clockwise from north, usually written with three digits from 000° through values below 360°. Thus east is bearing 090°, south is 180° and west is 270°. We treat the small region as a flat plane; these calculations are not a navigation method for long distances over the curved Earth. Here “north” means the north direction chosen for the map, without introducing a separate magnetic-compass correction.

There are two reliable approaches. You can convert the bearing into a standard angle and use the established component formulas, or sketch the bearing and identify opposite and adjacent sides directly. Either approach begins by fixing the reference direction. From the quarter-turn between east and north, the angle conversion is

θ=90∘−β,

up to adding or subtracting whole turns.

For example, a bearing of 300° gives θ=−210∘, equivalent to 150° after adding 360°. The negative result is not an error; it is a clockwise representative of the same direction.

Example 1.25 · Resolving a bearing

A displacement is 8 m on a bearing of 300°. Find its east and north components.

Solution

The bearing points northwest: the east component must be negative and the north component positive. Its standard angle is 150°. The exact values give

dx=8cos150∘m=−43m,
dy=8sin150∘m=4m.

So the displacement is ⟨−43,4⟩m. Alternatively, the arrow lies 60° west of north. The northward leg is adjacent to that acute angle and has length 8cos60∘=4 m; the westward leg is opposite and has length 8sin60∘=43 m. Assigning east as positive makes that second contribution negative, giving the same result.

Key idea 1.23 · Components from a bearing

With positive x east, positive y north, and bearing β measured clockwise from north,

vx=Lsinβ,
vy=Lcosβ.

At bearing 000° the whole vector points north; at 090° it points east. These checks explain which function belongs to which component. A differently defined angle requires its own sketch or conversion.

Descriptions such as “25° west of north” are also common. Start from north, then turn 25° towards west. Its standard angle is 115°, and its bearing is 335°. In contrast, “25° north of west” begins at west and turns towards north, giving standard angle 155°. The order of the words matters. Before calculating, draw the stated reference direction and mark the turn next to it.

A wider view · optional context

Angles as observations

The word sextant may suggest navigation at sea, but optical angle measurements also had a place in the Apollo programme. This photographed instrument flew on the uncrewed AS-202 test in 1966. The Apollo guidance system used observations of stars and landmarks alongside other information; an angle measurement was part of a larger calculation, not a position supplied by itself.

The distinction is already visible in our simple geometry. A direction angle identifies a ray from a starting point. Infinitely many possible destinations lie on that ray. To select one by the methods in this lesson, we also need a distance. Real navigation has additional information, instruments and uncertainties; our plane model isolates the geometrical relationship without claiming to reproduce the complete procedure.

1.27 Combining descriptions

We can now choose the description suited to each part of a problem. A magnitude and angle may be convenient for giving an instruction. Components are convenient for combining several instructions. A magnitude and direction may then be the clearest way to describe the result. Moving between these descriptions is a method, not a change in the physical displacement.

Consider two consecutive straight displacements. There is no general rule that says their direction angles should be added or averaged. Nor is their resultant magnitude generally the sum of their magnitudes. Resolve each displacement in the same coordinate frame, add its signed components, then reconstruct the resultant. If one direction is a bearing and another a standard angle, reconcile their conventions first.

Example 1.26 · A route described by lengths and angles

A small survey platform makes a 4 m displacement at standard angle 30°, followed by a 3 m displacement at 120°. Find its resultant displacement and its direction. Treat the surface as a plane and ignore any details of how the platform turns.

Solution

The first stage points northeast; the second points northwest. With the same eastward and northward axes for both, their components are

a=⟨23,2⟩m,
b=⟨−32,332⟩m.

Add horizontal to horizontal and vertical to vertical:

rx=(23−32)m,
ry=(2+332)m.

Numerically this is approximately ⟨1.9641,4.5981⟩m. Both components are positive, so the resultant points northeast. Its magnitude can be found by squaring these components. Keeping their exact forms makes the cancellation visible:

(23−32)2=12−63+94,
(2+332)2=4+63+274.

The sum is 25, giving |r|=5m. The two stages are perpendicular because their direction angles differ by 90°, so a 3–4–5 right triangle gives an independent geometrical check. For the direction, both resultant components are positive:

θ=arctan(2+33223−32)≈66.9∘.

The endpoint lies 5 m away at approximately 66.9°, although the two-stage route is 7 m long. The angle is neither 150°, the sum of the stage angles, nor 75°, their simple average. It reflects both stage lengths as well as their directions.

The return displacement is ⟨−4,−23⟩m, with the same magnitude and bearing 229.1°. Adding outward and return components gives the zero vector, which checks the return.

1.27.1 What changes in three dimensions?

The angle description developed here belongs to a plane. A single angle around the z-axis cannot tell us whether an arrow also points up or down. We will continue to use three signed components for a vector in space; we are not introducing a new system of spatial angles in this lesson.

Nevertheless, a plane calculation can form part of a three-dimensional one. If a horizontal displacement and a separate vertical displacement are specified, resolve the horizontal part in its plane and retain the supplied vertical component. Be careful about which length belongs in the trigonometric formulas: a horizontal length is not automatically the magnitude of the whole three-dimensional vector.

Example 1.27 · A horizontal direction and a vertical change

A displacement has a horizontal projection of length 4 m at standard angle 60° in the xy-plane, together with a vertical change of −3 m. Take positive z upwards. Find the full vector, its magnitude and its unit direction.

Solution

Resolve the 4 m horizontal projection, then attach the separately specified vertical component:

d=⟨2,23,−3⟩m.

The horizontal length checks as 22+(23)2=4. For the full vector, include the vertical contribution:

|d|=42+(−3)2m=5m.

Dividing the full vector by its magnitude gives the dimensionless direction

u=⟨25,235,−35⟩.

Its squared components sum to one. Using 5 m in place of the given 4 m horizontal length would enlarge the horizontal components and describe a different displacement. The 60° angle specifies only the horizontal projection’s direction.

Activity 1.31 · Keep the plane and the space distinct

A displacement has horizontal projection length 6 cm at standard angle 150° and an upward vertical change of 8 cm.

  1. Find its exact three components, full magnitude and unit direction.
  2. Write a second displacement with the same horizontal projection but a downward change of 8 cm. Which results from part (a) change?
  3. Someone supplies only “magnitude 10 cm and horizontal direction 150°”. Does this uniquely specify a three-dimensional displacement? Use your answers to explain.
Hint

The horizontal components follow from the 6 cm projection. The sign of the vertical component changes between parts (a) and (b), but its square does not.

Discussion

(a) The displacement is ⟨−33,3,8⟩cm. Its magnitude is 27+9+64cm=10cm, and its unit direction is

⟨−3310,310,45⟩.

(b) Reverse only the final component, giving ⟨−33,3,−8⟩cm. The magnitude remains 10 cm; the vertical component of the unit direction becomes −4/5. (c) No. These two distinct vectors already satisfy the supplied description. Other divisions between horizontal and vertical lengths are possible too. A spatial direction requires more information than its horizontal angle.

Key idea 1.24 · A dependable conversion sequence

State the axes and angle convention; sketch and predict signs; resolve each vector in the same frame; combine components if needed; reconstruct magnitude and direction with an explicit quadrant or axis check. Keep extra digits until the final answer and report units and the angle convention with the result.

We have now completed a useful circuit. Components gave us a way to calculate with directed changes. Magnitude and unit vectors separated size from direction. Angles supplied another description of direction in a plane, and trigonometry connected that description back to components. Choosing between these forms is part of mathematical judgement: use the one that makes the next step clear, and check the translation when you change forms.

Lesson 5 will bring the unit together. Its mixed exercises will ask you to select methods across all four teaching lessons, rather than being told which conversion to use. Before moving on, check that you can explain why inverse tangent needs a quadrant check, why a bearing changes the sine–cosine assignment, and why the zero vector has no direction to recover.
