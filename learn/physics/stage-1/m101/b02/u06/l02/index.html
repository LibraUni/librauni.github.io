M101 · Unit 6 · Lesson 2

Concavity and extrema

A graph can be falling while its slope rises. Following the slope itself reveals the bend of the original curve.

In Lesson 1 we used signs of the first derivative to turn a collection of tangent slopes into intervals of increase and decrease. The second derivative asks how those slopes change. It will help us classify some stationary points, but it does not remove the need to examine the domain or compare endpoint values.

Four hours: 80 minutes reading/examples, 90 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback and correction, and 30 minutes reserved for the unit exercises.

6.6 How the slope changes

Compare x2 and x for positive x. Both increase. For the square, the derivative 2x increases as x increases: successive tangent lines become steeper. For the square root, the derivative 1/(2x) decreases: successive tangent lines become flatter even while the function rises. “Increasing” therefore leaves an important part of the graph's behaviour unspecified.

We call a differentiable graph concave up on an interval when its derivative is increasing there, and concave down when its derivative is decreasing. Imagine reading the tangent slopes from left to right. If the numbers grow, the graph bends upwards. They may grow from −5 to −2, from −2 to 1, or from 1 to 4; all three are increases in slope. Concavity concerns this ordering, not whether the tangent currently slopes upwards or downwards.

Key idea 6.4 · Apply the sign test to the slope

Where f″ exists throughout an interval, f″>0 gives concavity up and f″<0 gives concavity down. The first derivative tells whether f rises or falls; the second tells whether its slope rises or falls. A zero second derivative at an isolated input does not settle either neighbouring concavity.

Example 6.5 · Falling and bending upwards

Describe f(x)=e−x on the real line using both derivatives.

Working. We have f′=−e−x<0 and f″=e−x>0. The function decreases and is concave up. Its negative slopes become less negative as x increases: for instance, the slope goes from −1 at zero to approximately −0.368 at 1. The size of the downward slope becomes smaller while the signed slope becomes larger. Saying merely “the rate is slowing” can hide that distinction; say which signed quantity is increasing or decreasing.

For one-dimensional motion with nonzero velocity, speed is |v|. If v<0, then |v|=−v, so increasing velocity can mean decreasing speed. Concavity up of a position-time graph means positive acceleration; it does not automatically mean speeding up. Direction must be considered as well.

A wider view · Motion has a signed rate

Activity 6.6 · Separate the two signs (15 minutes)

Classify increase/decrease and concavity for (a) lnx on x>0; (b) −ex on the real line; (c) x2 on x<0 and on x>0. Then interpret a one-dimensional motion interval with v<0 and a<0: is position increasing, and is speed increasing?

Discussion of Activity 6.6

(a) f′=1/x>0, f″=−1/x2<0: increasing, concave down. (b) Both derivatives are −ex<0: decreasing, concave down. (c) The first derivative 2x changes sign at zero, but the second is 2 everywhere: decreasing then increasing, concave up on both sides. For the motion, position decreases because velocity is negative; speed increases because d|v|/dt=−a>0. More negative velocity means greater speed in this case.

6.7 Inflection requires a change of concavity

A curve can change from concave down to concave up without turning around. For f(x)=x3, the second derivative is 6x. It is negative before zero and positive afterwards, while the function itself increases through zero. The origin is a point of inflection: a point on a continuous graph where the concavity changes between the neighbouring intervals.

To find candidates, look for inputs where f″=0 or where f″ fails to exist. Then check for an actual sign change on the two sides, and check that the function is defined and continuous at the proposed point. A candidate equation is not a classification. The graph of x4 has f″(0)=0, but 12x2 is positive on both sides. There is no inflection at zero.

Nor need an inflection have a horizontal tangent. For x3+x, the second derivative is still 6x, but the first derivative is 3x2+1 and equals 1 at zero. The concavity changes while the function is already rising at a nonzero rate. “Stationary” and “inflection” describe different properties and can occur together or separately.

Example 6.6 · A complete concavity analysis

Find the intervals of concavity and inflection points of f(x)=x4−4x3.

Working. Differentiating twice gives f′=4x3−12x2 and f″=12x2−24x=12x(x−2). The polynomial is continuous and twice differentiable everywhere. Its second derivative is positive for x<0, negative for 0<x<2, and positive for x>2. Both candidate inputs therefore give genuine changes of concavity. Their points are (0,0) and (2,−16).

At zero, f′=0, so the first inflection is stationary. At 2, f′=−16, so the second is not. Neither inference required guessing the bending from a plotted window. We also have not claimed that either point is an extremum; that question concerns first-derivative signs.

Activity 6.7 · Candidate or conclusion? (20 minutes)

For each function, investigate the candidate at zero: (a) x5; (b) x6; (c) 1/x. State the second derivative, concavity on both sides and whether an inflection point exists. For (a) and (b), also decide whether zero is a stationary point and whether it is an extremum.

Discussion of Activity 6.7

(a) f″=20x3 changes from negative to positive. The continuous graph has an inflection at (0,0); f′=5x4 vanishes there but remains positive on both sides, so it is stationary without an extremum. (b) f″=30x4 is positive on both sides, so there is no inflection. Since f′=6x5 changes from negative to positive, zero is a stationary strict minimum. (c) f″=2/x3 is negative to the left and positive to the right, but the function is undefined at zero. Separate branches of a graph do not make an inflection point at a missing input.

6.8 The second-derivative test

Suppose a stationary point lies in a region where the first derivative is increasing. The slope passes through zero from negative to positive, producing a minimum. If the derivative is decreasing through zero, the change is from positive to negative, producing a maximum. This gives a useful shortcut when the second derivative at a stationary point is nonzero.

Key idea 6.5 · A sufficient test at a stationary point

Suppose f is differentiable on a neighbourhood of c, f′(c)=0, and f″(c) exists. If f″(c)>0, there is a strict local minimum at c; if f″(c)<0, there is a strict local maximum. If f″(c)=0, this test is inconclusive. Use neighbouring first-derivative signs or compare function values.

The condition at the point is enough for this test, even without separately assuming continuity of f″. To see the reason in the positive case, use the definition f″(c)=limh→0[f′(c+h)−f′(c)]/h. A positive limit makes the quotient positive for sufficiently small nonzero h. Because f′(c)=0, the numerator f′(c+h) is negative for negative h and positive for positive h. This recovers the first-derivative classification. We will not need a general theory of irregular second derivatives, but the argument identifies what the shortcut actually uses.

Example 6.7 · Choose the cheaper test

Classify the stationary point of r(x)=xe−x on x>0.

Working. The product rule gives r′=e−x(1−x), so the only stationary input is 1. A second differentiation gives r″=e−x(x−2). At 1 this equals −e−1<0, proving a strict local maximum at (1,e−1). Here the first derivative also gives a quick independent check: the exponential is positive, while 1−x changes from positive to negative. Either test is sufficient. Computing a complicated second derivative is unnecessary if a simple sign factorisation already settles the question.

Activity 6.8 · An inconclusive test is not “neither” (15 minutes)

Use the second-derivative test where possible for a(x)=x2−6x+4. At zero, compare the outcomes for b(x)=x4, c(x)=−x4 and d(x)=x3. Explain what you must write when the test gives zero.

Discussion of Activity 6.8

For a, a′=2x−6 vanishes at 3 and a″=2>0, giving a strict local minimum at (3,−5). For the other three functions, both the first and second derivatives vanish at zero. The test is inconclusive in all three cases. Comparing values or derivative signs gives a strict minimum for x4, a strict maximum for −x4, and neither for x3. “Inconclusive” describes the test's information, not the function's behaviour.

6.9 Absolute extrema on a closed interval

A local maximum is the highest point in a small neighbourhood. It may be lower than a distant part of the graph or an endpoint. To find the greatest and least values on a specified closed interval, we need a guarantee that those values are attained, a complete candidate list and a comparison of the original function's values.

Key idea 6.6 · Existence, candidates, comparison

A function continuous on a closed bounded interval [a,b] attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum there (the extreme value theorem). Find all interior critical inputs, include a and b, and compare the values of f at every candidate. For the finite candidate lists in this unit, the largest and smallest values settle the question. Report both the value and every input where it occurs.

This result does not require differentiability everywhere. A corner can be an interior critical input and must be included. Nor does it say that a stationary point exists: a rising straight line reaches its extrema at the endpoints. The theorem guarantees the existence of extreme values; differentiation helps locate the candidates. We use the theorem without a proof from real analysis.

Example 6.8 · An endpoint wins the comparison

Find the absolute extrema of f(x)=x3−3x on [−2,3].

Working. This polynomial is continuous on the closed interval. Its derivative 3(x−1)(x+1) vanishes at −1 and 1; no interior derivatives are missing. Evaluate the original function:

The absolute maximum is 18 at x=3, not the local maximum 2 at x=−1. The absolute minimum is −2, attained twice, at x=−2 and x=1. There is no need to apply a second-derivative test to make this absolute comparison; the complete list and function values suffice.

The hypotheses explain when this guarantee is unavailable. On (0,1), f(x)=x has neither a greatest nor a least value: every allowed output can be exceeded and undercut by another allowed output. On [0,1) it has a minimum but no maximum. A missing endpoint cannot be inserted into a comparison table as if it belonged to the domain. A limit approached at a missing endpoint may bound the values without being attained.

Activity 6.9 · Make the candidate list complete (25 minutes)

Find the absolute extrema of (a) x2−4x+1 on [0,5]; (b) |x−1| on [−1,4]; (c) 1/x on [1,4]. State the existence guarantee, candidates, values and locations. Then decide which conclusions in (a) survive if the domain is changed to (0,5).

Discussion of Activity 6.9

Each function is continuous on its original closed bounded interval. (a) Candidates 0, 2, 5 give values 1, −3, 6. Minimum −3 at 2; maximum 6 at 5. (b) Include the corner at 1 along with endpoints −1 and 4; values 2, 0, 3 give minimum 0 at 1 and maximum 3 at 4. (c) The derivative −1/x2 never vanishes on the interval, so compare endpoints: maximum 1 at 1, minimum 1/4 at 4. On the open interval in (a), the minimum remains −3 at 2, but the maximum disappears: 6 is approached as x→5− and never attained. The theorem is unavailable on that open interval, but direct reasoning still decides the answer.

6.10 Choosing the right conclusion

Three questions can now look superficially similar: “Where is the function stationary?”, “Where does it have a local maximum?” and “What is its greatest allowed value?” The first asks for zeros of the derivative. The second asks about comparisons near candidates. The third asks about the entire domain. Before differentiating, decide which question was asked.

Example 6.9 · A domain belongs to the model

A supplied response model is R(t)=te−t/2 for numerical time t in seconds, valid only for 0≤t≤6. The numerical response is measured in a fixed response unit. Find its largest value during the model's validity period.

Working. The model is continuous on its stated closed interval. Differentiate: R′=e−t/2(1−t/2). The only interior stationary input is 2. Endpoint and stationary values are R(0)=0, R(2)=2/e≈0.736 and R(6)=6/e3≈0.299. The greatest modelled response is 2/e response units at 2 s. This is an absolute claim on [0,6]. It says nothing about the instrument after 6 s, where the model was not asserted to apply. The coefficients in the numerical-time formula carry the units required for the exponent to be dimensionless.

Activity 6.10 · Critique an answer (15 minutes)

A solution says: “For f(x)=x3−3x on [−2,3], f′(x)=0 at ±1. Since f″(−1)<0, the maximum is at −1.” Identify exactly which claim is justified and which is not. Rewrite the conclusion, including both the input and output. Then explain why a zero second derivative must never be recorded automatically as an inflection point.

Discussion of Activity 6.10

The second-derivative test justifies a strict local maximum at x=−1, with value 2. It does not compare that value with endpoints; the absolute maximum on the specified domain is 18 at 3, as Example 6.8 shows. A zero second derivative merely identifies a possible concavity change. For x4, f″(0)=0 but the graph is concave up on both sides, so there is no inflection. The missing step is a neighbouring concavity check.

During correction, label each error as a calculation error, an incomplete candidate list or an overstrong conclusion. These need different repairs. You have now connected derivatives to the shape and extreme values of a function on a fixed domain.
