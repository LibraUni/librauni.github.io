M101 · Unit 6 · Lesson 1

Increase, decrease and stationary points

A horizontal tangent gives a candidate to investigate. The behaviour on either side tells us what kind of point it is.

Unit 5 ended with checks on derivative calculations. We now ask what a checked derivative allows us to conclude about its original function. Begin by separating three things that a graph displays together: its height, the sign of its slope and the way that slope changes. This lesson concerns the first two; Lesson 2 will take up the third.

Four hours, including the unit introduction: 80 minutes reading and examples, 90 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback and correction, and 30 minutes reserved for the unit-end exercises. Attempt activities before opening their discussions.

6.1 What increasing means

A function can be negative and increasing. For example, f(x)=x−5 has negative values throughout [0,3], but its outputs rise from −5 to −2 as the input increases. The sign of f tells us which side of the horizontal axis the graph occupies. The sign of f′ tells us the direction of change as we read from left to right. Confusing these two signs would make a function below the axis seem to be falling even when it is rising.

To make “rising” precise, choose any two inputs in the interval, with the second larger than the first. An increasing function gives a larger output at the second input. The word any matters: checking the two ends, or several selected pairs, does not by itself establish what happens between them.

Key idea 6.1 · Compare outputs on a stated interval

In this unit, increasing means strictly increasing: whenever x1<x2 in the interval, f(x1)<f(x2). Decreasing means f(x1)>f(x2). For nondecreasing use f(x1)≤f(x2), allowing equal outputs. A constant function is nondecreasing and nonincreasing, but is neither strictly increasing nor strictly decreasing.

The distinction lets us describe plateaux without ambiguity. A reading that rises, stays constant for a while, then rises again is nondecreasing throughout; it is not strictly increasing on an interval containing two different inputs on the plateau. Either rising or falling behaviour throughout an interval is called monotonic; state which direction and whether equality is allowed.

Example 6.1 · Endpoints do not tell the whole story

For f(x)=x2−2x on [0,3], the ending value is larger than the starting value. Is the function increasing throughout?

Working. The endpoints give f(0)=0 and f(3)=3. But the pair 0<1 gives f(0)=0>−1=f(1). One counterexample disproves the claim about every pair. In fact, completing the square gives f(x)=(x−1)2−1: the function falls towards x=1 and rises afterwards. A positive overall change can contain a period of decrease.

Activity 6.1 · Height or change? (15 minutes)

For each function on the stated interval, decide whether it is positive, negative or changes sign; then decide whether it increases or decreases. Use a comparison of outputs or a known graph: (a) 2x−9 on [1,3]; (b) 6−x on [1,4]; (c) x2 on [−3,−1]. Finally explain why f(1)<f(4) alone cannot establish increase throughout [1,4].

Discussion of Activity 6.1

(a) Values run from −7 to −3: negative and increasing. (b) Values run from 5 to 2: positive and decreasing. (c) As a negative input moves towards zero, its magnitude falls, so its square falls from 9 to 1: positive and decreasing. The final comparison tests only one input pair. A function may dip, rise and dip again while still ending above its initial value; Example 6.1 already shows why a net change is insufficient.

6.2 From a derivative to an interval

At a differentiable point, f′(x) is the tangent slope. If that slope is positive at every interior point of an interval, the graph cannot make a downward excursion while remaining continuous and differentiable there. This is the intuitive connection between derivative signs and monotonicity. Lesson 4 will justify it using the mean value theorem; for now we state the precise result we need.

Key idea 6.2 · The derivative sign test

If f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), then:

  • f′(x)>0 throughout (a,b) implies that f is increasing on [a,b].
  • f′(x)<0 throughout (a,b) implies that f is decreasing on [a,b].
  • f′(x)≥0 throughout gives nondecrease; f′(x)=0 throughout gives a constant function.

On an open interval, apply the same argument to each closed subinterval lying inside it. A derivative's sign at just one input gives no conclusion about a whole interval.

The strict-positive condition is sufficient, but it is not necessary. The function x3 is strictly increasing on the real line even though its derivative 3x2 is zero at zero. To see why, compare two different inputs: cubing preserves their order, including when they lie on opposite sides of zero. A momentary horizontal tangent does not make a flat interval.

Example 6.2 · A positive quantity that falls

Show that q(x)=e−2x is decreasing for all real x.

Working. The exponential and its derivative exist everywhere. The chain rule gives q′(x)=−2e−2x. Since an exponential is positive, multiplying by −2 gives a negative derivative for every real input. Hence q decreases on every interval, and therefore on the whole real line. Its values remain positive; decreasing does not mean negative. Adding a constant to q would shift its height without changing this derivative argument.

Activity 6.2 · State enough evidence (15 minutes)

Use derivatives to determine the monotonic behaviour of (a) 3x+7 on the real line; (b) lnx on its domain; (c) x2 on each side of zero. Decide whether “f′(2)=3, therefore f increases on [1,3]” follows. Give a counterexample if it does not.

Discussion of Activity 6.2

(a) Derivative 3 is positive everywhere. (b) Derivative 1/x is positive on x>0, the logarithm's domain. Both increase. (c) 2x is negative for x<0 and positive for x>0, so the square decreases on (−∞,0] and increases on [0,∞), including zero by continuity. For the last claim, take f(x)=3x−4(x−2)2. Then f′(2)=3, but f′(3)=−5 and the function decreases near 3. One positive derivative does not control all inputs.

6.3 Stationary points are candidates

A point on a differentiable graph is stationary when its tangent is horizontal: f′(c)=0. The point is (c,f(c)), so finding the input c is only part of locating it. “Stationary” refers to the output's instantaneous rate of change with its input; it need not describe an object physically at rest unless the function is a position as a function of time.

A local maximum at an interior input c means that f(c) is at least as large as every sufficiently nearby value. A local minimum reverses that comparison. We use this non-strict definition so a flat region can have non-strict local extrema. A strict local maximum is larger than all sufficiently nearby values other than itself. An absolute maximum compares with the entire specified domain; Lesson 2 will make that larger comparison systematically. Here “local extremum” refers to an interior point with neighbours on both sides; endpoints will be considered separately.

Why is zero derivative relevant? At an interior local maximum, the differences f(c+h)−f(c) are nonpositive for sufficiently small positive and negative h. Dividing by positive h gives a nonpositive quotient on the right; dividing by negative h gives a nonnegative quotient on the left. If a single two-sided derivative exists, it must be both nonpositive and nonnegative, so it must be zero. The corresponding argument applies to a minimum. Differentiability and an interior point are essential to this reasoning.

Example 6.3 · Three horizontal tangents, three behaviours

Classify the stationary point at zero for x2, −x2 and x3 using their values or first derivatives.

Working. All three derivatives vanish at zero. For x2, nearby nonzero values are positive and f(0)=0, so zero is a strict minimum. For −x2, nearby values are negative, giving a strict maximum. For x3, negative inputs give negative values and positive inputs give positive values; the function passes through zero while continuing to increase. It has no extremum there. Thus the equation f′(c)=0 finds candidates, not their classification.

Activity 6.3 · Find the point, then interpret it (20 minutes)

Find all stationary points of g(x)=x3−6x2+9x+2. Give both coordinates. Use the signs of g′ just to either side to decide the nature of each point. Explain why solving g(x)=0 would answer a different question.

Hint for Activity 6.3

Factor g′(x) before testing its sign; the critical inputs split the real line into three intervals.

Discussion of Activity 6.3

g′=3x2−12x+9=3(x−1)(x−3), so the stationary inputs are 1 and 3. Values are g(1)=6, g(3)=2. The derivative is positive before 1, negative between 1 and 3, and positive after 3. Therefore (1,6) is a strict local maximum and (3,2) a strict local minimum. Zeros of g would locate crossings of the horizontal axis; their heights would be zero, with no requirement that the tangent be horizontal.

6.4 A sign chart is an argument

A sign chart organises the factors of a derivative over intervals. It is more than a list of sampled values. We must first find every point where a factor can be zero or where the derivative or function is undefined. Between consecutive such points, the simple continuous factors in our examples cannot change sign without passing through zero. That is why one chosen input can identify the sign throughout each interval. Randomly testing a few inputs without finding all the boundaries gives no such assurance.

Example 6.4 · A repeated factor need not change sign

Determine the intervals of increase and decrease and classify all stationary points of F(x)=14x4−23x3.

Working. First factor the derivative: F′(x)=x3−2x2=x2(x−2). It vanishes at 0 and 2. The squared factor is positive everywhere except zero, so only x−2 determines the sign away from those two inputs.

The function decreases on both sides of zero, so (0,0) is stationary but not an extremum. It changes from decreasing to increasing at 2, giving a strict local minimum at (2,−4/3). Continuity combines the first two decreasing pieces: F decreases throughout (−∞,2], despite the isolated zero derivative. For two inputs straddling zero, compare each with F(0) to see the strict decrease across the join.

Key idea 6.3 · First-derivative classification

Suppose f is continuous at an interior point c and differentiable in neighbouring intervals on either side. A change of f′ from positive to negative gives a strict local maximum; negative to positive gives a strict local minimum. The same nonzero sign on both sides gives neither. If the signs are not settled on whole neighbouring intervals, this test has not yet been applied.

Activity 6.4 · Read the factors (20 minutes)

A differentiable function has derivative p′(x)=(x+2)(x−1)2 on the real line. Make a sign chart, classify the stationary inputs and state where p increases or decreases. Can you give the heights of its stationary points from this information? Then repeat the sign analysis for q′(x)=−(x+2)2(x−1).

Discussion of Activity 6.4

For p′, the squared factor is nonnegative and does not change sign at 1. The derivative is negative before −2 and positive after −2 except at 1. Hence p decreases to −2, has a strict local minimum there, and increases afterwards; 1 is stationary without an extremum. The heights are unknown: adding any constant leaves p′ unchanged. For q′, the sign is positive on both sides of −2 and becomes negative at 1. Thus −2 is stationary without an extremum and 1 is a strict local maximum. Explicit factor signs are stronger evidence than a rough sketch.

6.5 Domain boundaries and missing derivatives

Some extrema have no horizontal tangent. The function |x| has a strict minimum at zero, where the left slope is −1 and the right slope is 1. The two-sided derivative does not exist. We therefore need a wider set of candidates: an interior input in the function's domain where its derivative is zero or does not exist is called a critical input. A stationary input is one kind of critical input. A hole outside the function's domain is neither a critical input nor a point of the graph.

For r(x)=1/x, the derivative r′(x)=−1/x2 is negative wherever it exists. The function decreases separately on (−∞,0) and (0,∞). We cannot apply the interval sign test across zero, where the function is undefined. Indeed, −1<1 but r(−1)=−1<1=r(1); the outputs rise between this pair. “Decreases everywhere on its domain” would be false under the all-pairs definition, even though the derivative is negative at every allowed input. State the separate intervals.

Continuity at the candidate is equally important. Define h(x)=x2 for x≠0 but set h(0)=10. The derivatives on either side change from negative to positive, yet the actual point (0,10) is a strict local maximum: all sufficiently nearby nonzero values are smaller. The usual minimum conclusion failed because the pieces did not meet at the function's value. Checking the assumptions protects the reasoning.

Activity 6.5 · Audit four claims (20 minutes)

Correct each claim, using a reason or counterexample: (a) Every local minimum has zero derivative. (b) Every zero derivative gives a local extremum. (c) A negative derivative wherever a function is defined makes the function decreasing across every pair of domain inputs. (d) The function f(x)=x on [0,2] has no minimum because its derivative is never zero. Include a precise domain in each correction.

Discussion of Activity 6.5

(a) An interior differentiable local minimum has zero derivative; |x| at zero shows the missing differentiability condition. (b) x3 on the real line is stationary at zero without an extremum. (c) The derivative test needs an interval contained in the domain, with the relevant continuity; 1/x supplies the disconnected-domain counterexample. (d) The least value is 0 at the included endpoint x=0. The zero-derivative necessary condition concerns interior extrema, not endpoints. Lesson 2 will combine interior candidates with endpoints in a complete comparison.

You can now turn a derivative into an interval claim and identify which points need further investigation. Use the 40-minute feedback period to redraw any incorrect sign charts and write the missing assumption beside each corrected claim. Keep the reserved 30 minutes for the cumulative set. The next lesson asks a new question about the same graph: as it rises or falls, does its slope become larger or smaller?
