M101 · Block 2 · Unit 6

Rates, extrema and the shape of a function

A derivative describes change at one input. How can it tell us what happens across a whole interval?

Imagine watching a reservoir fill. The water level is rising, but perhaps each successive centimetre takes longer to arrive. Three questions are already present: is the level increasing, is its rate increasing, and what limits the level can reach? They concern different features of the same process. A graph can suggest answers, but the shape drawn on a screen depends on which times were sampled and which part of the graph is visible.

In Unit 5 you learned to find derivatives and check their meaning, their units and the points where they exist. This unit puts those calculations to work. We will use the sign of a first derivative to decide where a function rises or falls, and the sign of a second derivative to describe how its slope changes. We will distinguish a horizontal tangent from a turning point, and a nearby high point from the greatest value on the entire allowed domain. The qualifications are part of each conclusion, rather than small print added afterwards.

Related rates bring the same ideas into supplied models. A volume and a depth may both change with time without being given by explicit time formulas. Differentiating the relation between them can still connect their rates. The geometry, the instant at which values are known and the choice of positive direction will matter as much as the differentiation rule.

We will then meet the mean value theorem. It links the rate averaged over an interval to an instantaneous rate somewhere inside that interval, provided the function meets stated conditions. This explains why local derivative information can support interval-wide claims. The final lesson turns to computation: you will deliberately change a graph's window and sampling, see which features disappear, and decide which conclusions survive an analytical check.

Preparing to begin

You need the domain, limit and continuity ideas of Unit 4, and the first and second derivatives, product, quotient, chain and implicit methods of Unit 5. For example, be able to explain why differentiating y2 with respect to time gives 2ydy/dt when y changes with time. Factorising a quadratic and deciding the signs of its factors will be frequent tools. If one of these is uncertain, use the corresponding unit's worked examples and feedback before proceeding.

The final investigation requires the P101 introduction to arrays and labelled plots, as well as functions and loops. That is a readiness condition, not an assumption that you have completed P101. Complete that preparation before the two-hour investigation if needed; the mathematical lessons and the written graph comparisons remain readable without running code. Required preparation is not hidden in optional context.

Working through the unit

The five lessons total 18 hours, including two hours of Python. Lessons 1 and 2 each have four hours, Lessons 3 and 4 each have three, and Lesson 5 has four. Keep 30 minutes from each of the first four lessons for the cumulative exercises at the end: that creates a two-hour attempt period without adding work to the approved total. Each lesson also budgets its own activity feedback; Lesson 5 contains the closing correction and reference time. The allocations are estimates, so record actual study time honestly.

When you make a claim, write its domain and the evidence supporting it. “There is a minimum” is incomplete until you say whether it is local or absolute, where it occurs, and why other candidates have been excluded. By the end you should be able to justify an extremum, formulate and interpret a rate relation, check the assumptions of the mean value theorem, and diagnose a misleading plotted picture. These habits prepare the local approximations and numerical derivatives of Unit 7, as well as later physical model analysis.
