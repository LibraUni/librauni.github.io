M101 · Unit 6 · Lesson 3

Related rates

Two quantities can share the same clock without changing at the same rate. Their relationship tells us how the rates are connected.

A water level and a stored volume rise together, but their numerical rates have different units and usually different behaviour. Related-rate problems begin with a relation between changing quantities and differentiate that relation with respect to time. The method is the implicit differentiation of Unit 5 with a modelling question attached.

Three hours: 60 minutes reading/examples, 65 minutes activities, 25 minutes feedback and correction, and 30 minutes reserved for the unit-end exercises. No new physical law must be recalled: every model used below is supplied.

6.11 Two quantities, one clock

Let h(t) be a water depth and V(t) the volume of water in a container. If its geometry gives V=F(h), then both sides depend on time even when the letter t is not written. The chain rule gives

dVdt=F′(h)dhdt.

Here F′(h) is a derivative with respect to depth, whereas dV/dt and dh/dt are derivatives with respect to time. They are not interchangeable. If F′(h)≠0, the known volume rate can be converted into a depth rate by division.

Units help expose a missing factor. Volume has units m3 and depth has units m, so F′(h) has units m2. Multiplying it by a depth rate in mmin−1 produces a volume rate in m3min−1. Writing just dV/dt=F′(h) would confuse a sensitivity to depth with a rate in time.

Example 6.10 · A cylindrical container

A vertical cylinder has fixed radius r=0.5m. Water volume is V=πr2h. At one instant, water enters at 0.03m3min−1 and leaves at 0.01m3min−1. Find the instantaneous rate at which the depth rises, before overflow.

Working. The net stored-volume rate is inflow minus outflow, V˙=0.02m3min−1, where a dot denotes differentiation with respect to time. The radius is genuinely constant, so differentiating the geometry gives V˙=πr2h˙. Therefore

h˙=0.02π(0.5)2=0.08π mmin−1≈0.0255 mmin−1.

The positive sign indicates rising water. Depth is not needed because the cylinder has the same horizontal cross-sectional area at every height. The given flow rates need hold only at this instant; the answer does not assume that they remain constant tomorrow or even a minute later.

Key idea 6.7 · Differentiate a relation, then evaluate an instant

Define the quantities and their units. Identify what is constant and which rates are known. Write a relation valid as the quantities vary. Differentiate with respect to the common independent variable, retaining chain/product factors. Only then insert the instantaneous values and solve for the requested rate. Finally interpret its sign, units and model conditions.

Activity 6.11 · A negative stored-volume rate (15 minutes)

A cylinder has horizontal area A=0.4m2, with V=Ah. At an instant it receives 0.015m3min−1 and loses 0.025m3min−1. Find h˙ and express it in centimetres per minute. A draft solution substitutes the current depth h=0.6m into V=Ah and then differentiates the constant number. Explain the error.

Discussion of Activity 6.11

The net rate is V˙=−0.010m3min−1, so h˙=V˙/A=−0.025mmin−1=−2.5cmmin−1. The level is falling. The depth 0.6 m describes one instant, not a constant depth during the process. Substituting it before differentiating destroys the changing dependence and falsely suggests zero rate. Substituting a genuinely fixed area before differentiating is legitimate.

6.12 Geometry before numbers

The opening water-tower photograph showed a tank whose width varies with height. We now investigate an ideal spherical container, without assuming that the photographed tank has this internal geometry. Let its fixed radius be R, and measure water depth h upwards from the bottom. The supplied volume model is

V=πh2(R−h3),0≤h≤2R.

You do not need to derive this solid-geometry formula here. Check its limits: at zero depth it gives zero water, while at h=2R it gives the full sphere volume 4πR3/3. These checks help ensure that h is a depth from the bottom, rather than an elevation measured from the centre.

Keep h variable while differentiating. Expanding the product makes the derivative particularly clear:

V=πRh2−π3h3,V˙=(2πRh−πh2)h˙=πh(2R−h)h˙.

The factor πh(2R−h) is the area of the water surface. Indeed, a horizontal section at depth h has radius ρ satisfying ρ2+(h−R)2=R2, so ρ2=h(2R−h). This geometry supplies an independent interpretation: the same small addition of volume raises a wide surface less than a narrow one.

Example 6.11 · Same flow, different depth rates

Take R=2m and a constant positive net inflow V˙=0.2m3min−1. Compare the rise rate at depths 1 m and 2 m.

Working. For interior depths,

h˙=0.2πh(4−h).

At 1 m the rate is 1/(15π)≈0.0212mmin−1; at 2 m it is 1/(20π)≈0.0159mmin−1. Water is still rising at 2 m, but less rapidly because the surface is wider. This is an example of positive first rate with a decreasing rate during the lower half of filling.

Above the equator, the surface narrows, so the same inflow makes the depth rise increasingly rapidly. At exactly h=0 or h=4, the conversion factor vanishes and we cannot divide by it. The ideal model predicts unbounded depth-rate behaviour towards those endpoints under constant nonzero inflow. It does not give a finite endpoint rate or a model of overflow, inlet size or splashing.

Activity 6.12 · Interpret the conversion factor (20 minutes)

For the same spherical model, compare rise rates at 1 m and 3 m. At what interior depth is the rise rate smallest under constant positive inflow, and why? If the tank instead drains at net rate −0.1m3min−1, find h˙ at 2 m. State one reason not to extrapolate this ideal model through a full or empty tank.

Discussion of Activity 6.12

The area factors at 1 and 3 m are both 3πm2, giving the same positive rise rate 1/(15π)mmin−1. The denominator h(4−h)=4−(h−2)2 is greatest at 2 m, so the positive rate is smallest there. Under draining, h˙=−0.1/(4π)=−1/(40π)≈−0.00796mmin−1. A real tank has finite inlet/outlet geometry, and a full tank can overflow; neither is represented by continuing the ideal depth formula outside 0≤h≤4.

6.13 Two changing lengths

Sometimes no single quantity has already been eliminated. Suppose two markers move along perpendicular tracks from a common origin. Their nonnegative distances from the origin are x(t) and y(t), and their separation is d(t). The right triangle gives d2=x2+y2. All three lengths may change, so all three squared terms require a chain factor:

2dd˙=2xx˙+2yy˙,d˙=xx˙+yy˙d,d>0.

The separation rate is not generally the sum of the two track speeds. Only the components of motion along the changing line of separation contribute to its first rate. The algebra accounts for the changing geometry without requiring a new vector method.

Example 6.12 · One marker approaches the junction

At an instant, x=6m, y=8m, x˙=2ms−1 and y˙=−1ms−1. Find the separation rate.

Working. First recover the missing length: d=62+82=10m. Insert the simultaneous values into the differentiated relation:

d˙=6(2)+8(−1)10=0.4ms−1.

The markers are becoming farther apart, even though one moves towards the origin. Its negative contribution partly offsets the other's positive contribution. Units in the numerator are m2s−1, divided by metres to give a length rate. These values describe one instant; no common starting time is needed.

At d=0 the displayed division fails. Both markers would be at the origin, and their subsequent motions would require separate analysis. A formula obtained by division always carries the nonzero condition of that division. Treating a zero denominator as merely a computer inconvenience can hide a genuine change in the geometry.

Activity 6.13 · Zero separation rate without rest (15 minutes)

At x=3m and y=4m, suppose x˙=4ms−1. Find y˙ that makes d˙=0 at this instant. Are the markers at rest? Does the answer establish a constant separation for an entire interval?

Discussion of Activity 6.13

Set the numerator to zero: 3(4)+4y˙=0, so y˙=−3ms−1. Both markers move, but the first-order contributions to separation cancel at this instant. Zero rate at one instant does not imply constant separation over an interval; the geometry and rates can change. To establish constancy, the rate relation would need to remain zero throughout the interval under the model's conditions.

6.14 Rates in a supplied physical model

The same method works when the connecting relation comes from physics instead of geometry. For a fixed amount of an ideal gas held at constant temperature, the supplied model is pV=C, with positive absolute pressure p (measured from vacuum, not relative to atmospheric pressure), positive volume V and constant C. This is a restricted model: a changing temperature or a leak would change the relation used for differentiation. We are not deriving gas physics here; we are working out what this stated relation implies.

Example 6.13 · A pressure rate during expansion

At one instant, p=120kPa, V=0.050m3 and V˙=0.002m3s−1. Find p˙ under the constant-temperature, fixed-amount model.

Working. Both factors in pV vary. The product rule, with C˙=0, gives Vp˙+pV˙=0. Hence

p˙=−pVV˙=−1200.050(0.002)=−4.8kPas−1.

The negative sign agrees with expansion reducing the pressure in this model. Dividing the differentiated relation by pV also gives

p˙p=−V˙V.

The instantaneous fractional rates have opposite signs and equal magnitudes, here 0.04s−1. This is not a statement that a finite 4% volume increase produces exactly a 4% pressure decrease; finite changes obey p2V2=p1V1, and instantaneous rates describe a different comparison.

Activity 6.14 · Audit the assumptions and calculation (15 minutes)

At p=200kPa and V=0.040m3, the same model gives p˙=5kPas−1. Find V˙ and explain its sign. What extra term appears if the relation is instead pV=C(t)? Finally, a solution writes p˙V˙=0 by “differentiating both factors”. Diagnose that error.

Discussion of Activity 6.14

V˙=−(V/p)p˙=−0.001m3s−1. The volume falls while pressure rises, consistent with fixed positive product. With changing C, the equation becomes Vp˙+pV˙=C˙; without the additional rate information, the previous answer is not warranted. A product derivative is a sum of two contributions, not the product of the two derivatives. The erroneous product also has units of pressure × volume/time squared, unlike the derivative of pV.

In the feedback period, take one geometry example and one physical-model example and rewrite them from the definitions of their variables. Check that each numerical value belongs to the same instant, each fixed quantity really is fixed, and every division is allowed. Keep the reserved 30 minutes for the unit exercises. We next return to interval-wide reasoning: under what conditions must a function achieve its average rate as an instantaneous rate somewhere inside the interval?
