M101 · Unit 5 · Lesson 1

From secants to a derivative

We can measure a change over an interval. What would it mean to assign a rate to one input?

In Unit 4, a limit described what happened near a point. Here the quantity whose limit we take will itself be a ratio: change in output divided by change in input. The denominator tends to zero, but so does the numerator. We need to understand their relationship, not inspect either in isolation.

Four hours includes the unit opening, reading and worked examples (75 minutes), activities and reconstruction of the reasoning (100 minutes), feedback and correction (35 minutes), and 30 minutes reserved for the unit-end exercises. Activity times are guides, including sketching and explanation.

5.1 Average changes and secants

Suppose a small carriage moves along a straight track. Let t denote the numerical time in seconds and let s(t)=t2+2t+1 give its numerical position in metres, for 0≤t≤4. This is a supplied model, not a conclusion drawn from measurements. It lets us calculate positions at any allowed time.

At t=1 the position is 4 m; at t=3 it is 16 m. The displacement over the interval is therefore 12 m, and the elapsed time is 2 s. The average velocity is 12/2=6ms−1. It says that a constant velocity of 6 metres per second would produce the same displacement in the same time. It does not say that the carriage moved at that velocity throughout.

Key idea 5.1 · Average rate

For two distinct inputs a and b in the domain, the average rate of change is

f(b)−f(a)b−a.

Its units are output units divided by input units. The slope of the line through (a,f(a)) and (b,f(b)) is this ratio. That line is called a secant.

The order of subtraction must agree above and below the fraction bar. Reversing both orders leaves the ratio unchanged. Reversing only the numerator gives the opposite slope. A negative slope means that the output decreases as the input increases; it need not mean that the output itself is negative.

A wider view · Tracks need times

To calculate an average velocity we would need positions in a stated coordinate system and their times. Our straight-track model supplies both through a formula; it does not represent this Martian drive. Keeping that distinction visible prevents a graph of position against time from being mistaken for a picture of the physical path.

Example 5.1 · One model, different intervals

Compare the carriage's average velocities over [1,2] and [2,3].

Working. The positions are s(1)=4, s(2)=9 and s(3)=16. Each interval lasts one second, so the average velocities are (9−4)/1=5ms−1 and (16−9)/1=7ms−1. The later interval has the larger average. Their equal durations make the whole-interval average the arithmetic mean, (5+7)/2=6ms−1. Unequal durations would require weighting by time.

The position 9 m at two seconds is neither of these average velocities. Even if a position and a velocity happened to have equal numerical values, their meanings and units would still differ.

Activity 5.1 · Interpret the ratio (15 minutes)

A volume model gives V(t)=18−3t litres for numerical time t in minutes, 0≤t≤5. Find average rates on [0,2] and [2,5]. Sketch its graph and explain why the rates agree. Then explain the mistake in reporting “the volume is −3 litres per minute”.

Discussion of Activity 5.1

The volumes at 0, 2 and 5 minutes are 18, 12 and 3 litres. The ratios are (12−18)/2=−3 and (3−12)/3=−3, both in litres per minute. A straight line has the same slope for every secant. The negative rate describes decreasing volume; the volume itself stays positive on this interval. A sound report is “the volume decreases at 3 litres per minute”.

5.2 A rate at one input

Return to the carriage and fix the input at t=2. Compare its position with that at 2+h, where h is a nonzero time offset. Positive h samples later; negative h samples earlier. The average rate, with the signs used consistently, is

Q(h)=s(2+h)−s(2)h=(2+h)2+2(2+h)+1−9h.

Expanding the entire numerator gives 6h+h2. Both terms contain h, so for h≠0 we obtain Q(h)=6+h. This simplification is valid on −2≤h≤2 except at zero, because both sampled times must lie in the model's domain. The quotient at zero is still undefined.

For h=0.5 and 0.1, the rates are 6.5 and 6.1 metres per second. For h=−0.5 and −0.1, they are 5.5 and 5.9 metres per second. The calculations approach 6 from opposite sides. More decisively, the exact expression 6+h has limit 6 as h→0. Thus the model has instantaneous velocity 6ms−1 at two seconds.

Key idea 5.2 · Derivative at a point

For a function defined on an open interval about a, its derivative at a is the finite two-sided limit

f′(a)=limh→0f(a+h)−f(a)h,

when that limit exists. We then say that f is differentiable at a. Every quotient uses h≠0. The two-sided limit requires agreement from positive and negative offsets.

Nothing is divided by a zero time interval. Each quotient describes two different inputs; the limit describes the value approached by those quotients. A derivative is therefore not a very small average rate, though a suitably small average rate may approximate it. At a boundary we may explicitly define a one-sided derivative; that is a different convention from the ordinary two-sided derivative used at interior points here.

Activity 5.2 · Make the approach quantitative (20 minutes)

For the carriage at t=2, calculate the four quotients with h=±0.2,±0.02. How small must |h| be to make the difference from 6 less than 0.005? Explain why this algebra gives stronger evidence than four decimal answers.

Discussion of Activity 5.2

The answers are 6.2, 5.8, 6.02 and 5.98, in the corresponding order. Since |Q(h)−6|=|h|, any 0<|h|<0.005 gives the required numerical tolerance in metres per second. All these times remain in the model domain. The identity applies to every such offset; the table checks only four.

5.3 Deriving a whole function

We need not choose a numerical input before forming the quotient. Keeping the centre x general produces a formula for the derivative wherever the same reasoning works. The letter x is held fixed during the limit; h is the changing offset. Distinguishing their roles avoids the mistake of making both tend to zero.

Example 5.2 · A quadratic from first principles

Derive the derivative of f(x)=2x2−3x+4 on the real line.

Working. Replace every occurrence of x by x+h, then subtract the whole original value:

f(x+h)−f(x)h=2(x+h)2−3(x+h)+4−(2x2−3x+4)h.

The final brackets matter: subtracting f(x) reverses the signs of all its terms. Expanding and collecting gives

4xh+2h2−3hh=4x+2h−3(h≠0).

Taking the limit yields f′(x)=4x−3. There are no restrictions on the centre x here. The new function assigns a slope to every input. For example, f′(2)=5, whereas f(2)=6; these numbers answer different questions.

The prime in f′ names the derived function. For y=f(x), the notation dy/dx describes the same derivative and makes the independent variable explicit. The symbol d/dx is an instruction to differentiate the expression that follows. It is not a request to divide the numerical value of y by that of x.

Changing a function by a constant vertical shift leaves every output difference unchanged. Indeed, if g(x)=f(x)+C, then g(x+h)−g(x)=f(x+h)−f(x). Consequently g′(x)=f′(x) wherever the derivative exists. This explains why a derivative alone cannot reveal the absolute height of a graph.

Activity 5.3 · Rebuild the argument (25 minutes)

Use the definition, without quoting a power rule, to differentiate (a) p(x)=x2+5x; (b) q(x)=7−2x; (c) r(x)=1/x for x≠0. For (c), combine the fractions before dividing by h. State the restrictions on h for a fixed centre x.

Discussion of Activity 5.3

(a) The numerator is 2xh+h2+5h, so the quotient is 2x+h+5 and p′(x)=2x+5. (b) The numerator is −2h, so every nonzero quotient is −2 and q′(x)=−2. (c)

1/(x+h)−1/xh=−hx(x+h)h=−1x(x+h).

Require x≠0, h≠0 and x+h≠0. For this fixed nonzero x, sufficiently small offsets avoid −x. The limit is r′(x)=−1/x2. This derivative does not supply a value at the excluded input zero.

5.4 Tangent lines and units

As secant slopes settle, their limiting slope specifies a line through the fixed point of the curve. This is the tangent line at that point when the derivative is finite. A point and a slope determine a line, so the equation is

y−f(a)=f′(a)(x−a).

Here a is the fixed contact input and x is a variable input along the line. The tangent has the same value and slope as the curve at a; it generally has different values elsewhere. We will study its use as a local approximation in Unit 7. For now, constructing it makes the derivative's geometrical meaning explicit.

Example 5.3 · A point as well as a slope

Find the tangent to f(x)=2x2−3x+4 at x=2.

Working. Example 5.2 supplies f′(2)=5. The point is (2,6), so y−6=5(x−2), or y=5x−4. Check by inserting x=2: the line gives 6. The tempting line y=5x has the correct slope but misses the contact point. At x=3 the tangent gives 11 and the curve gives 13, so the tangent is not the curve itself.

For a position–time graph, the derivative has units of length per time. For a voltage–temperature calibration graph, it has units of volts per degree. If the scales on a drawing change, the line's apparent angle changes, but the physical rate obtained from the labelled axes does not. Never read a rate from a visual angle without checking scales and units.

Activity 5.4 · A calibration slope (20 minutes)

A supplied sensor model is V(T)=0.002T2+0.1T+1, with numerical temperature T in degrees Celsius and output V in volts, on 0≤T≤40. Use first principles to find the rate at T=20 and give its units. Find the tangent line there. Does the rate say that the voltage equals 0.18 V?

Discussion of Activity 5.4

The quotient is 0.004T+0.002h+0.1, giving derivative 0.004T+0.1. At 20 the rate is 0.18V/∘C and the output is 3.8V. The tangent is V−3.8=0.18(T−20), with numerical coordinates in the stated units. A rate of 0.18 volts per degree is not a voltage of 0.18 volts. The model is supplied; differentiation does not verify a real sensor's calibration.

5.5 Where a derivative fails

A graph can be continuous without having a single local slope. For f(x)=|x| at zero, the quotient is |h|/h. It is 1 for positive offsets and −1 for negative offsets. The graph joins without a gap, but the slopes do not join. There is no ordinary derivative at zero.

Conversely, a finite derivative forces continuity. To see why, write the output change for h≠0 as

f(a+h)−f(a)=hf(a+h)−f(a)h.

If the quotient tends to a finite value, the right side tends to zero: a factor tending to zero multiplies a factor tending to a finite number. Hence f(a+h)→f(a), which is continuity at a. A discontinuity therefore rules out differentiability, but continuity alone does not establish it.

A different failure occurs for f(x)=x1/3 at zero. Its quotient is 1/h2/3, which grows without bound from both sides. The graph has a vertical tangent, but no finite derivative there. We do not assign “infinity” as a real derivative value. At the endpoint of x, only positive offsets are allowed, and even the one-sided quotient 1/h grows without bound.

Activity 5.5 · Test a claim (20 minutes)

A colleague says, “If a function is continuous, its derivative exists; if its derivative is zero at a point, the function is zero there.” Refute both statements with explicit functions and points. Finally use the definition to decide whether g(x)=x|x| is differentiable at zero.

Discussion of Activity 5.5

|x| is continuous at zero but has unequal one-sided quotients. A constant function such as f(x)=8 has derivative zero everywhere and value 8 everywhere. For g, the quotient at zero is h|h|/h=|h|, tending to zero from both sides. Thus g′(0)=0. The presence of an absolute-value symbol does not by itself decide differentiability; the complete quotient matters.

We now have a definition, a method for deriving simple results, and a way to recognise failures. In the next lesson we will turn these ideas into a collection of derivative families. The rules will shorten calculations, but every rule will retain the meaning and conditions established here.
