M101 · Unit 5 · Lesson 4

The chain rule

A change can pass through several stages. Each stage alters the rate seen at the end.

Composition was an operation on functions in Unit 4: feed the output of one rule into another. We now ask how its rate depends on the rates of the component rules. The most important habit is to distinguish the input to the whole composition from the input seen by its outer function.

Four hours: 75 minutes reading/examples, 100 minutes activities, 35 minutes correction and 30 minutes reserved for the closing unit exercises. Write intermediate variables when they make a chain easier to see.

5.16 A change passed through another function

Consider a supplied temperature model T(t)=20+2t, where numerical time t is in minutes, and a sensor conversion V(T)=0.01T2, giving volts from numerical temperature in degrees Celsius. At t=5, the temperature is 30 degrees and the voltage is 9 V. The rate of temperature change is 2 degrees per minute. The sensor's rate of voltage change with temperature at 30 degrees is 0.02(30)=0.6 volts per degree.

A small time change first produces a temperature change; that change produces a voltage change. Multiplying the two local rates gives 0.6×2=1.2 volts per minute. We can check directly: the complete model is V(t)=0.01(20+2t)2=4+0.8t+0.04t2, whose derivative at 5 is 0.8+0.08(5)=1.2. The multiplication has not lost the intermediate temperature—it has evaluated the outer rate at precisely the temperature reached by the inner function.

Key idea 5.8 · Chain rule

If u=g(x) is differentiable at x and f is differentiable at u=g(x), then

ddxf(g(x))=f′(g(x))g′(x).

In intermediate-variable notation,

dydx=dydududx,y=f(u),u=g(x).

Evaluate the outer derivative at the inner value, then multiply by the inner derivative.

The rate interpretation motivates this theorem, but simply cancelling the symbols du is not a proof. The statement remains valid when g′(x)=0, and even when some nearby inner changes are zero. A formal difference-quotient proof must handle those cases rather than divide by a possibly zero inner increment. We use the established theorem with its differentiability conditions.

Activity 5.16 · At which input? (15 minutes)

Suppose g(2)=5, g′(2)=−3, f′(5)=4 and f′(2)=7. Find the derivative of f(g(x)) at x=2. Which given number is not needed? Explain the sign in terms of two successive changes.

Discussion of Activity 5.16

The result is f′(g(2))g′(2)=4(−3)=−12. The outer function is receiving 5, so f′(2)=7 is irrelevant. As the original input increases a little, the inner output decreases; the outer function has positive rate at that inner output, so the final output decreases locally too. Multiplying 7(−3) would evaluate the correct derivative at the wrong input.

5.17 Reading inner and outer functions

To identify the outer function, imagine replacing the entire inner expression by a single letter. In (1+3x)5, replacing 1+3x by u leaves u5. The outer derivative is 5u4; the inner derivative is 3. Restoring the inner expression gives 15(1+3x)4. Expanding a fifth power would also work, but would hide the simple two-stage structure.

Example 5.12 · A root around a polynomial

Differentiate F(x)=1+x2.

Working. The inner function u=1+x2 is positive at every real input, so the outer root is differentiable wherever it is used. With dF/du=1/(2u) and du/dx=2x,

F′(x)=121+x22x=x1+x2.

The result is zero at zero because the inner square has zero rate there. This does not invalidate the rule: the outer root is differentiable at its actual input 1. The derivative is defined for all real x.

The same reasoning gives useful general forms:

ddxeu(x)=eu(x)u′(x),ddxlnu(x)=u′(x)u(x)(u(x)>0).

For trigonometric functions, differentiate the outer function while leaving the whole angle in place: (sinu)′=cos(u)u′ and (cosu)′=−sin(u)u′. The angle is still measured in radians. A fixed phase shift affects the argument but contributes zero to its derivative.

Example 5.13 · A decaying model and a scaled angle

Differentiate q(t)=5e−2t and w(x)=sin(3x+π/4), treating the inputs as dimensionless numerical variables.

Working. For the first, the inner derivative is −2, so q′(t)=−10e−2t. The positive quantity decreases. For the second, the inner derivative is 3, so w′(x)=3cos(3x+π/4). Neither the negative sign in the exponential nor the factor 3 in the angle may be dropped. The phase π/4 remains inside cosine even though its derivative is zero.

Activity 5.17 · Identify, then differentiate (25 minutes)

For each expression, name inner and outer functions before differentiating: (a) (2−x)6; (b) ex2+1; (c) ln(4x+1); (d) cos(5x); (e) 9−x2. State derivative domains, paying particular attention to the root endpoints.

Discussion of Activity 5.17

The derivatives are (a) −6(2−x)5, all real x; (b) 2xex2+1, all real x; (c) 4/(4x+1) for x>−1/4; (d) −5sin(5x), all real x; (e) −x/9−x2 for −3<x<3. In each case the expression in parentheses, exponent or radicand is the inner function. The last function exists at ±3, but its one-sided slopes are unbounded there, so there is no finite endpoint derivative. Function domain and derivative domain differ.

5.18 Nested chains and powers of functions

The notation sin2x means (sinx)2, not sin(x2). The first applies sine and then squares; the second squares and then applies sine. Their derivatives are correspondingly 2sinxcosx and 2xcos(x2). Writing the implied brackets before differentiating prevents a notational shorthand from changing the problem.

A chain can have more than two stages. In esin(x2), the input is squared, sine is taken, then the exponential is applied. Start with the last operation and work back, multiplying a rate for every stage:

ddxesin(x2)=esin(x2)cos(x2)2x.

All three factors have a role. Omitting 2x would give a derivative with respect to the squared input, not with respect to x. The order of multiplication does not matter once the factors are correct, but the argument at which each factor is evaluated does matter.

Example 5.14 · Three layers with a domain

Differentiate H(x)=ln(1+x).

Working. The original domain is x≥0, because the logarithm then receives a value at least 1. For x>0, the layers are x↦x↦1+x↦ln(1+x). Thus

H′(x)=11+x·1·12x=12x(1+x).

At zero the root derivative used by this chain calculation is unavailable, so this formula applies on x>0. The derivative does not become valid at zero just because H(0)=0 exists. We need no endpoint value for the subsequent calculations.

A failed condition in a chosen decomposition does not prove that the whole function is nondifferentiable. For example, |x2|=x2 has derivative zero at zero even though the outer absolute-value function is not differentiable at its inner value zero. The chain theorem does not apply to that decomposition at zero; simplifying the complete function settles the question. A theorem's conditions guarantee a conclusion; their failure is not automatically the negation of that conclusion.

Activity 5.18 · Keep every layer (20 minutes)

Differentiate (a) cos3x; (b) cos(x3); (c) ecos(2x); (d) (1+lnx)−2. For (d), separate the logarithm restriction from the nonzero-denominator restriction.

Discussion of Activity 5.18

(a) −3cos2xsinx. (b) −3x2sin(x3). (c) −2ecos(2x)sin(2x). These hold for every real input in radians. (d) −2/[x(1+lnx)3]; require x>0 and 1+lnx≠0, so exclude e−1. The two restrictions arise at different stages of the composition.

5.19 Combining chain, product and quotient rules

Longer expressions rarely need a single rule. Their outer structure determines the first rule, while the pieces may need further rules. It helps to draw a small operation tree or simply mark the two top-level factors. Do not search for an isolated square or sine and differentiate it while ignoring how it is connected to the rest.

Example 5.15 · A product with an inner chain

Differentiate f(x)=x2e−x2.

Working. The whole expression is a product. The second factor is a composition. Applying both rules gives

f′(x)=2xe−x2+x2e−x2(−2x)=2xe−x2(1−x2).

At zero the derivative is zero; at 1 it is also zero, but for a different algebraic reason. We are only calculating the rates here. Deciding what these zeros imply about extrema requires the additional reasoning of Unit 6.

Example 5.16 · Two routes through a denominator

Differentiate R(x)=sinx/(1+x2).

Working. The denominator is positive for every real input. The quotient rule gives

R′(x)=(1+x2)cosx−2xsinx(1+x2)2.

Alternatively write R=sinx(1+x2)−1. The product rule then gives cosx(1+x2)−1−2xsinx(1+x2)−2. A common denominator recovers the same answer. The chain rule in the reciprocal form accounts for the 2x that is easy to miss.

Activity 5.19 · State the first decision (20 minutes)

Differentiate (a) (x+1)ln(1+x2); (b) e2x/(1+x); (c) 1+sin2x; (d) (x2+1)2. For each, state the outermost rule and domain. Check (d) by expansion.

Discussion of Activity 5.19

(a) Product then chain: ln(1+x2)+2x(x+1)/(1+x2), all real x. (b) Quotient then chain: e2x[2(1+x)−1]/(1+x)2=e2x(1+2x)/(1+x)2, x≠−1. (c) Nested chain: sinxcosx/1+sin2x, all real x. (d) Chain: 4x(x2+1); expansion gives x4+2x2+1 and derivative 4x3+4x, all real x. Different valid routes should agree on both expression and domain.

5.20 Dimensional arguments and derivative checks

Exponential and logarithmic arguments in physical models must be dimensionless, as must the numerical arguments supplied to sine and cosine. A temperature T measured in kelvin cannot simply appear as lnT without specifying a reference scale. A meaningful version is ln(T/T0), where T0 is a fixed positive temperature. Its rate with respect to T is (T0/T)(1/T0)=1/T, with units of inverse kelvin.

Likewise, for a supplied decay model Q(t)=Q0e−t/τ with constant positive time scale τ, the exponent is dimensionless and

dQdt=−Q0τe−t/τ.

The missing chain factor 1/τ would produce wrong units as well as a wrong rate. This is a dimensional check of a given model; we have not solved a differential equation or established a physical decay law.

A wider view · Calibration as a chain of conversions

The supplied quadratic conversion in §5.16 is a teaching model, not a calibration curve obtained from this apparatus. Its purpose is to show why volts per degree and degrees per minute combine into volts per minute. A derivative calculation can propagate a specified model's rates; it cannot establish the model's empirical validity.

Activity 5.20 · Check the scale (20 minutes)

A supplied displacement is s(t)=Asin(ωt+ϕ), where A is a constant length, ω has units of inverse time and ϕ is a fixed radian phase. Find its derivative and units. For A=0.03 m, ω=4s−1 and ϕ=0, evaluate the rate at zero. Then differentiate L(t)=ln(1+t/τ) for constant τ>0, stating its domain.

Discussion of Activity 5.20

s′(t)=Aωcos(ωt+ϕ) has units of length per time. The specified value is 0.12ms−1. The output's numerical amplitude 0.03 is not its velocity amplitude. For L, the logarithm requires t>−τ. Its derivative is (1/τ)/(1+t/τ)=1/(τ+t), with inverse-time units. The argument and domain are as important as the simplified answer.

We have learned to follow a rate through composition, combine the rule with products and quotients, and use domains and units to challenge an answer. Next we will use exactly this chain rule when the changing quantity is named by a symbol such as y but not yet isolated by a formula.
