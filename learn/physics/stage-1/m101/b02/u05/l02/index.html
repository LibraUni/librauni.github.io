M101 · Unit 5 · Lesson 2

Elementary derivative families

A small collection of results can replace many repeated limit calculations—provided we recognise the function and keep its conditions.

The definition remains our account of what a derivative means. We now use it to justify some reusable rules and introduce others as established results. Learning a rule means learning its input as well as its output: a power of a variable is not the same structure as a variable exponent, and a trigonometric rate depends on the angular scale.

Four hours: 75 minutes reading and examples, 100 minutes activities, 35 minutes correction and 30 minutes reserved for the cumulative unit exercises. Recalculate the examples after reading them; fluent rule use grows through explained practice.

5.6 Constants, sums and powers

If f(x)=C for every input, each numerator in its difference quotient is C−C=0. Its derivative is zero. If instead f(x)=Cx, the numerator is Ch and the derivative is C. The constant in the first expression is the whole output; in the second it scales a changing input. This distinction accounts for many early errors.

Suppose u and v are differentiable at the input under discussion. The output change of u+v is the sum of their separate output changes. Dividing by h and applying the limit sum law therefore adds their derivatives. A fixed multiplier can likewise be taken outside the quotient and its limit. Thus

ddx[Au(x)+Bv(x)]=Au′(x)+Bv′(x),

where A and B are constants with respect to x. Subtraction is included by taking a negative multiplier. We have justified a rule for linear combinations, not a rule for multiplying two changing functions.

For positive integer powers, expanding (x+h)n leaves a first change term nxn−1h, with all remaining change terms containing at least h2. After division by h, those remaining terms contain h and tend to zero. The result is the power rule. For instance, direct multiplication gives (x+h)3=x3+3x2h+3xh2+h3; its quotient tends to 3x2. The general rule follows the same pattern in the finite binomial expansion.

Key idea 5.3 · Polynomial differentiation

dCdx=0,ddxxn=nxn−1(n=1,2,3,…).

Add derivatives term by term and retain constant multipliers. Polynomials are differentiable at every real input.

Example 5.4 · Keep the coefficients visible

Find p′(x) and p′(−1) for p(x)=3x4−2x3+7x−9.

Working. Treat each term separately:

p′(x)=3(4x3)−2(3x2)+7(1)−0=12x3−6x2+7.

Only then substitute: p′(−1)=−12−6+7=−11. The term −2x3 contributes −6x2, including its sign. Differentiating after first substituting x=−1 would instead differentiate a constant and erase the variation we need to study.

Activity 5.6 · Constants in different roles (20 minutes)

Differentiate 8, 8x, 8x2, x5−4x2+6, and Ax3+Bx+C, where A,B,C do not depend on x. Use the definition to check the derivative of 8x. Explain why the derivative of a polynomial need not have the same degree as the polynomial.

Discussion of Activity 5.6

The derivatives are 0, 8, 16x, 5x4−8x, and 3Ax2+B. For 8x, the quotient [8(x+h)−8x]/h is 8 whenever h≠0. A nonconstant polynomial of degree n has derivative of degree n−1 because its leading coefficient is multiplied by nonzero n and its exponent falls by one. A constant has zero derivative, for which the usual degree convention needs separate treatment.

5.7 Negative and fractional powers

The same formula extends beyond positive integers, but domains now matter. For a real exponent r, the power rule is valid on x>0:

ddxxr=rxr−1.

For integer powers it also applies at negative inputs where the original function is defined. Negative integer powers exclude zero. Rational powers may allow negative inputs when interpreted through an odd root; apply the formula on the corresponding open intervals where that real expression is defined, and check zero separately. This formulation avoids pretending that every real power is a real-valued function on the entire real line.

A reciprocal or root often becomes easier to differentiate after rewriting it as a power. For x>0, x=x1/2 and 1/x=x−1/2. The derivative of the first is 12x−1/2; that of the second is −12x−3/2. The minus sign in the second derivative has a sensible interpretation: as a positive root increases, its reciprocal decreases.

Example 5.5 · Rewrite before differentiating

Differentiate f(x)=4/x−3/x2+2x and state its domain.

Working. The denominator root requires x>0; this already satisfies the other restrictions. Write f(x)=4x−1/2−3x−2+2x1/2. Then

f′(x)=−2x−3/2+6x−3+x−1/2=−2x3/2+6x3+1x,x>0.

The exponent −2 becomes −3, not −1. “Subtract one” means an algebraic subtraction even when the exponent is already negative.

The domain of a derivative need not equal the domain of the function. The square-root function exists at zero, but its positive difference quotients grow without bound there. A formula such as 1/(2x) signals a problem at zero, yet the definition is what settles it. Conversely, x4/3 has derivative zero at zero: its quotient there is h4/3/h=h1/3, tending to zero. An apparently delicate input deserves a direct check rather than an automatic rejection.

Activity 5.7 · Powers with conditions (20 minutes)

Find derivatives and domains for (a) x−4; (b) 5x−1/x; (c) x2/3. In (c), use the real cube-root interpretation and examine zero by the definition. Compare with the x4/3 case above.

Discussion of Activity 5.7

(a) −4x−5, for x≠0. (b) 52x−1/2+12x−3/2, for x>0. (c) For x≠0, the derivative is 23x−1/3, with the real cube root. At zero the quotient is h−1/3, tending to opposite unbounded signs from the two sides; no finite derivative exists. The function itself exists at zero. The extra factor of x2/3 in x4/3 changes the quotient's limiting behaviour, so the two cases cannot be decided from the denominator 3 alone.

5.8 The natural exponential and logarithm

A power xr has a fixed exponent and changing base. An exponential bx has a fixed positive base and changing exponent. The power rule therefore does not differentiate an exponential. In particular, differentiating 2x as x2x−1 would apply the wrong rule to the wrong variable.

The natural exponential ex has the special property that its slope equals its value. One way to characterise the constant e≈2.71828 is that the slope of ex at zero equals 1. With that established property, the exponential law explains the derivative everywhere:

ex+h−exh=exeh−1h⟶ex.

The nontrivial result used here is limh→0(eh−1)/h=1. We use it as an established elementary limit, rather than claiming that a table proves it. The logarithm is the inverse function of the exponential: y=lnx means ey=x, with x>0. Its derivative is 1/x. Later, implicit differentiation will give a short explanation by differentiating that inverse relation.

Key idea 5.4 · Exponential and logarithmic families

ddxex=ex,ddxlnx=1x(x>0).

For fixed b>0,

ddxbx=bxlnb.

For a logarithm to base b>0, b≠1, use logbx=lnx/lnb, hence derivative 1/(xlnb) on x>0. The natural logarithm is the one with derivative exactly 1/x.

Example 5.6 · Different structures in one sum

Find the derivative of F(x)=2ex+lnx−x3 and its value at 1.

Working. The logarithm restricts the complete expression to x>0. Each term belongs to a known family, so F′(x)=2ex+1/x−3x2 on that domain. Then F′(1)=2e−2. This is an exact value; a decimal is optional. The ex term retains its form, whereas the polynomial term loses one power. The derivative at 1 must be evaluated from the complete sum, not from just the term that looks largest.

Activity 5.8 · Base or exponent? (15 minutes)

Differentiate x3, 3x, ex+4, lnx+2x, and log10x. State where each is differentiable. Explain why the rate of bx is negative when 0<b<1, despite the function always being positive.

Discussion of Activity 5.8

The derivatives are 3x2, 3xln3, ex, 1/x+2, and 1/(xln10). The first three are defined on the real line; the final two require positive inputs. For 0<b<1, bx>0 but lnb<0, so their product is negative. A positive quantity can be decreasing. For b=1, the exponential is constant and its derivative is zero, consistent with ln1=0.

5.9 Trigonometric derivatives in radians

A sine curve rises most steeply where it crosses zero upwards and flattens at its highest point. A cosine curve gives exactly that pattern of slopes: at zero it is 1, at π/2 it is 0, and at π it is −1. This geometric observation suggests the derivative rule; it does not alone prove it.

The rule depends on two established limits with angles measured in radians: sinh/h→1 and (cosh−1)/h→0. Using the angle-addition identity, the sine quotient becomes

sin(x+h)−sinxh=sinxcosh−1h+cosxsinhh.

The first term tends to zero and the second to cosx. The same method with the cosine addition identity gives −sinx. This shows exactly where the angular convention enters the argument. The geometric proof of the two auxiliary limits is not required here; their assumptions must still be retained.

Key idea 5.5 · Trigonometric derivatives

ddxsinx=cosx,ddxcosx=−sinx.

Both hold for all real x, in radians. Also

ddxtanx=1cos2x=sec2x,cosx≠0,

where secx means 1/cosx. We will derive this last rule from tanx=sinx/cosx in Lesson 3.

If the independent variable is a numerical angle in degrees, one degree is π/180 radians. The rate per degree is therefore π/180 times the rate per radian at the corresponding angle. For example, the sine of a degree-valued angle has slope π/180, approximately 0.01745 per degree, at zero—not 1. A calculator in degree mode can quietly invalidate a numerical check of a radian derivative.

Example 5.7 · Keep a negative sign through evaluation

Differentiate g(x)=4cosx−3sinx and evaluate at x=π/2.

Working. The derivative is g′(x)=−4sinx−3cosx. At π/2, it is −4(1)−3(0)=−4. The function's value there is −3. A negative output and a negative slope happen together here, but neither determines the other.

Activity 5.9 · Read a wave and its rate (20 minutes)

Sketch sinx on [0,2π] and mark where its tangent is horizontal. Use the derivative to confirm those locations. Differentiate 2sinx+5cosx+x and evaluate at 0 and π. Explain why a horizontal tangent does not mean that the original function is zero.

Discussion of Activity 5.9

The horizontal tangents occur at π/2 and 3π/2, where cosx=0 but sine is 1 and −1 respectively. The requested derivative is 2cosx−5sinx+1, giving 3 at zero and −1 at π. A horizontal tangent describes a zero rate at a point; the graph may be at any height.

A wider view · Projected area changes with angle

For an ideal flat patch of area A, its projected area towards parallel incoming rays is Acosθ, when θ is the angle between the surface normal and the direction towards the light source, with 0≤θ<π/2. Holding A fixed, the angular rate is −Asinθ per radian. It is zero when the patch faces the rays squarely, even though the projected area is then largest. This geometrical model is not a complete model of an actual solar array's electrical output: illumination, shadowing and conversion efficiency also matter.

5.10 Choosing a family and stating its domain

Before differentiating, identify the outermost arithmetic operation. A sum of familiar families can now be handled term by term. A product such as xsinx cannot: both factors change with the input. A composition such as sin(x2) also needs another rule, because the angle is changing at a rate set by x2. Recognising what is not yet covered is part of using the present rules correctly.

Check the domain before and after the calculation. For lnx+x, the logarithm forces x>0 even though the root alone allows zero. Its derivative 1/x+1/(2x) has the same domain. For x alone, the derivative domain is smaller than the function domain. In neither case can a convenient formula silently enlarge the original domain.

Activity 5.10 · Fluency with explanations (25 minutes)

Find derivatives of (a) 7x6−4x−3+9; (b) x+lnx−2ex; (c) 5sinx−2tanx; (d) aex+bx2+c, with fixed constants. Give the domain for each. For xex and ex2, identify why the rules taught so far do not directly finish the calculation. Finally correct: “the derivative of lnx is ln1=0”.

Discussion of Activity 5.10

(a) 42x5+12x−4, x≠0. (b) 1/(2x)+1/x−2ex, x>0. (c) 5cosx−2/cos2x, excluding π/2+kπ for integers k. (d) aex+2bx, all real x. The first unfinished expression is a product of changing functions; the second is an exponential applied to an inner square. A derivative is an operation on a function's local variation, not substitution of 1 for its input. The logarithm's derivative is 1/x, and its derivative at 1 is 1 even though its value there is zero.

At this point, a compact rule table is useful, but your explanation should still identify the family and any domain restriction. In Lesson 3, we will see why a product needs two terms and why a quotient's denominator is squared. Those structures are consequences of comparing nearby values, not arbitrary additions to remember.
