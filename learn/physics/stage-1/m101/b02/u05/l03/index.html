M101 · Unit 5 · Lesson 3

Products and quotients

When two factors change, a rate must account for both changes.

Adding derivatives works for a sum because the output changes add. It is tempting to extend that pattern to multiplication and division. A single counterexample shows why we must not: x·x=x2 has derivative 2x, whereas multiplying the two derivatives gives only 1. We need to return to the change in the complete expression.

Four hours includes 75 minutes reading/examples, 100 minutes activities, 35 minutes correction and a reserved 30 minutes for cumulative unit exercises. Keep the unsimplified rule substitution visible until you have checked every term.

5.11 Why two changing factors need two terms

Imagine a rectangle whose side lengths are u and v. If they change by Δu and Δv, the area change is

Δ(uv)=(u+Δu)(v+Δv)−uv=vΔu+uΔv+ΔuΔv.

The first two terms describe strips along the sides. The final term is the small corner needed to complete the enlarged rectangle. This picture is drawn for positive changes, but the algebraic identity also holds for signed changes. If the side lengths depend differentiably on an input x, divide the whole equation by its increment h. The last term becomes (Δu/h)Δv. The first factor approaches u′ and the second approaches zero, so that term vanishes in the derivative limit.

The two strip terms remain. Each combines the rate of one factor with the current value of the other. A derivative of a product therefore needs information about both values and both rates at the same input.

Key idea 5.6 · Product rule

If u and v are differentiable at x, then

ddx(uv)=u′v+uv′.

Differentiate one factor at a time, retaining the other, and add the two contributions. The product of the derivatives alone is generally incorrect.

The rectangle is a motivation, not a restriction to positive lengths or geometrical functions. The limiting algebra establishes the rule for any two real differentiable functions. Notice also the use of continuity: differentiability makes Δv tend to zero. We do not merely declare the corner “small” and discard it without examining what happens after division by h.

Activity 5.11 · Values and rates (15 minutes)

At one input, u=3, v=5, u′=2 and v′=−1. Find the derivative of uv. Explain how a product can be increasing even when one factor is decreasing. Give a second choice of v′ that would make the product's derivative zero with the other three values unchanged.

Discussion of Activity 5.11

The derivative is 2(5)+3(−1)=7. Growth of the first factor contributes 10; reduction of the second contributes −3. The total is positive. Setting 10+3v′=0 gives v′=−10/3. Values and rates are separate pieces of information: replacing u′v+uv′ by u′v′ would ignore the current factors and predict the wrong result.

5.12 Using and checking the product rule

A useful first line identifies the factors and their derivatives. This is especially helpful when signs or brackets make mental bookkeeping difficult. Only after the substitution is correct should you expand or factor. Simplification is a choice made to clarify the answer or support its next use.

Example 5.8 · Two routes provide a check

Differentiate F(x)=(x2+2)(3x−1).

Working. Set u=x2+2, v=3x−1. Then u′=2x and v′=3, giving

F′(x)=2x(3x−1)+3(x2+2)=9x2−2x+6.

As an independent algebraic route, expand the original expression: F(x)=3x3−x2+6x−2. Differentiating term by term gives the same result. Agreement of these different routes is a useful check of the rule application, although either route could still contain an arithmetic error if not read carefully.

Example 5.9 · A product that does not become a polynomial

Find the derivative of g(x)=x2lnx.

Working. The domain is x>0. The changing factors are x2 and lnx, so

g′(x)=2xlnx+x21x=2xlnx+x.

At x=1, the logarithm term vanishes but the second term does not: g′(1)=1. The original value g(1)=0 gives no reason for its rate to vanish. Factoring as x(2lnx+1) is equivalent and retains the same positive domain.

A wider view · A collecting surface and a changing signal

In an ideal collection model, suppose the collected power is P(t)=A(t)I(t), with effective area A in square metres and incident power per unit area I in watts per square metre. If both vary, then P′=A′I+AI′. Each contribution has units of watts per second. The product A′I′ instead has units of watts per second squared, exposing the error even before a numerical example. A real radio receiver needs additional modelling; this supplied relation isolates the mathematics of two changing factors.

Activity 5.12 · Product fluency (25 minutes)

Differentiate (a) (2x+1)(x2−4) and check by expanding first; (b) xex; (c) (x+2)sinx; (d) xx for x>0, checking with a single power. Explain which derivatives can be usefully left factored.

Discussion of Activity 5.12

(a) 2(x2−4)+(2x+1)2x=6x2+2x−8; expansion of the original gives 2x3+x2−8x−4. (b) ex+xex=ex(1+x). (c) sinx+(x+2)cosx. (d) x+x/(2x)=32x, agreeing with differentiating x3/2 on x>0. Factoring (b) displays a common exponential; (c) has no comparably useful common factor. At zero the original product exists, but the product-rule calculation using the root's derivative is unavailable. A separate right-hand check gives zero; do not present it as an ordinary two-sided derivative on this domain.

5.13 Deriving the quotient rule

For a ratio q=u/v, changes in the denominator affect every part of the ratio. To derive its rule directly, compare nearby values and place the two fractions over a common denominator:

q(x+h)−q(x)h=u(x+h)v(x)−u(x)v(x+h)hv(x+h)v(x).

Subtracting and adding u(x)v(x) in the numerator groups it as [u(x+h)−u(x)]v(x)−u(x)[v(x+h)−v(x)]. Divide each change by h, then take the limit. Both denominator factors approach v(x), which must be nonzero. The result is

Key idea 5.7 · Quotient rule

(uv)′=u′v−uv′v2,v≠0,

provided u and v are differentiable at the point. Keep the subtraction order: derivative of the numerator times the denominator, minus the numerator times derivative of the denominator.

Why is the nearby denominator also nonzero? Because differentiability implies continuity. If v(x) is a fixed nonzero number, sufficiently nearby values stay away from zero. This is the same nonzero-denominator reasoning used in the limit quotient law in Unit 4. The squared denominator and the minus sign both arise from the common-denominator subtraction.

Example 5.10 · Subtract the complete second term

Differentiate q(x)=(x2+2)/(x−1).

Working. The domain excludes 1. Choose u=x2+2, v=x−1, so u′=2x, v′=1. Then

q′(x)=2x(x−1)−(x2+2)(x−1)2=x2−2x−2(x−1)2,x≠1.

The minus sign applies to both terms inside x2+2. At zero, q′(0)=−2. Reversing the numerator's two contributions would give the opposite rate; denominator squaring does not remove that sign error.

Activity 5.13 · Ratios and a trigonometric result (20 minutes)

Differentiate x/(x+2), ex/x, and tanx=sinx/cosx. Give every domain restriction. Use sin2x+cos2x=1 to simplify the last numerator.

Discussion of Activity 5.13

The results are 2/(x+2)2 for x≠−2; ex(x−1)/x2 for x≠0; and (cos2x+sin2x)/cos2x=1/cos2x where cosx≠0. For tangent, subtracting sinx(−sinx) produces a plus. This derives the rule used in Lesson 2 and explains why the excluded inputs remain excluded.

5.14 Simplification and domain preservation

Sometimes algebra makes the quotient rule unnecessary. For x≠0, the expression (x3+2x)/x equals x2+2. Its derivative is therefore 2x on the original domain x≠0. The shorter formula is easier to calculate, but the missing input has not been restored. The function defined by x2+2 on all real inputs would be a continuous extension, not the identical original function.

Example 5.11 · Two valid forms, one domain

Find the derivative of r(x)=(x2−4)/(x−2).

Working. Factor the numerator: r(x)=x+2 for x≠2. Hence r′(x)=1 on that punctured domain. Alternatively, the quotient rule gives

r′(x)=2x(x−2)−(x2−4)(x−2)2=(x−2)2(x−2)2=1,x≠2.

Neither route gives r′(2), because r(2) does not exist. Assigning r(2)=4 would create a different, extended function whose derivative at 2 is 1. The derivative formula alone does not record that change of definition.

Do not cancel terms across an addition sign. In (x2+2)/(x+2), neither the x2 nor the constant 2 is a common factor of the whole numerator and denominator. Long division or an explicit identity can still help: x2+2=(x+2)(x−2)+6, so the quotient is x−2+6/(x+2) for x≠−2. At this stage the final reciprocal is a composition, so the quotient rule may be the more direct available route.

Activity 5.14 · Repair a cancellation (20 minutes)

A solution says (x2+x)/x=x+1, so its derivative is 1 for every real x. Correct it. Then differentiate (x3−x)/x and (x2+1)/x by simplifying first, and verify one with the quotient rule. Why is cancelling the x in (x+1)/x to obtain 2 invalid?

Discussion of Activity 5.14

The first formula and derivative are valid only for x≠0. The next functions simplify to x2−1 and x+1/x on x≠0, giving 2x and 1−1/x2. For the last one, the quotient numerator is 2x·x−(x2+1)=x2−1, giving the same derivative. In (x+1)/x, division gives 1+1/x, not 2. Only a factor multiplying the whole numerator can be cancelled against the denominator.

5.15 Choosing a route through a calculation

Rule choice is not determined by the first symbol that catches your eye. Read the complete expression. Is it a sum of terms, a product of factors or a ratio? Could a simple identity reduce it? Write the restriction that makes that identity valid. Then identify each derivative you need before substituting into the outer rule.

For a product of three factors, treat two as a temporary group. Applying the product rule twice gives (uvw)′=u′vw+uv′w+uvw′. Each contribution differentiates exactly one factor. For example, differentiating xexsinx gives exsinx+xexsinx+xexcosx. There is no need to invent a new principle for three factors.

Activity 5.15 · Decide, calculate, check (20 minutes)

Differentiate (a) (x2+1)ex; (b) lnx/x; (c) (x2−1)/(x+1); (d) xsinxcosx. State the domain and explain your first rule choice for each. At x=1, check (b) directly from the quotient rule using the values of its two factors and their derivatives.

Discussion of Activity 5.15

(a) Product: ex(2x+x2+1), all real inputs. (b) Quotient: (1−lnx)/x2, x>0; at 1 this is (1·1−0·1)/1=1. (c) Simplification gives x−1 and derivative 1, retaining x≠−1. (d) Three-factor product: sinxcosx+xcos2x−xsin2x, all real inputs in radians. Leaving the last two terms grouped as x(cos2x−sin2x) is equivalent. A correct route includes its reason and domain, not just the final expression.

We can now differentiate combinations in which familiar functions sit alongside one another. In the next lesson one function will sit inside another. Products require adding separate contributions; composition requires following how one change is passed into the next.
