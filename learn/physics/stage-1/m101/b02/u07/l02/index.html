M 101 · Unit 7 · Lesson 2

Numerical derivatives

Two values determine a secant slope. Whether that slope tells us about a derivative is a further question.

Lesson 1 began with a known derivative and used it to predict a nearby value. Now suppose that a formula, a simulation or a table supplies function values, while the derivative is inconvenient to obtain. We can approximate a rate by dividing an output difference by an input difference. The calculation is short; interpreting the number requires care about the sample locations, the domain and the existence of the derivative.

Three hours: 45 minutes reading/examples, 50 minutes written activities and feedback, one hour Python including correction, and 25 minutes reserved for the unit exercises. Computing requires P 101 functions, loops and lists. All required code uses Python 3's standard library.

7.6 The forward difference

Choose a positive step h and compare the outputs at a and a+h. The slope of the line joining these two graph points is

D+(h)=f(a+h)−f(a)h.

This is called a forward difference approximation to f′(a). Its numerator is the output change and its denominator is the full input change. It has derivative units. At any fixed nonzero step it is a secant slope, not the limit in the derivative definition. If f is differentiable at a, exact forward differences approach f′(a) as h tends to zero through positive values.

The subscript + identifies the side of the base point used. We take h>0 throughout this lesson so that “forward” and “backward” remain unambiguous. Both required inputs must lie in the domain. A function evaluation outside its domain is not a numerical approximation to an allowed value.

Example 7.4 · A finite step leaves a trace

Estimate the derivative of f(x)=x2 at a=2 using h=0.2 and then h=0.1.

At the larger step, the estimate is (2.22−22)/0.2=4.2. At the smaller step it is (2.12−22)/0.1=4.1. The exact derivative is 4. Expanding before substituting explains both errors:

D+(h)=(2+h)2−4h=4h+h2h=4+h.

Halving the step halves the error in this example. The positive error is expected: the square function becomes steeper as we move right, so the secant includes slopes larger than the starting slope. This is an exact algebraic result for the square, not yet a universal error rule.

Activity 7.6 · Keep the step in the calculation

Find the forward difference for f(x)=3x2−x at a=1 in terms of h. Use it at steps 0.1 and 0.05. State the exact derivative and explain the observed error change.

Discussion of Activity 7.6

f(1)=2 and f(1+h)=2+5h+3h2, so D+(h)=5+3h. The two estimates are 5.3 and 5.15, compared with f′(1)=5. The errors are 0.3 and 0.15. They halve because the exact error is 3h, not because halving a step always halves every numerical error.

7.7 Looking on both sides

A forward difference uses an interval whose left endpoint is the target input. A symmetric comparison uses one sample on each side. The input separation is now 2h, giving the central difference

D0(h)=f(a+h)−f(a−h)2h.

The base input a is at the centre of the secant interval. Its output need not be evaluated, but the derivative we are trying to approximate is still the derivative at a. Replacing the denominator by h would double the slope. Draw the two input positions whenever this is unclear.

When a derivative exists at a, the central difference also converges to it in exact arithmetic. To see the connection, split the numerator by adding and subtracting f(a):

D0(h)=12(f(a+h)−f(a)h+f(a)−f(a−h)h).

The first term inside the brackets is a forward slope. The second is a backward slope, measured with a positive input separation. Both approach the derivative when it exists, so their average does too. The converse is false: an average can settle even when the two sides disagree.

Example 7.5 · Symmetry cancels one error

For f(x)=x2 at 2, the two values are 4+4h+h2 and 4−4h+h2. Subtracting cancels the equal h2 terms, leaving 8h. Division by 2h gives exactly 4 for every positive step in exact arithmetic.

That fortunate result does not make the formula exact for every function. For g(x)=x3 at 1, expanding the two cubes gives

D0(h)=(1+3h+3h2+h3)−(1−3h+3h2−h3)2h=3+h2.

The exact derivative is 3, so a remaining error is h2. A step of 0.1 gives 3.01, and 0.05 gives 3.0025. Halving the step quarters the error here. Lesson 3 will use these exact identities to separate mathematical approximation error from computing effects.

Key idea 7.2 · State the sample locations

Forward: a and a+h, denominator h. Central: a−h and a+h, denominator 2h. Require h>0 and all sampled inputs in the domain. Convergence to the derivative follows when the derivative exists; numerical agreement does not establish its existence.

Activity 7.7 · A wrong denominator

A program estimates the derivative of x3 at 1 by dividing f(1.1)−f(0.9) by 0.1. Calculate its answer, identify the error, and give the correctly scaled central estimate. Would repeatedly halving the step repair the incorrect formula?

Discussion of Activity 7.7

The output difference is 1.331−0.729=0.602. Division by 0.1 gives 6.02, but the input separation is 0.2, giving 3.01. The incorrect formula is twice the central difference, so in exact arithmetic it approaches 6 rather than the derivative 3. A sequence can converge consistently to the wrong answer when the implemented formula is wrong.

7.8 Boundaries and corners

Near a domain boundary, a symmetric sample can leave the allowed interval. For a function supplied only on [0,1], a central estimate at 0.98 with h=0.05 requests the forbidden input 1.03. One option is a smaller step that stays inside. Another is a backward difference, using [f(a)−f(a−h)]/h. At the boundary itself, a one-sided estimate can target a one-sided rate, but it should be labelled that way; it does not create an ordinary two-sided derivative where the domain supplies no other side.

A more subtle problem occurs when all values exist but the derivative does not. For f(x)=|x| at zero and every h>0, the forward slope is 1, the backward slope is −1, and the central difference is

|h|−|−h|2h=0.

The central estimates are perfectly stable. Nevertheless, the two one-sided slopes disagree, so f′(0) does not exist. Symmetry cancels the disagreement instead of resolving it. This is why a numerical check needs mathematical information about the point being tested.

Activity 7.8 · Stable is not enough

For r(x)=1+|x−2|, calculate forward, backward and central differences at a=2 for an arbitrary positive step. A report says, “The central derivative is zero to every printed digit, so 2 is a stationary point.” Diagnose both parts of that reasoning.

Discussion of Activity 7.8

The constant 1 cancels from every difference. The forward estimate is 1, the backward estimate −1 and the central estimate 0. The ordinary derivative does not exist because the one-sided limits disagree. Thus 2 is not a stationary point under our definition, which requires an existing zero derivative. It is an interior critical input and a minimum, but those facts follow from the absolute-value model, not from treating the symmetric estimate as a derivative.

7.9 Implementing a check you can explain

The functions below express the two formulas directly. The argument f is a Python function: passing it lets the same difference rule evaluate different mathematical models. The calls f(a + h) and f(a) evaluate that model at the indicated inputs. This extends the P 101 function pattern without introducing an unexplained numerical solver.

Use finite real inputs and steps. Both functions reject nonpositive steps; raise ValueError stops the calculation with the displayed explanation rather than returning a misleading number. They also reject an input addition that rounds back to the original input. The latter is a finite-arithmetic issue we will examine in Lesson 3. This guard does not detect every source of inaccuracy. Domain restrictions and differentiability remain the caller's responsibility.

def forward(f, a, h):
    if h <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Use a positive step.")
    if a + h == a:
        raise ValueError("The forward input did not change.")
    return (f(a + h) - f(a)) / h

def central(f, a, h):
    if h <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Use a positive step.")
    if a + h == a or a - h == a:
        raise ValueError("A sampled input did not change.")
    return (f(a + h) - f(a - h)) / (2 * h)

def cube(x):
    return x ** 3

for h in [0.2, 0.1, 0.05]:
    dp = forward(cube, 1.0, h)
    dc = central(cube, 1.0, h)
    print(h, dp, dc, abs(dp - 3.0), abs(dc - 3.0))

The last two printed columns are absolute errors against the independently derived derivative 3. They are not errors against another finite-difference output. Python may display values slightly different from hand-calculated decimals because of finite precision. Do not round the intermediate function values to force agreement.

Activity 7.9 · Predict, implement, test (60 minutes, including correction)

Use the starter notebook or companion script. Before running, predict the central estimates from Example 7.5 and derive the forward expression for the cube at 1. Run from a fresh kernel or a new script process. Then define a linear function, a square and an absolute-value function and test both methods at one stated input for each. Include the corner at zero. Record expected results before the output, compare them, and finish with a short explanation of what the checks establish.

Hint for Activity 7.9

For the cube, expand (1+h)3−1 before dividing by h. A linear function should give its slope with either rule in exact arithmetic. For the corner, test the two sides separately on paper.

Discussion of Activity 7.9

The cube's forward expression is 3+3h+h2, so the three forward predictions are 3.64, 3.31 and 3.1525. The central predictions are 3.04, 3.01 and 3.0025. For example, f(x)=2x+1 should return 2 with either rule, while x2 at 2 gives 4+h forward and 4 central. Small floating-point residuals are possible. For |x| at zero the outputs 1 and 0 reproduce the formulas but do not establish a derivative. A good report includes the model, input, steps, predicted values, observed values, any differences and the corner limitation. Other linear slopes and smooth inputs are valid tests if their expected answers are worked out independently.

If you obtain values near twice the target with the central rule, inspect the denominator. If every function returns the same answer, check that the function argument is actually being called. Restarting and running top to bottom helps expose an old definition left in a notebook's memory.

7.10 Reading a finite table honestly

A table can reveal a pattern, but its conclusions should name the tested cases. “The errors decreased at these three steps” is supported by the cube calculation. “The smallest possible step is always best” is not. A correct implementation can still produce a poor approximation when the step, point or function is unsuitable.

With measured values, we may not be free to choose any step we like. Suppose positions at times 1.8 s and 2.2 s are 4.1 m and 5.3 m. The secant velocity is (5.3−4.1)/(2.2−1.8)=3ms−1. This is an average rate across the interval. If an appropriate smooth position model describes the motion, it may be useful as an estimate of the velocity at the midpoint, 2 s. The two readings alone neither establish that model nor determine the exact midpoint derivative.

Activity 7.10 · Report what was observed

A table gives f(0.9)=2.42, f(1)=2.50 and f(1.1)=2.63. Find the forward, backward and central slopes at 1 using the available samples. Explain what extra assumptions would be needed to treat one as an estimate of f′(1), and whether the table proves that the derivative exists.

Discussion of Activity 7.10

The forward slope is 1.3, the backward slope 0.8, and the central slope (2.63−2.42)/0.2=1.05, their average. Treating these as derivative estimates requires a function or physical model with suitable local behaviour, and consideration of reading errors and the 0.1 step. Many functions, including ones with a corner, can pass through three points. The table does not establish differentiability or a rigorous error bound. Disagreement between the one-sided slopes is useful evidence to investigate, not by itself proof that the limiting slopes disagree.

A wider view · Samples come from somewhere

With an analytic formula, we can request another input in code. With an existing measured record, an intermediate input may have no corresponding measurement. Inventing an interpolated point does not add a new observation. Keep the distinction between available samples and an assumed continuous model when interpreting a difference quotient.

The next lesson keeps the formula fixed while varying the step over a much wider range. Exact polynomial identities will tell us what should happen without arithmetic error. Comparing that prediction with actual computation will make the competing effects visible.
