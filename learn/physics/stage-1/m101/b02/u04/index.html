M101 · Block 2 · Unit 4

Functions, limits
and continuity

Suppose a sensor reading is missing at one instant. A model may suggest how to fill the gap, but only if its nearby values support one value. What if the model approaches different values from either side? That difference is the starting point of this unit.

A rule may be perfectly usable near an input where its formula cannot be evaluated. Conversely, a function can have a value at an input while behaving quite differently nearby. We need to separate three questions: which inputs are allowed, what outputs are approached, and whether the value at the point agrees with that approach. Functions, limits and continuity supply the corresponding language.

The image is a reminder that a displayed number has a context: what is measured, when, and on which scale. A mathematical function lets us specify such a dependence precisely. Its domain says which inputs we are describing; its rule says how to obtain an output. A model may predict between observations, but those predictions remain consequences of the model's assumptions.

We begin by retrieving domains, graphs and composition. Composition will feel familiar after matrix transformations: the output of one operation becomes the input of another, and order matters. We then ask what happens near a chosen input without insisting on evaluating at that input. This leads to finite limits and to separate approaches from the left and right. Algebra will turn several graphical or numerical suggestions into justified conclusions. Finally, continuity will connect the limiting value to the actual value.

You should already be comfortable substituting into expressions, rearranging equations, factorising simple quadratics, using fractions and reading coordinate graphs. Lesson 1 retrieves the function notation and inequalities needed here. The one-hour Python investigation uses scalar arithmetic, lists, loops, conditions and returning functions from P101 Unit 1; it needs no arrays or plotting library. If that preparation is pending, complete the written work first and return to the coding activity after the handover.

What you are working towards

By the end, aim to specify a real function with its domain, form a composition with its restrictions, distinguish a function value from a finite limit, use one-sided limits to diagnose failure, calculate elementary limits with justified algebra, test continuity and explain what a numerical table cannot prove.

The 16 hours include reading, attempts, feedback, correction, Python and reference work. Lesson 1 has four hours; Lessons 2 and 3 have three and a half hours each; Lesson 4 has five hours, including the unit exercise set and review. These are editorial study estimates, not measured completion times. Pause to attempt activities before revealing their discussions. When the unit is complete, its final exercises will ask you to choose methods across the unit, with full solutions hidden until requested. Only this introduction and Lesson 1 are available in the current release; later teaching and closing resources are in preparation.

No derivative rules are required yet. In Unit 5, a difference quotient will ask us to take precisely the sort of limit developed here. Understanding why an expression can approach a sensible value while remaining undefined at one input will make that next step much less mysterious.
