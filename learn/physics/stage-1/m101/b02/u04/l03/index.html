M101 · Unit 4 · Lesson 3

Calculating limits

A plausible number becomes a mathematical conclusion only when the steps leading to it are justified.

Lesson 2 used tables and graphs to suggest what nearby values might do, then used simple identities or bounds to explain the approach. We now organise that reasoning into reliable calculation methods. The algebra is familiar; the new care lies in deciding where it is valid and why it preserves a limit.

Three and a half hours: about 75 minutes of reading and worked examples, 85 minutes of your own calculations, and 50 minutes of feedback and correction. Write domain restrictions beside your working. A calculator may check an ordinary input, but it should not decide which algebraic steps are permitted.

4.12 Building limits from simpler ones

A constant function has the same output at every input, so its limit is that constant. The identity function simply returns its input, so as the input approaches a, its output does too:

limx→ac=c,limx→ax=a.

To move beyond these two starting points, consider a function built by adding and multiplying simpler functions. If the separate outputs settle near definite finite numbers, addition and multiplication preserve that settling. Division needs more care: dividing by values approaching zero can magnify small differences without bound.

Key idea 4.7 · Arithmetic of finite limits

Suppose f(x)→A and g(x)→B as x→a, with A and B finite. On a common deleted neighbourhood of a—an interval around a with a itself omitted—the following laws apply:

limx→a(f(x)+g(x))=A+B,limx→a(f(x)−g(x))=A−B,limx→acf(x)=cA,limx→af(x)g(x)=AB.

For a quotient there is an additional condition:

limx→af(x)g(x)=ABprovided B≠0.

The same laws apply to an available one-sided approach when the component limits are taken from that same side. These are results we use here; a full formal proof belongs to a later treatment of analysis.

The condition on the quotient has a concrete purpose. If g(x) approaches 4, then sufficiently near the target its values can be kept, for example, between 3 and 5. Its reciprocal remains controlled. If g(x) approaches zero, that protection disappears. The quotient law then says nothing about the answer; it does not say the answer must fail to exist.

Example 4.10 · Substitution with a reason

Find limx→2(3x2−x+1)/(x+2).

Working. The identity limit gives x→2. The product law then gives x2→4, and the sum and constant-multiple laws give 3x2−x+1→12−2+1=11. The denominator approaches 2+2=4, which is nonzero. Therefore the quotient law applies:

limx→23x2−x+1x+2=114.

The function excludes x=−2, but that excluded input is not near the target 2. No difficulty arises from it in this calculation. The result is the same number obtained by direct substitution because the component laws justify that substitution here.

A polynomial is built from constants and nonnegative integer powers by finitely many additions and multiplications. Repeated use of the laws therefore makes its limit at a equal to its value at a. A quotient of polynomials may likewise be evaluated by substitution wherever its denominator at the target is nonzero. This is a useful first test, not a rule to apply regardless of conditions.

We will also use the square-root limit rule: if f(x)→A≥0 and f(x)≥0 throughout the available nearby domain, then f(x)→A. When A=0, closeness follows by requiring 0≤f(x)<e2 to make the root less than e. When A>0, the identity

|f(x)−A|=|f(x)−A|f(x)+A

bounds the difference by |f(x)−A|/A, which becomes small with the numerator. This explains both the result and the need for a real, nonnegative radicand. It also introduces an identity that will help with a more difficult limit shortly.

Activity 4.15 · Which law is available?

Find the limits as x→−1 of (a) 2x2+3x−4; (b) (x2+2)/(x−2); (c) x+5. For (b), state the condition permitting division. If instead you know only that f(x)→0 and g(x)→0, which can you determine: the limit of f+g, of fg, or of f/g?

Discussion of Activity 4.15

(a) 2−3−4=−5. (b) The numerator approaches 3 and denominator −3, so the limit is −1; the denominator’s limit is nonzero. (c) The radicand approaches 4 and is positive near −1, so the limit is 2. For the two unspecified functions, f+g→0 and fg→0. Their quotient is not determined by those two facts; the quotient law’s nonzero condition fails.

A wider view · Calculation and checking

NASA’s account of Katherine Johnson’s work describes her verification of electronic-computer calculations for John Glenn’s orbital flight. That historical example is a reminder that producing a number and checking its grounds are distinct tasks. Our limit calculations are far simpler, but they need the same habit of asking which assumptions and operations support the answer. A matching decimal is useful evidence; it is not a substitute for an explanation.

4.13 Cancelling without filling a hole

Suppose numerator and denominator both approach zero. Writing “0/0” records the failed substitution, but it is not an answer and is not an arithmetic operation we may carry out. It does not mean zero, one or infinity. Different quotients can have very different behaviour even though both separate parts approach zero.

For instance, as x→0, the quotients x/x, x2/x and x/x2 all give the same failed substitution. For nonzero x they simplify respectively to 1, x and 1/x. Their behaviour is a limit of 1, a limit of 0, and no finite two-sided limit. The unprocessed “0/0” form has not distinguished them.

The way forward is often to find a common factor. We may cancel it at nearby inputs where it is nonzero. The resulting expression can make the approach clear even though it has a different value or domain at the centre.

Example 4.11 · Two exclusions, one relevant neighbourhood

Find the limit at 2 of r(x)=(x2+x−6)/(x2−4), keeping track of the original domain.

Working. The denominator factors as (x−2)(x+2), so the original domain excludes both 2 and −2. At 2, numerator and denominator both approach zero. Factor the numerator rather than dividing those two zeros:

x2+x−6x2−4=(x−2)(x+3)(x−2)(x+2)=x+3x+2,x≠2,−2.

For inputs in, say, (1,3) other than 2, the equality is valid. That is all we need for this limit. The simplified denominator approaches 4, so

limx→2r(x)=limx→2x+3x+2=54.

The original function remains undefined at 2. At −2, cancellation has not removed the denominator problem at all; a different target requires a new local check. A manipulation useful at one input need not settle every question about the function.

Key idea 4.8 · Nearby equality preserves a limit

If two rules agree for every input sufficiently near a, except possibly a itself, they have the same limit there whenever that limit exists. Record the domain on which the algebraic equality holds. A limit can be found without assigning a missing point value.

Cancellation applies to a common factor multiplying the whole numerator and denominator. It does not apply to a term inside a sum. For example, (x2+4)/x is x+4/x for x≠0, not x+4. Multiplying the proposed simplification by the denominator is a quick way to expose this error.

Example 4.12 · Expand before cancelling

Find limh→0((2+h)2−4)/h.

Working. Expand the square before looking for a factor:

(2+h)2−4=4+4h+h2−4=h(4+h).

For h≠0, division by h gives 4+h. Hence

limh→0(2+h)2−4h=limh→0(4+h)=4.

There is no substitution of zero into the original denominator. Instead, we have shown that every nonzero nearby input has exactly the output 4+h, and then taken its limit. Small positive and negative offsets both give the same approached value.

Activity 4.16 · Keep the restriction in the answer

Find (a) limx→−2(x2+5x+6)/(x+2) and (b) limh→0((3+h)2−9)/h. In each case give the simplified expression and where the simplification is valid. A learner claims that the first limit proves the original function has value 1 at −2. Correct the claim.

Hint for Activity 4.16

For (a), find two numbers with product 6 and sum 5. For (b), the cross term in the square is essential.

Discussion of Activity 4.16

(a) The numerator is (x+2)(x+3), giving x+3 for x≠−2 and limit 1. (b) The numerator is 6h+h2, giving 6+h for h≠0 and limit 6. The first original function is undefined at −2. Giving it value 1 would define an extension; the limit identifies a natural candidate for that extra value but does not create it.

4.14 Removing a radical difference

A square root can hide a cancellable factor. Ordinary polynomial factorisation may not expose it, but the difference-of-squares identity can. For quantities A and B,

(A−B)(A+B)=A2−B2.

The expressions A−B and A+B are called conjugates in this setting. Multiplying a fraction’s numerator and denominator by the same nonzero conjugate keeps its value unchanged. The aim is to turn a difference of roots into a difference of their radicands, where cancellation may become possible.

Example 4.13 · A small difference of square roots

Find limh→09+h−3h.

Working. The real domain is h≥−9 with h≠0. Inputs on both sides of zero are available. Substitution gives zero in both numerator and denominator, so it does not finish the calculation. Multiply by the conjugate 9+h+3, which is positive throughout this domain:

9+h−3h=(9+h−3)(9+h+3)h(9+h+3).

The numerator becomes (9+h)−9=h. Since h≠0, it cancels:

9+h−3h=19+h+3,h≥−9, h≠0.

The new denominator approaches 3+3=6, a nonzero number. The limit is therefore 1/6. The original rule remains undefined at zero, while the simplified expression can also be evaluated there. That extra evaluation belongs to the extension, not to the original function.

There are two logically distinct operations here. Multiplying by a nonzero expression divided by itself preserves the fraction. Cancelling h then uses the fact that we are approaching zero through nonzero offsets. Writing both conditions prevents a useful algebraic method from becoming a mysterious instruction to “get rid of the root”.

The alternative form also matters for numerical work. When h is very small, 9+h and 3 are nearly equal. Their computed difference can lose significant information through rounding. The rearranged expression avoids that subtraction. We will investigate this difference in Lesson 4. At this stage the limit follows from the exact identity, regardless of what a particular calculator displays.

Activity 4.17 · The coefficient survives

Find limh→0(4+3h−2)/h. State the original domain and show why the numerator after multiplication by the conjugate is 3h, not h.

Discussion of Activity 4.17

The domain is h≥−4/3, h≠0. Multiplying by 4+3h+2 gives numerator (4+3h)−4=3h. For nonzero h, the quotient equals 3/(4+3h+2), so the limit is 3/4. The conjugate is at least 2 and therefore nonzero on the real domain. Losing the factor 3 would change every nearby value, not merely the missing value at zero.

4.15 Combining fractions and choosing a method

A common factor may also be hidden by subtraction between fractions. In that case, put the terms over a common denominator before trying to cancel anything. Keep the outer denominator visible: subtracting two fractions and then dividing their difference is a two-stage operation.

Example 4.14 · A quotient containing a difference

Find limh→012+h−12h.

Working. The original expression excludes h=−2 and h=0. For other inputs, combine the numerator:

12+h−12=2−(2+h)2(2+h)=−h2(2+h).

Now divide this entire difference by h:

12+h−12h=−h2h(2+h)=−12(2+h),h≠−2,0.

The denominator tends to 4, so the limit is −1/4. A sign check helps: for small positive h, 1/(2+h) is less than 1/2, so the numerator is negative while h is positive. For small negative h, the numerator is positive and h is negative. The quotient is negative on both sides near zero, consistent with the result.

Method selection starts with the original domain and target, not with a list of tricks. If substitution is allowed by the laws, there is no need to make the expression more complicated. If both numerator and denominator approach zero, inspect how the zeros arise: a polynomial factor, an expanded square, a difference of roots, or a difference of fractions suggests a different first move. If the numerator instead approaches a nonzero number and the denominator approaches zero, ordinary cancellation cannot remove that imbalance; investigate sign and unbounded behaviour.

Activity 4.18 · Choose, calculate, justify

Find each finite limit if it exists. First write one sentence choosing your method: (a) limx→1(x2+2)/(x+3); (b) limh→0(1/(3+h)−1/3)/h; (c) limx→3(x2−9)/(x−3); (d) limx→11/(x−1)2. Explain why applying the quotient law directly to (d) is invalid.

Discussion of Activity 4.18

(a) Direct substitution is justified by a denominator limit of 4; the answer is 3/4. (b) A common denominator gives numerator −h/[3(3+h)], so after division by h the rule is −1/[3(3+h)] for h≠−3,0, with limit −1/9. (c) Factor the difference of squares, leaving x+3 for x≠3, with limit 6. (d) The numerator is 1, while the denominator approaches zero through positive values. Outputs are unbounded above; there is no finite limit. The quotient law requires a nonzero denominator limit.

Activity 4.19 · Repair the reasoning, not just the answer

A solution says: “At x=0, x2/x gives 0/0. Cancel the zeros to get 1, so the limit is 1.” Identify two distinct errors and write a correct replacement solution. Then give a different quotient whose numerator and denominator both approach zero but whose limit really is 1.

Discussion of Activity 4.19

Zero cannot be cancelled as a nonzero factor, and direct evaluation at zero is not defined for this quotient. For x≠0, x2/x=x, so the limit is 0. By contrast, x/x=1 for every nonzero input and therefore has limit 1. Both examples produce the failed substitution 0/0; the surrounding algebra, not the two zeros, determines the answer.

4.16 Absolute values and one-sided algebra

Some expressions must be simplified differently on different sides of the target. Absolute value is an important example. The identity |u|=u holds for u≥0, while |u|=−u holds for u<0. Forgetting that change can make a jump disappear on paper even though it remains in the function.

Example 4.15 · A factor with a changing sign

Find the one-sided limits of b(x)=(x2−4)/|x−2| at 2, and decide whether its two-sided limit exists.

Working. The original domain excludes 2. Factor the numerator as (x−2)(x+2). On the right, x−2>0, so |x−2|=x−2 and b(x)=x+2. On the left, x−2<0, so |x−2|=−(x−2) and b(x)=−(x+2). Hence

limx→2+b(x)=4,limx→2−b(x)=−4.

The two-sided limit does not exist. Cancelling x−2 directly against |x−2| would have silently assumed that we were on the right. Writing the branch conditions first makes the two valid cancellations clear.

This combines the lessons so far. Domain restrictions tell us which inputs are allowed. Branch conditions tell us which local formula applies. Algebra then finds each approached value. Finally, the one-sided-limit criterion decides whether those values describe one two-sided limit. None of these checks can be replaced by assigning a convenient value at the excluded input.

Activity 4.20 · Same method, different outcome

Compare u(x)=(x−2)2/|x−2| and v(x)=(x−2)/|x−2|, both defined for x≠2. Find both one-sided limits for each function. Explain why the presence of an absolute value does not itself imply that the two-sided limit fails.

Discussion of Activity 4.20

For u, the right-hand rule is x−2 and the left-hand rule is −(x−2); both approach zero, so the two-sided limit is 0. Equivalently, u(x)=|x−2| away from 2. For v, the right-hand rule is 1 and the left-hand rule is −1, so no two-sided limit exists. The decisions come from the actual branch values, not from the appearance of the absolute-value signs.

You now have a small set of methods that can be combined rather than memorised as unrelated procedures. A valid calculation states the local domain, exposes the useful structure, retains any exclusions and identifies the law that finishes the limit. In the final lesson, we ask when that limiting value matches the function’s assigned value, and we test what computer arithmetic can add to—or obscure in—our reasoning.
