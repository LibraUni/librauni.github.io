M101 · Unit 13 · Lesson 1 · 4 hours

Choose and connect representations

We begin with one journey and several ways to describe it. Each change of representation must preserve the question's meaning. A useful first habit is to name the input and output before doing any algebra.

Allow 85 minutes for the unit introduction, reading and examples, 85 minutes for activities and reconstruction, and 40 minutes for feedback. Reserve 30 minutes for the unit exercises in Lesson 4.

13.1 Specify a planar journey

A tracker follows an object in a horizontal plane. Choose a fixed origin, with perpendicular axes pointing east and north. The supplied position model is

x(t)=ut,y(t)=wt−kt2,0≤t≤4 s,
u=2 ms−1,w=4 ms−1,k=1 ms−2.

Both coordinates are measured from the same origin and are in metres. The object starts there at time zero. The plane, axes and origin remain fixed throughout the observation. We take these functions as the model on the stated interval; they supply all the motion information needed here. In numerical calculations we insert the value of time in seconds, so the coordinate values become x=2t and y=4t−t2.

The position vector collects the two coordinates in their agreed order:

r(t)=(utwt−kt2).

The vector makes it convenient to perform the same operation on both components. For example, a displacement between times a and b is r(b)−r(a). Subtracting positions cancels the common origin. Its components retain signs, because a change towards the west or south is different from a change towards the east or north.

Example 13.1 · Position, displacement and a chord

Find the displacement from 1 s to 3 s and its magnitude. Explain what the magnitude measures.

Evaluate the positions first, keeping the time attached to each:

r(1)=(23)m,r(3)=(63)m.

Subtract the earlier vector from the later one:

Δr=(40)m,|Δr|=42+02 m=4 m.

The endpoints have the same north coordinate. The displacement is therefore 4 m east. Its magnitude is the straight-line separation of the endpoints, represented by a chord of the path. The object also visits (4,4) m at 2 s, so its route between the endpoints is curved and its travelled distance is greater than 4 m.

Before calculating a requested “change”, decide whether it means a directed displacement, the change in one coordinate or distance along a route. These quantities can share units while giving different answers.

Activity 13.1 · What survives a new origin?

A second observer uses the same axes and metres but chooses an origin 10 m east and 5 m south of ours. Write the second observer's coordinates at 1 s and 3 s. Then find the displacement between them and explain the comparison.

Discussion of Activity 13.1

The new coordinates are x′=x−10, y′=y+5. They are (−8,8) m and (−4,8) m. Subtracting gives (4,0) m again. Both positions shift by the same constant vector, which cancels in their difference. This is why an origin must be stated for a position even though the displacement between two specified events is unchanged by this fixed translation.

13.2 Recover coordinates with a matrix

Suppose a recording system reports two channels rather than east and north coordinates. Its calibration is supplied as p=x+y and q=x−y, with all four quantities in metres. The channels are linear combinations of the coordinates. Place them in the column vector z:

z=(pq)=Ar,A=(111−1).

The first row adds the coordinates; the second subtracts north from east. The columns give the readings produced by one metre east and one metre north respectively. Thus a unit east displacement changes both channels by +1 m, while a unit north displacement changes them by +1 m and −1 m. This interpretation supplies a simple check on the matrix entries.

To recover position, solve the two equations. Addition gives p+q=2x; subtraction gives p−q=2y. Hence

r=Bz,B=12(111−1).

Multiplication gives BA=I, the identity matrix, because the unscaled matrix squared is 2I. This explains why recovery works for every input vector. The factor one half is essential: multiplying by A twice doubles both coordinates rather than restoring them.

Example 13.2 · Decode, then check forward

The tracker reports p=7 m and q=−3 m. Find the coordinates and decide whether this reading lies on the supplied journey.

x=7+(−3)2=2 m,y=7−(−3)2=5 m.

The forward check gives x+y=7 m and x−y=−3 m, so the decoding is internally correct. However, the trajectory has x=2 m only at t=1 s, where y=3 m. The decoded point is not on this model path. A correct inverse calculation and agreement with a physical model are distinct checks; the mismatch could prompt examination of the reading, calibration or motion model.

Key result 13.1 · Decode the quantity before using its geometry

For this supplied calibration, r=Bz, with BA=I. Channel values are not east–north components. In particular, p2+q2=2(x2+y2): the channel-vector length is 2 times the position-vector length.

The length relation follows by expanding (x+y)2+(x−y)2. The cross terms cancel, leaving 2x2+2y2. Although the channels have length units, taking their ordinary vector magnitude without accounting for calibration would give the wrong physical distance. Units are a necessary check, but they do not identify a quantity by themselves.

Activity 13.2 · Two ways to check the recovery

Calculate the channels at 2 s and recover the position. Then suppose the measured channels contain additive errors δp=0.2 m and δq=−0.2 m. Find the resulting coordinate errors without recalculating the entire journey.

Discussion of Activity 13.2

At 2 s, r=(4,4) m, so (p,q)=(8,0) m and recovery gives (4,4) m. Linearity means errors transform by the same matrix: δx=(δp+δq)/2=0, δy=(δp−δq)/2=0.2 m. Opposite channel errors cancel in the east coordinate and reinforce in the north coordinate. This is an exact relation for the fixed calibration, not a small-change approximation.

13.3 Read a path and its component graphs

To draw the path, plot (x(t),y(t)) for successive times. To draw a component graph, plot (t,x(t)) or (t,y(t)). The same numbers appear, but the horizontal axes have different meanings. A slope in a position–time graph is a velocity component; a slope in the path diagram compares northward and eastward changes.

The east coordinate increases uniformly. The north coordinate first increases, reaches 4 m at 2 s, then decreases. The path nevertheless continues east throughout. Its highest point is therefore not a stopping point. The five time markers show both the order of travel and equal one-second intervals. Their unequal spatial separations are an early clue that the speed is not constant.

We can eliminate time because x=2t is one-to-one on the interval. Substituting t=x/2, with numerical values in seconds and metres, gives

y=2x−x24,0≤x≤8.

This equation is a relation between coordinate values expressed in metres. It describes the shape and domain of the path. The original position functions also tell us when the object reaches each point. If another object traversed the same curve twice as slowly, the path equation would be unchanged while the position–time functions would differ.

Activity 13.3 · Recover what a graph can tell you

Sketch both component–time graphs for 0–4 s. Mark the positions at 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 s on the path sketch. Which representation answers each question most directly: (a) when is the object farthest north; (b) what is the shape of its route; (c) how long does it take to move from east coordinate 2 m to 6 m? Explain why a bare path curve is insufficient for (c).

Discussion of Activity 13.3

The east graph is the line from (0,0) to (4,8); the north graph passes through 0, 3, 4, 3, 0 m at the five times. Use the north–time graph for (a), giving 2 s, and the path diagram for (b), a downward-opening parabolic arc. The east–time graph gives 1 s and 3 s for (c), an elapsed 2 s. The route alone supplies positions but no timetable.

13.4 Choose a representation for the question

A representation is useful because it makes a particular operation or pattern accessible. A table is good for tracing a calculation at selected inputs. A graph makes shape and signs visible. A formula supports exact differentiation and integration. A matrix keeps a calibration or transformation consistent across many vectors. None of these choices removes the need to identify what the output means.

Example 13.3 · Reach a target region

During which times is the object at least 3 m north and no more than 6 m east of the origin?

The conditions concern coordinates, so use the coordinate functions rather than channel magnitudes. The north condition is

4t−t2≥3⟺(t−1)(t−3)≤0.

The product is non-positive between its roots, giving 1≤t≤3. The east condition 2t≤6 gives t≤3. Intersect these with the model domain [0,4]. The answer is [1,3] s, including the boundaries. A sketch checks the interval: the object enters through north coordinate 3 m at 1 s, remains north of that line, and reaches the east boundary at 3 s.

The example uses algebra to find exact boundary times and a sketch to check their interpretation. Repeating the algebra in a second notation would be less informative than checking that the plotted segment lies in the specified region.

Activity 13.4 · Plan before calculating

For each task, state the quantity, a suitable representation and an independent check: recover position from two channel readings; estimate motion over the next 0.1 s; find net northward change over the full journey; find total travelled distance. Complete the first and third tasks for the model. Leave a method plan for the other two until Lesson 2.

Discussion of Activity 13.4

Use the inverse matrix for channel recovery and check by applying the forward calibration. For the full journey, the north change is y(4)−y(0)=0 m; the north graph confirms equal endpoint heights. A short prediction should use the current rate and be checked against the exact position function. Distance requires accumulating the magnitude of velocity, so that changes in direction do not cancel. The endpoint separation gives a useful lower check on that distance, but not its value.

We now have a consistent chain from channel readings to position and from position to the geometry of a journey. In the next lesson, timing becomes central: differentiation describes the motion locally, while integration reconstructs its accumulated consequences.
