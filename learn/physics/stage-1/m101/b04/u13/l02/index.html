Local rates and accumulated change

The path shows where the object goes. To describe how it moves, we must bring time back into the calculation. We will use differentiation on each position component, then ask what information integration can recover.

Allow 65 minutes for reading and examples, 55 for written activities, 30 for feedback and 60 for the Python exploration. Reserve 30 minutes for the unit exercises.

13.5 Differentiate the position components

Over a time interval h, the average velocity is the displacement divided by elapsed time. Using the vector notation from Lesson 1 gives

r(t+h)−r(t)h=(x(t+h)−x(t)hy(t+h)−y(t)h).

As the interval shrinks, each component approaches its ordinary derivative. Thus the instantaneous velocity is the column of coordinate rates. This uses the differentiation you already know twice, with both derivatives taken at the same time.

v(t)=r′(t)=(uw−2kt)=(24−2t)ms−1,

where the final expression uses the numerical time in seconds. Eastward motion remains steady, while the northward component decreases, becomes zero at 2 s and then becomes negative. The velocity vector points along the path in the direction of travel. A northward component of zero means that motion is momentarily eastward; it does not imply zero velocity.

Speed is the magnitude of velocity. Perpendicular physical components give

s(t)=|v(t)|=u2+(w−2kt)2.

The symbol s denotes speed here, in metres per second. Since u=2 m/s, the speed can never fall below 2 m/s. It reaches that minimum at 2 s, when its north component is zero. It has the same value at times equally spaced either side of 2 s, because squaring removes the sign of that component.

Example 13.4 · Direction at one instant

Find velocity and speed at 1 s and interpret the slope of the path there.

v(1)=(22)ms−1,s(1)=22 ms−1.

The equal positive components point northeast. Since the east component is nonzero, the path slope is the ratio of the coordinate rates, vy/vx=1. Differentiating the path equation y=2x−x2/4 gives dy/dx=2−x/2, also 1 at x=2 m. This checks the direction through a geometric representation that eliminates time. The path slope is dimensionless; the speed has units of metres per second.

Activity 13.5 · Turn without stopping

Find velocity, speed and path slope at 3 s. Compare them with their values at 1 s. Explain which sign changes and why the speed does not.

Discussion of Activity 13.5

At 3 s, v=(2,−2) m/s, speed 22 m/s and path slope −1. The object moves southeast. The north component has changed sign, while the east component is unchanged; the sum of squared components is therefore unchanged. The comparison concerns two instants, not a claim of constant speed between them.

13.6 Use a tangent prediction

Unit 7 used a derivative to predict a small change in a scalar function. Apply the same reasoning to each coordinate:

r(t+h)≈r(t)+hv(t).

The prediction travels along the tangent at the current velocity for time h. It is useful when the interval is short enough that the velocity changes little for the purpose of the question. The approximation should be assessed in position units, because position is the predicted output.

Our polynomial model lets us calculate its error exactly. Expanding the north component at t+h gives

w(t+h)−k(t+h)2=(wt−kt2)+h(w−2kt)−kh2.

The east component is linear and has no remainder. Therefore

r(t+h)−[r(t)+hv(t)]=(0−kh2).

The tangent prediction is north of the true position by kh2. Doubling the time interval quadruples that position error. This is an exact statement for the supplied quadratic model; its derivation uses ordinary expansion, without requiring a general series theory.

Example 13.5 · Decide whether the prediction is adequate

Predict the position at 1.2 s using the velocity at 1 s. Is the predicted position within 0.05 m of the exact model position?

r(1)+0.2v(1)=(23)+0.2(22)=(2.43.4)m.

The exact north coordinate is 4(1.2)−(1.2)2=3.36 m. The east coordinate is 2.4 m in both calculations. The error vector is (0,−0.04) m, whose magnitude is 0.04 m; the requirement is met. For a 0.4 s step the error would be 0.16 m and fail. We have checked the approximation to the supplied model; applying the same tolerance to an observed position would also require accounting for measurement and model error.

Activity 13.6 · A useful step size

At 1 s, find the largest positive step for which tangent-prediction error is at most 0.01 m. Check that the predicted finishing time remains in the model domain. Would the same error formula hold for a negative step that stays within the domain?

Discussion of Activity 13.6

With k=1 m/s², the condition is h2≤0.01 in numerical seconds, so the largest positive step is 0.1 s. Its endpoint is 1.1 s, within [0,4]. The remainder depends on h2, so a negative step of the same magnitude has the same downward position error. The starting and finishing times must both remain in the supplied interval.

13.7 Recover displacement by accumulation

Now reverse the question. If we know the two velocity components throughout an interval, integrate each one. The fundamental theorem identifies the results as coordinate changes:

∫abv(t)dt=(∫abvx(t)dt∫abvy(t)dt)=r(b)−r(a).

A starting position is then needed to recover a finishing position. Integrating velocity alone determines change, just as integrating the pump rate in Unit 12 determined delivered amount rather than the tank's initial contents.

Example 13.6 · Cancellation has a physical meaning

Find the displacement over the full four seconds by integration and check it using endpoints.

Δx=∫042dt=8 m,Δy=∫04(4−2t)dt=[4t−t2]04=0 m.

The vector displacement is (8,0) m. This agrees with r(4)−r(0). In the north component, the positive accumulated change from 0–2 s is 4 m and the negative change from 2–4 s is −4 m. They cancel because the object returns to its original north coordinate. The integral preserves that directed meaning.

The fixed channel calibration also respects accumulation. Since p=x+y and q=x−y, their rates are p′=vx+vy and q′=vx−vy. Applying B to these channel rates recovers physical velocity. We may decode first and then integrate, or integrate channel rates and then decode the channel changes: constant matrix multiplication is built from sums and constant multiples, which commute with integration.

Activity 13.7 · Follow both routes

Find the channel-rate functions and integrate them over 0–4 s. Decode the two accumulated changes with B. Why must the calibration be fixed for the derivative relation used here?

Discussion of Activity 13.7

The numerical channel rates are p′=6−2t and q′=−2+2t, in m/s. Their integrals are 8 m and 8 m. Decoding gives ((8+8)/2,(8−8)/2)=(8,0) m. For a time-varying matrix, differentiating its entries would introduce additional product-rule terms. Our stated fixed calibration makes those derivatives zero.

13.8 Separate distance from displacement

For distance, each short contribution is non-negative: speed multiplied by elapsed time. Accumulating those contributions gives

D=∫04s(t)dt=∫044+(4−2t)2dt.

The last integral uses numerical seconds and produces metres. Its integrand is a square root even though the position components are polynomials. We will estimate it with the trapezoidal method already developed in Unit 11. Exact component integration remains an independent benchmark for displacement, while geometric bounds check whether the distance calculation is plausible.

The endpoint separation is 8 m, so the distance cannot be smaller. More directly, speed is at least 2 m/s throughout the four seconds. It is at most 20 m/s, its value at either endpoint. Thus 8≤D≤420 m. These bounds are deliberately broad but useful for catching a gross error.

Key distinction 13.2 · Accumulate before or after taking a magnitude?

Δr=∫abv(t)dt,D=∫ab|v(t)|dt.

For forward time a≤b, distance D is non-negative and at least |Δr|. Taking the magnitude after integration gives endpoint separation; taking it before integration gives distance travelled.

With four one-second panels, the sampled speeds are 20,8,2,8,20 m/s. The endpoint half-weights give

D4=20+28+2≈12.1290 m.

Keep the square roots unrounded until the final display. The result lies within the bounds, but that alone cannot establish its accuracy. Lesson 3 will vary the resolution, compare a special exact case and trace the calculation to establish what the numerical evidence supports.

Activity 13.8 · Diagnose two totals

A student obtains 8 m by integrating velocity and then taking its magnitude. Another obtains 16 m by adding the absolute east and north coordinate changes along the journey. Explain both quantities and why neither is the distance along the curved path.

Discussion of Activity 13.8

The first is the endpoint separation. The second adds 8 m of eastward travel to 4 m north and 4 m south, treating simultaneous perpendicular changes as if they occurred in separate stages. Actual short-step length uses the square root of the sum of squared components, not their sum. This gives another useful upper bound, D≤16 m, but not equality for this journey.

The distinction between a rate and its magnitude determines what an accumulation means. In the next lesson, we will preserve that distinction in code and use the analytical connections from this lesson to test the implementation.
