M101 · Unit 12 · Lesson 1 · 4 hours

Comparing answers to the same question

Agreement is useful only when the calculations concern the same quantity. We begin by making that comparison precise. The aim is to turn a disagreement into a question that a further calculation can resolve.

Allow 95 minutes for the introductions, explanations and worked examples, 75 minutes for the activities and reconstruction on paper, and 40 minutes for feedback. Reserve the remaining 30 minutes for the unit exercises in Lesson 4. These are study estimates: pause to check a derivation before continuing.

12.1 Specify the quantity and the model

A pump delivers water into a tank. For the first three minutes, its inflow is described by the supplied model

q(t)=a−bt2,a=3 Lmin−1,b=14 Lmin−3.

Time t is measured from the start of pumping. The coefficient units make both terms rates: multiplying b by t2 gives litres per minute. The tank initially contains 10 L, has capacity 20 L and has no outflow during this interval. We assume that all delivered water enters the tank. These statements define the question as much as the formula does.

Let A(T) denote the amount delivered by time T. For 0≤T≤3 min, accumulation gives

A(T)=∫0Tq(t)dt=aT−bT33,V(T)=10 L+A(T).

The upper limit is a variable: A(T) is a function, not just one number. The integration variable t runs from the start to that finishing time. The fundamental theorem gives A′(T)=q(T), so the slope of the accumulated amount is the current delivery rate. The stored volume V has the same slope and a different starting value.

Example 12.1 · Three numbers with two meanings

Find the amount delivered and final stored volume after three minutes. Interpret the candidate answers 6.75 L, 6.625 L and 16.75 L.

Insert T=3 min in the exact accumulated amount:

A(3)=3(3)−(1/4)333=9−94=6.75 L.

The stored volume is therefore 10+6.75=16.75 L. It remains below capacity. The answer 6.625 L differs from the delivered amount by 0.125 L and may be a numerical approximation; its method still needs inspection. The value 16.75 L answers the stored-volume question exactly within the model. Selecting it as a more accurate estimate of the amount delivered would confuse two quantities with identical units.

Before judging a numerical difference, write a sentence naming the output, its units, the interval and the model. This prevents a surprisingly common problem: performing more arithmetic on quantities that were never comparable.

Activity 12.1 · What must change?

A second tank starts with 14 L and has capacity 20 L. The same inflow is proposed for three minutes. Is 14+A(3) an acceptable prediction for the water stored? Identify the failed assumption and what information is needed next.

Discussion of Activity 12.1

The expression gives 20.75 L, exceeding capacity. It still describes initial water plus delivered water, but cannot be the stored volume if overflow occurs. A storage prediction needs an overflow rule: for an open tank that spills immediately, with the pump unchanged, storage reaches 20 L and stays there while the excess escapes. A controller that stops the pump would require a different inflow model. The original no-loss assumption has failed; improving quadrature cannot repair it.

12.2 Build independent checks

An independent check uses information or reasoning that does not simply repeat the most vulnerable step of the first calculation. For A(T), differentiating the proposed antiderivative checks the integration, while evaluating A(0) checks its starting value. Both conditions are needed: adding an arbitrary constant leaves the derivative unchanged.

A graph gives another route into the problem. The inflow remains positive and decreases from 3 to 0.75 L/min over the three minutes. The accumulated amount must therefore increase, with a slope that becomes less steep. A falling graph of accumulated amount would conflict with the rate even before we examined individual values.

We can also compare the whole area with rectangles. Since the rate lies between 0.75 and 3 L/min on an interval of length 3 min, the amount must lie between 2.25 and 9 L. This broad bound would reject 67.5 L, though it cannot distinguish 6.625 from 6.75 L. A check's usefulness depends on the size and kind of defect it can expose.

Example 12.2 · Recover the numerical answer by hand

Use three equal trapezoidal panels to estimate A(3). The panel width is 1 min and the rates at 0, 1, 2 and 3 min are 3, 2.75, 2 and 0.75 L/min. Therefore

Q3=1(32+2.75+2+0.752)=6.625 L.

We have now identified the second candidate. Each straight chord lies below this concave-down rate curve, so the trapezoids underestimate its integral. The sign of the discrepancy is consistent with the geometry. It is not evidence of a code defect: an accurately implemented approximation need not equal the exact answer.

For a tighter bracket, the decreasing rate gives right and left sums of 5.5 L and 7.75 L. The exact result and the trapezoidal value lie between them. The bracket uses monotonicity; the more precise statement that the trapezoid is below the exact value uses concavity.

Key idea 12.1 · A check has a purpose

Use units and scale to test interpretation; an exact special case to test implementation; differentiation and an initial value to test accumulation; a graph or inequality to test sign and behaviour. Explain which possible failure each check addresses.

Activity 12.2 · A derivative check with a missing piece

A proposed accumulated amount is B(T)=aT−bT3/3+2 L. Its derivative equals q(T). Explain why it fails as the amount delivered since pumping began, and say what it could represent.

Discussion of Activity 12.2

At T=0, it gives 2 L, whereas no water has yet been delivered. It could describe stored water in a tank initially containing 2 L, subject to the same no-loss assumptions. Checking the derivative establishes the rate of change; the starting value establishes which accumulation function is intended.

12.3 Read a discrepancy

Let Q be an approximate answer and I the exact value for the specified mathematical problem. The signed discrepancy Q−I tells us the direction of the error; the absolute error |Q−I| tells us its size. If I≠0, dividing by |I| gives a dimensionless relative error. A percentage is that ratio multiplied by 100.

For the three-panel calculation, Q3−I=−0.125 L. Its absolute error is 0.125 L and its relative error is 0.125/6.75=1/54, about 1.85%. These calculations compare with an exact benchmark. If the exact value were unknown, a difference between two approximations would be a different kind of evidence.

Unit 11 derived the trapezoidal error for a quadratic. We can apply that reasoning here without assuming a general error law. On a panel of width h, the linear and constant parts are integrated exactly; the term −bt2 contributes error −bh3/6. With n equal panels and h=T/n, their errors add to

Qn−A(T)=−bT36n2.

At T=3 and b=1/4, this is −1.125/n2 L. Doubling n divides the error by four. Thus Q6=6.71875 L: its discrepancy from Q3 is 0.09375 L, while its remaining error is 0.03125 L. Here one third of the successive difference gives the finer error exactly in exact arithmetic, because we know the quadratic error formula.

This distinction matters when you report accuracy. “The last two answers differ by 0.094 L” is an observation. “The finer answer is within 0.032 L of the model integral” requires the error argument. “The real tank receives that amount within 0.032 L” would additionally require evidence about the flow model and its inputs.

Activity 12.3 · Decide the resolution from a requirement

For the same three-minute calculation, choose the smallest positive integer panel count that makes the absolute trapezoidal error less than 0.01 L in exact arithmetic. Explain why testing only three and six panels does not establish this requirement.

Hint for Activity 12.3

Use the explicit error formula, then check the integer on either side of the threshold.

Discussion of Activity 12.3

We need 1.125/n2<0.01, or n2>112.5. The smallest integer is 11: the error is approximately 0.00930 L. Ten panels give 0.01125 L and fail. The six-panel error is 0.03125 L. Refinement has improved the answer but has not yet met the requested tolerance.

12.4 Choose the next test

A useful next test distinguishes plausible explanations. Suppose a program gives exactly 6.75 L at every resolution. It might use the analytical formula, a special exact rule, or a hard-coded answer. Agreement at this one input does not tell us which. Change a case whose correct behaviour you can predict: use a constant rate, change the interval, or change a coefficient.

For a constant rate c, the integral on [u,v] is c(v−u). This benchmark exposes missing widths and incorrect total weights. A nonzero starting point checks whether code constructs nodes as u+ih, rather than ih. Reversing the integration bounds checks sign. A nonconstant linear function tests sample placement while retaining an exact trapezoidal answer. These tests build confidence for different reasons.

Example 12.3 · Two checks that share a mistake

A program calculates the trapezoidal sum. Its “check” calls the same function a second time, stores the result under a new name and subtracts the two answers. The difference is zero.

This confirms repeatability for that run, but the two results share the same sample positions, weights and interpretation. A missing factor of h would survive unchanged. A better first test is the integral of the constant 2 on [1,4], which is 6. A hand calculation obtains this result independently of the program's loop. Then use f(t)=t on [1,4], whose integral is 7.5, to test the shifted sample positions.

Activity 12.4 · Match a discrepancy to an investigation

Propose a next check in each case: (a) an answer is larger by a factor of 60; (b) two programs agree on a negative amount delivered by the positive pump rate; (c) refinement changes an answer very little, but measured inflow and the model differ systematically. Explain what each check would establish.

Discussion of Activity 12.4

(a) Inspect time units and rate units: using seconds with a rate in litres per minute can introduce a factor of 60. Trace a single panel with its units. (b) Check bounds and signs, then compare against the positive rectangle bound. Shared agreement cannot overturn that bound. (c) Inspect the input calibration and model fit against observations. Further refinement addresses the integral of the supplied formula, so it will not remove a persistent difference between that formula and the observations.

The lesson's central habit is to state the mathematical question before testing the answer. Once the quantities match, use checks with different vulnerabilities and interpret each discrepancy at the level where it arose. Next we will keep the method fixed and change the inputs, asking how much stability we should expect from the problem itself.
