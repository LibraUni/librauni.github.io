Sensitivity, scale and useful precision

A correct calculation responds to its inputs. Sometimes a small input change barely affects the answer; sometimes it changes the conclusion. To interpret that response, we must distinguish the sensitivity of the mathematical problem from the accuracy of the method used to solve it.

Use 75 minutes for the reading and examples, 50 minutes for written activities, 25 minutes for feedback and one hour for the computational investigation. Reserve 30 minutes for the cumulative unit exercises. Keep the baseline and each changed input visible in your working.

12.5 Change an input deliberately

Return to the supplied pump model q(t)=a−bt2, with a=3 L/min and b=0.25 L/min³. At a fixed finishing time T, the delivered amount is A=aT−bT3/3. Suppose the rate coefficient a is increased by δa, with b and T unchanged. Subtracting the old prediction from the new one gives

ΔA=(a+δa)T−bT33−(aT−bT33)=Tδa.

This relation is exact because A depends linearly on a. At three minutes, increasing a by 0.02 L/min increases the amount by 0.06 L. Changing b alone gives ΔA=−T3δb/3. A larger b makes the rate fall more rapidly, so the negative sign has a direct interpretation.

To study an input's effect, change it while holding the others fixed. If you change the input and the numerical resolution together, a difference in the output combines two causes. A baseline table makes that confusion easier to avoid: record the varied input, all fixed inputs, the method and the result.

Example 12.4 · A bounded input range

Suppose the admissible coefficient ranges are 2.98≤a≤3.02 L/min and 0.245≤b≤0.255 L/min³, with all combinations allowed. Find the range of delivered amounts at exactly three minutes.

At this time, A=3a−9b. It increases with a and decreases with b. The minimum therefore uses the smallest a and largest b; the maximum uses the opposite corner:

Amin=3(2.98)−9(0.255)=6.645 L,
Amax=3(3.02)−9(0.245)=6.855 L.

The baseline 6.75 L lies at the centre of this range, with a possible change of 0.105 L in either direction. The lowest admissible rate occurs at the final time with a=2.98, b=0.255: it is 0.685 L/min, still positive. The model remains an inflow throughout the tested interval.

This is a worst-case interval for the stated input ranges. It assigns no probabilities to the values inside. Correlated or otherwise restricted inputs would require their allowed combinations to be specified before claiming that both extreme corners can occur.

Activity 12.5 · Exact change or numerical error?

At T=3 min, increase b from 0.25 to 0.26 L/min³. Predict the exact change in delivered amount. A three-panel result also changes, from 6.625 to 6.53 L. Explain why its change differs from your prediction.

Discussion of Activity 12.5

The exact change is −9(0.01)=−0.09 L, giving 6.66 L. The three-panel quadrature error is −b/2 L when the numerical value of b uses the stated units, so changing b also changes that error by −0.005 L. Its output changes by −0.095 L. Holding resolution fixed separates input changes from resolution changes, but does not remove the input-dependence of the approximation error.

12.6 Use a derivative to predict a change

Now keep the rate coefficients fixed and change the finishing time. The dependence on T is nonlinear, so a constant multiplier cannot describe every time change exactly. Unit 7 provides a local prediction: for a small change δT,

ΔA=A(T+δT)−A(T)≈A′(T)δT=q(T)δT.

The interpretation is simple. During a short extra interval, the delivered amount is approximately the current rate multiplied by the extra time. If the rate is changing noticeably across that interval, the tangent prediction needs checking.

Example 12.5 · How local is local enough?

At T=2 min, the delivered amount is 16/3 L and the rate is 2 L/min. Estimate the extra delivery if pumping continues for another 0.1 min, then compare with an exact calculation.

The tangent prediction is 2(0.1)=0.2 L. Expanding the cubic in the exact accumulated amount gives

ΔA=(3−T2/4)δT−T(δT)24−(δT)312.

At T=2, δT=0.1, the exact change is 0.2−0.005−0.00008333…=0.19491666… L. The tangent overestimates by about 0.00508 L because the inflow declines during the extra interval. If the acceptable discrepancy is 0.01 L, this approximation meets it for this specified change.

For an extra 0.5 min, the tangent gives 1 L but the exact change is 1−0.125−0.01041666…=0.86458333… L. The discrepancy is now about 0.13542 L. The same tangent is useful over one interval and inadequate over another because adequacy depends on the required accuracy and the size of the input change.

The expansion above is ordinary finite algebra for a cubic. We have retained every term, so it supplies an exact comparison here. For a different function, you may instead compare direct function values with the derivative prediction or use an available bound. A derivative at one point alone does not provide a guaranteed error bound over an interval.

Activity 12.6 · A shorter pumping time

At T=2 min, predict the change when the finishing time becomes 1.9 min. Calculate the exact change and interpret its sign. Does the tangent lie above or below the accumulated-amount curve on this side?

Discussion of Activity 12.6

Here δT=−0.1 min, so the tangent predicts −0.2 L. The exact change is −0.2−0.005+0.00008333…=−0.20491666… L. Less water has been delivered. The tangent prediction of the new total is still above the curve, now by about 0.00492 L. A concave-down curve lies below its tangent locally on both sides; the change's sign and the approximation error's sign answer different questions.

12.7 Cancellation and small residuals

Sensitivity can be large even when the formulas and arithmetic are simple. Two nearly equal quantities may leave a small difference. Suppose an inflow is 5.00 L/min and an outflow is 4.98 L/min. Their net rate is 0.02 L/min. If each rate can independently differ from its stated value by at most 0.01 L/min, the net rate can range from zero to 0.04 L/min. Small changes in the individual rates permit a change as large as the nominal net rate itself.

This is an issue with the information entering the problem. Computing more decimal places in the subtraction does not narrow those input ranges. Floating-point cancellation is a related but distinct arithmetic issue: subtracting rounded nearby values can magnify the importance of their existing rounding errors. In either case, inspect the contributions and their scales.

A similar effect appears in simultaneous equations. Consider the dimensionless system

x+y=2,x+1.01y=2.01.

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives 0.01y=0.01, so y=1, x=1. If only the second right-hand side changes to 2.02, the same subtraction gives 0.01y=0.02, so y=2, x=0. A change of about half a percent in that input has moved the intersection substantially.

The graph explains the algebra. The two lines are almost parallel. Moving one line slightly can shift their intersection a long way along the other line. Division by the small coefficient 0.01 expresses that geometry in the elimination step.

Example 12.6 · A small residual, a large solution error

Test the candidate (x,y)=(0,2) in the original system. The first left-hand side is 2 and the second is 2.02. Define each residual as the left-hand side minus the specified right-hand side. The residuals are 0 and 0.01.

Those are small compared with right-hand sides near 2, but the candidate differs from the exact solution (1,1) by (−1,1). The candidate exactly solves a nearby system: one with the second right-hand side changed to 2.02. Because the original system is sensitive to that change, a small residual alone does not guarantee small errors in x and y.

For contrast, in x+y=2, x−y=0, residuals r1,r2 imply coordinate errors (r1+r2)/2 and (r1−r2)/2. There is no division by 0.01. Small residuals control the coordinate errors much more tightly in this second system.

Key idea 12.2 · Ask what is sensitive

Input sensitivity concerns how the exact answer changes when the specified problem changes. Approximation error concerns how a chosen method differs from the exact answer to a fixed problem. A residual measures failure to satisfy an equation; interpreting it as solution accuracy requires attention to sensitivity.

Activity 12.7 · Diagnose before blaming the solver

Replace 2.01 by 2.009 in the nearly parallel system. Solve it and compare with (1,1). Would agreement between hand elimination and a program show that the original input was accurately measured?

Discussion of Activity 12.7

Subtraction gives 0.01y=0.009, hence y=0.9 and x=1.1. A right-hand-side change of −0.001 causes coordinate changes of 0.1 in magnitude. Agreement would support the solution of the supplied equations. Establishing measurement accuracy requires information about how the right-hand side was obtained.

12.8 Choose a meaningful tolerance

A tolerance states how much discrepancy you are prepared to accept for a particular purpose. An absolute tolerance carries the output units: for example, 0.01 L. A relative tolerance compares a discrepancy with a stated nonzero scale: one part in a thousand of a 10 L amount is 0.01 L. Near zero, the same relative rule becomes impractically strict or undefined, so an absolute allowance is essential.

For two numerical values u and v expressed in the same units, a useful comparison accepts them when

|u−v|≤max(εabs, εrelmax(|u|,|v|)).

The absolute tolerance εabs has those units; the relative tolerance εrel is dimensionless. This is a comparison rule, not evidence that either value is correct. If v is an exact benchmark, it tests numerical accuracy against that benchmark. If both values come from the same flawed formula, passing it establishes little.

Choose the tolerance before looking for a convenient pass. For the pump's three-minute amount, an input range of ±0.105 L and a quadrature error of 0.001 L describe quite different contributions. Reporting six decimal places would not remove the input range. Retain enough digits during calculation, then report the result at a precision that communicates the evidence without hiding a significant discrepancy.

Activity 12.8 · One-hour sensitivity investigation

Use the unit notebook's Lesson 2 prompts, or write a short script from the following starting point. First predict the amount change for each altered coefficient. Then tabulate a=2.98,3,3.02 with b=0.25,T=3, followed by b=0.245,0.25,0.255 with a=3,T=3. Finally compare exact and tangent changes at T=2 for time changes −0.1, 0.1 and 0.5 min.

def delivered(T, a=3.0, b=0.25):
    # Numerical units: minutes, L/min, L/min**3; result in L.
    return a*T - b*T**3/3

baseline = delivered(3.0)
for a in [2.98, 3.0, 3.02]:
    print(a, delivered(3.0, a=a), delivered(3.0, a=a)-baseline)

Record which inputs you held fixed. Check at least one row by hand. Explain why this investigation does not require quadrature, and why computing the same table with a numerical integral would add a second source of discrepancy. The tested times must remain in the model's 0–3 min interval.

Discussion of Activity 12.8

The first amounts are 6.69, 6.75 and 6.81 L. The second are 6.795, 6.75 and 6.705 L. The exact time changes are approximately −0.20491667, 0.19491667 and 0.86458333 L; tangent predictions are −0.2, 0.2 and 1 L. Direct evaluation of the known integral isolates input sensitivity. A useful report states the fixed parameters and compares each result with the predicted sign and scale, rather than treating a printed table as self-explanatory.

We now have a reason to be cautious in two different situations: the calculation may be inaccurate, or the accurately solved problem may be sensitive to its inputs. The next lesson makes that distinction operational by repairing a flawed check and choosing tests that expose its defects.
