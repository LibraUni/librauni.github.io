P101 · Unit 6 · Lesson 4

Potential energy and transfers

Lift an object, leave it at rest on a shelf, then let it fall. While it rests, it has no translational kinetic energy. Yet its arrangement with Earth allows gravity to increase its kinetic energy during the fall. Potential energy describes that arrangement and lets us connect different parts of the motion.

Allow four hours: 60 minutes for explanation, 90 minutes for written work, 60 minutes for Python and 30 minutes for feedback. Use g=9.8 m/s² unless stated otherwise. Heights are vertical coordinates, not distances along a slope.

6.16 Gravity and a change of height

Near Earth's surface, approximate gravity as a constant downward force of magnitude mg. Choose a vertical coordinate h increasing upwards. Its work between two positions is Wg=−mg(hf−hi). Horizontal displacement makes no contribution because gravity has no horizontal component. Within this uniform-field approximation, the gravitational work depends on the endpoint heights rather than the route between them.

Define the change in gravitational potential energy as the negative of gravity's work:

ΔUg=mg(hf−hi)=−Wg.

Rising increases this energy; falling decreases it. We may write Ug=mgh after choosing the height where Ug=0. That zero is a reference, not a place where gravity ceases. A point below the reference has negative potential energy; this is allowed. Only differences enter our predictions, unlike kinetic energy, which cannot be negative.

Example 6.12 · Move the zero, preserve the change

A 0.50 kg object moves from height 0.40 m to 1.60 m above a bench. With the bench as zero, its potential energies are 1.96 and 7.84 J, giving a change of +5.88 J. Choose the initial position as zero instead: the heights become 0 and 1.20 m, and the energies become 0 and 5.88 J. The change is still +5.88 J, and gravity does −5.88 J.

This energy belongs to the Earth–object interaction. Saying “the object's potential energy” is convenient shorthand, but it should not hide the second interacting body. We treat Earth's recoil and its kinetic-energy change as negligible here. The constant-g formula is for height changes small enough that variation of gravity is unimportant; it is not a general orbital potential formula.

Activity 6.16 · Height, route and reference (20 minutes)

A 1.2 kg load goes from 0.50 m to 2.0 m above a floor, either vertically or along a 4.0 m ramp. Find the potential-energy change and gravity work for each route. Recalculate after choosing a zero 3.0 m above the floor. Explain which numbers change and whether the ramp's length belongs in mgΔh.

Discussion of Activity 6.16

Δh=+1.5 m, so the change is +17.64 J and gravity work −17.64 J along either route. With the higher zero, the heights are −2.5 and −1.0 m; the energies are −29.4 and −11.76 J, still differing by +17.64 J. Ramp length is not vertical height. Resistance along the ramp could do additional work, but does not alter gravity's contribution for the same endpoints.

6.17 System choice and mechanical energy

For the object alone, gravity is external and the work theorem says ΔK=Wg+Wother. Substitute Wg=−ΔUg and rearrange:

Δ(K+Ug)=Wother.

This is the same physics expressed using the Earth–object system, whose gravitational interaction is internal and represented by potential energy. The sum Emech=K+Ug is mechanical energy. If other forces do zero net work, mechanical energy is conserved. Do not count gravity's work again on the right once its effect has been included as ΔUg on the left.

Example 6.13 · A descending particle

A particle starts with speed 1.0 m/s and descends through 0.75 m on a smooth fixed track. Assume the track maintains contact, its normal force does no work, and drag is negligible. Then

12mv2=12m(1.0)2+mg(0.75),
v=1.02+2(9.8)(0.75)≈3.96 m/s.

Mass cancels because both relevant energies scale with mass. The result supplies speed at that height, not the time taken or proof that any proposed track shape maintains contact. Those questions can require Newton’s laws as well.

Activity 6.17 · Two accounts, one prediction (20 minutes)

A 0.25 kg particle is projected vertically upwards at 2.8 m/s, with negligible drag. Find its maximum rise using (a) gravity's work on the particle and (b) mechanical energy of particle plus Earth. State the velocity at the highest point, the force there, and why zero speed does not mean zero force.

Discussion of Activity 6.17

Initial kinetic energy is 0.98 J. In (a), −mgh=0−0.98; in (b), 0.98=mgh. Both give h=0.40 m. At the top, velocity is zero for an instant but weight is still 2.45 N downwards. Gravity then makes the velocity downward. Adding gravity work to the mechanical-energy equation would count the same effect twice.

6.18 Dissipation and an energy ledger

A rough surface or the surrounding air can receive energy as a moving object slows. Mechanical energy then need not be conserved, even though a fuller energy account still balances. For our calculations, define Ed≥0 as the amount transferred out of the tracked mechanical-energy account by dissipation over the specified motion. If there is also supplied driving work Win, write

Kf+Ugf=Ki+Ugi+Win−Ed.

This notation is deliberately explicit: Ed is a positive amount, so it is subtracted. It is not an additional subtraction if friction's negative work has already been included in Wother. Use one accounting convention consistently.

Where does Ed go? For a system enlarged to include the contacting surfaces, some may appear as increased internal energy; some energy may leave as sound or other transfers. We cannot assign all of it to the temperature rise of one object without further information. “Dissipated” means unavailable as the tracked organised mechanical energy, not destroyed.

The parachutes bring the surrounding air into the energy account. A decreasing height does not imply that all the gravitational decrease appears as increased vehicle speed. Our simple ledger can represent an independently supplied total transfer, but it does not calculate the changing aerodynamic force shown by this setting.

Example 6.14 · Retain a missing-energy term

A 2.0 kg object starts from rest and descends 1.0 m. An independently supplied estimate says 7.6 J leaves its mechanical-energy account, and there is no driving input. The gravitational decrease supplies 19.6 J, leaving Kf=19.6−7.6=12.0 J. Thus

v=2(12.0)2.0≈3.46 m/s.

The zero-dissipation prediction would be about 4.43 m/s. A lower speed is consistent with the stated dissipative transfer. It is not evidence that the given 7.6 J was measured accurately; that requires checking its source.

Activity 6.18 · Infer a transfer without naming its destination (25 minutes)

A 1.0 kg object starts from rest, descends 0.50 m and reaches 2.0 m/s. No driving work enters. Infer Ed. Repeat for a proposed final speed of 4.0 m/s. Explain why the second result challenges the stated model, and give two possible missing facts rather than claiming that energy conservation has failed.

Discussion of Activity 6.18

Available gravitational energy is 4.9 J. At 2.0 m/s, kinetic energy is 2.0 J, so Ed=2.9 J. At 4.0 m/s, kinetic energy is 8.0 J, implying Ed=−3.1 J. That contradicts the stated dissipation-only account. An unreported initial speed, driving work or release of stored energy could supply the difference; a measurement or unit error could also explain it. The balance diagnoses missing or inconsistent information, not a violation of the general conservation principle.

6.19 Check an impossible prediction

Rearrange the ledger before taking a square root. Define Kavailable=Ki+mg(hi−hf)+Win−Ed. If this quantity is negative, the proposed endpoint cannot be reached under the supplied account. Replacing it silently by zero changes the problem. Taking the absolute value invents energy. The appropriate response is to stop and revisit the assumptions.

A nonnegative endpoint energy is only a necessary check. A particle may encounter a higher barrier earlier on its route or lose contact with a track. Endpoints alone do not prove that the whole motion is feasible. Our computational function will report the energy-compatible endpoint speed and keep that qualification in its documentation.

Example 6.15 · Too much claimed dissipation

Take Example 6.14 but claim 25 J dissipated. Only 19.6 J is available from the specified initial state and drop, so Kf=−5.4 J would be required. There is no real endpoint speed satisfying this account. The code should reject this set of inputs rather than return a plausible-looking number.

Activity 6.19 · Separate endpoint and path checks (25 minutes)

A particle starts from rest at height 1.0 m on a smooth track. Its proposed endpoint is at 0.20 m, but the track first rises to 1.2 m. Neglect drag and any drive. Calculate the energy-compatible endpoint speed, then explain why the route cannot be traversed from the stated initial state. What minimum initial speed would make the 1.2 m height energetically possible?

Discussion of Activity 6.19

The endpoint balance alone gives v=2(9.8)(0.80)≈3.96 m/s. But the particle has insufficient initial energy to reach the earlier crest. A rise of 0.20 m requires initial speed at least 2(9.8)(0.20)≈1.98 m/s. Equality means zero speed at the crest; it is an energetic threshold, not a guarantee of passing the crest or maintaining contact. This is why a successful endpoint calculation cannot certify a complete trajectory.

6.20 A reproducible energy comparison

The function below makes the bookkeeping visible. Heights may be negative because their zero is arbitrary. Initial speed and the dissipated amount must be nonnegative. The optional work input is signed: negative input represents work leaving by an explicitly modelled external mechanism. Do not also include that same transfer in dissipation.

from math import isfinite, sqrt

def endpoint(m, speed_i, h_i, h_f, dissipated, work_in=0.0, g=9.8):
    """SI inputs -> final K (J), speed (m/s); not a path solver."""
    values = (m, speed_i, h_i, h_f, dissipated, work_in, g)
    if not all(isfinite(x) for x in values):
        raise ValueError("Inputs must be finite numbers")
    if m <= 0 or g <= 0 or speed_i < 0 or dissipated < 0:
        raise ValueError("Check mass, g, speed and dissipation")
    k_i = 0.5 * m * speed_i**2
    k_f = k_i + m * g * (h_i - h_f) + work_in - dissipated
    if k_f < 0:
        raise ValueError("Endpoint requires negative kinetic energy")
    return k_f, sqrt(2 * k_f / m)

Activity 6.20 · Verify, vary and reject (60 minutes of Python)

Spend 10 minutes hand-checking Example 6.14, 15 reading and running the function, 20 comparing dissipated amounts 0, 5.0 and 7.6 J, and 15 testing the impossible 25 J case and writing a conclusion. Shift both heights upwards by 100 m and check that predictions do not change. Compare two masses while holding a dissipated amount fixed; explain why mass need not cancel in that comparison. Use the notebook or companion script, then rerun from a fresh kernel or process.

Discussion of Activity 6.20

For mass 2.0 kg, start from rest and drop 1.0 m: the final energies are 19.6, 14.6 and 12.0 J, and speeds approximately 4.43, 3.82 and 3.46 m/s. The 25 J case raises ValueError. A common shift of height zero leaves the difference unchanged. With the same 7.6 J dissipated and mass 1.0 kg, only 2.2 J remains, giving about 2.10 m/s. Holding a fixed number of joules dissipated is different from holding dissipated energy per kilogram fixed. Record which comparison you made.

Energy notebook · Companion script. These are standard-library calculations of constructed scenarios. They do not simulate the path or measure dissipative losses. Your conclusion should state those limits as well as the checked numbers.

We now have two useful conserved quantities with different conditions. Negligible external impulse preserves total momentum. An appropriate energy ledger tracks transfers and conversions, with mechanical energy conserved only when its net transfers vanish. The closing lesson asks you to decide which account is useful before calculating.
