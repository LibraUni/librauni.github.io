P101 · Unit 6 · Lesson 2

Collisions and conservation

During a collision, the contact forces may be large and complicated. Yet the total momentum can change very little. The apparent paradox disappears when we distinguish forces exchanged inside a system from impulse entering from outside.

Allow four hours: 60 minutes of explanation, 90 minutes of written work, 60 minutes for the Python comparison and 30 minutes for feedback. The numerical cases below are deliberately constructed model cases, not experimental observations.

6.6 Internal forces and a conserved total

Take two trolleys, A and B, as a fixed collection of matter. Measure their velocities in the same laboratory frame immediately before and after contact. From Lesson 1, Pfx−Pix=Jext,x. If the external horizontal impulse is negligible to the required accuracy, the total horizontal momentum is approximately conserved:

mAuA+mBuB=mAvA+mBvB.

Here u denotes an initial horizontal velocity and v a final horizontal velocity. Every velocity is signed. The masses stay the same, and we neglect any material leaving the trolleys. The equality is exact within the ideal zero-external-impulse model; actual measurements may only agree within their resolution.

Why can large contact forces disappear from this equation? A's impulse on B and B's impulse on A are opposite contributions to the pair's total. They can change each trolley's momentum substantially while their sum vanishes. They are not small; they are internal. A track force, however, does not cancel merely because it acts during the same encounter.

Example 6.5 · Recover the other velocity

A 0.50 kg trolley A initially moves at +2.0 m/s towards a stationary 1.0 kg trolley B. After contact A moves at −0.40 m/s. Neglect external impulse. The initial total is +1.0 kg m/s, so

1.0=(0.50)(−0.40)+(1.0)vB,
vB=+1.20 m/s.

B gains +1.20 kg m/s while A changes from +1.0 to −0.20 kg m/s, losing 1.20 kg m/s. The opposite changes provide a second check. A's rebound is compatible with a positive total momentum because B carries a larger positive momentum.

Activity 6.6 · Check the inventory first (20 minutes)

Repeat Example 6.5 with an external impulse of −0.10 N s on the pair. Find B's final velocity and each momentum change. Explain why retaining the original +1.20 m/s would imply a different external impulse. Sketch both states with signed arrows.

Discussion of Activity 6.6

The required final total is +0.90 kg m/s. A contributes −0.20, so B contributes +1.10 kg m/s and moves at +1.10 m/s. The changes −1.20 and +1.10 sum to −0.10 kg m/s. The original velocity would preserve the total exactly and therefore imply zero external impulse. A model answer is conditional on its force inventory.

6.7 Sticking and separating

With two unknown final velocities, one momentum equation is not enough. We need another physical fact or measurement. If the trolleys latch together and move as one after contact, their common velocity v supplies that fact. Substitute vA=vB=v and factor the final total:

mAuA+mBuB=(mA+mB)v,
v=mAuA+mBuBmA+mB.

The denominator is the total positive mass. The numerator can have either sign or be zero. For positive masses this common velocity lies between the two initial velocities: it is a mass-weighted average. That is a useful check, not a replacement for setting up the system. It applies to the zero-external-impulse sticking model, not to every collision.

Example 6.6 · A common final velocity

A has mass 0.60 kg and initial velocity +1.5 m/s; B has mass 0.40 kg and initial velocity −0.50 m/s. If they stick with negligible external impulse, the initial total is 0.90−0.20=+0.70 kg m/s. Dividing by 1.00 kg gives v=+0.70 m/s.

A's momentum changes by 0.60(0.70−1.5)=−0.48 kg m/s. B's changes by 0.40(0.70+0.50)=+0.48 kg m/s. A slows; B reverses. Equal and opposite momentum changes do not imply equal velocity changes when the masses differ.

Separation can be handled with the same total balance. If two initially stationary trolleys push apart using an internal release mechanism, their final momenta sum to zero. The lighter trolley must have the larger speed if there are only two moving parts. We do not need a model of the release force to make that prediction, but momentum alone cannot give either speed without additional information.

Activity 6.7 · Two different missing facts (20 minutes)

(a) A 0.20 kg trolley at +3.0 m/s sticks to a stationary 0.40 kg trolley. Find the shared velocity. (b) The same masses start at rest and separate; the 0.20 kg trolley ends at −1.2 m/s. Find the other's velocity. State the extra fact used in each part and explain why “momentum is conserved” alone would not have supplied both final velocities.

Discussion of Activity 6.7

(a) The initial momentum is +0.60 kg m/s; the shared velocity is +1.0 m/s. Sticking supplied equality of final velocities. (b) The final total must be zero: 0.20(−1.2)+0.40vB=0, so vB=+0.60 m/s. A measured final velocity supplied the missing information. Both answers assume negligible external horizontal impulse and account for all moving material in the system.

6.8 Momentum and kinetic energy

Can a collision conserve momentum while changing how much energy is associated with motion? To investigate that question we introduce the translational kinetic energy of a particle:

K=12mv2.

Here v is speed, or a signed one-dimensional velocity whose square gives the same result. Kinetic energy is a scalar and is nonnegative. Its SI unit is kg m²/s², called the joule (J). For several particles, add their individual kinetic energies. Do not square the system's total momentum and treat the result as if every part shared a velocity. The next lesson will derive why this particular squared-speed quantity is connected to work.

Example 6.7 · The total momentum survives, the kinetic energy changes

For Example 6.6, calculate energy before and after using the velocities of each trolley:

Ki=12(0.60)(1.5)2+12(0.40)(−0.50)2=0.725 J,
Kf=12(1.00)(0.70)2=0.245 J.

The decrease is 0.480 J. It is not a loss of total energy from the universe: motion energy can become deformation, internal energy and sound. Our momentum calculation did not track those destinations. We call a collision elastic when the total kinetic energy is unchanged, and inelastic when it is not conserved; sticking is a completely inelastic case. “Completely” does not mean that all kinetic energy vanishes.

A pair can have zero total momentum and substantial kinetic energy. Two equal masses moving in opposite directions provide an immediate example: their momenta cancel, whereas both squared-speed terms are positive. Conversely, an internal mechanism may release stored energy and increase kinetic energy while still conserving total momentum. Momentum and energy are distinct checks.

Activity 6.8 · Diagnose two claims (20 minutes)

For Activity 6.7(a), calculate initial and final kinetic energies. Then consider two 0.50 kg trolleys approaching with velocities +1.0 and −1.0 m/s and sticking. Find both total momenta and kinetic energies. Use your results to evaluate “zero momentum means no energy” and “sticking destroys all kinetic energy”.

Discussion of Activity 6.8

The first case has Ki=0.90 J and Kf=0.30 J: 0.60 J leaves the translational kinetic-energy account. The equal-mass case has zero total momentum before and after, but Ki=0.50 J and Kf=0. Zero total momentum need not imply zero kinetic energy. Complete sticking removes all translational kinetic energy only in a frame where the final joined body is at rest; the first case is a counterexample to the general claim.

6.9 A transparent collision comparison

Our computational task is to test a model, not to manufacture measurements that agree with it. Use the downloadable notebook or identical companion script. Both contain the original constructed cases and the same small function. The inputs are masses in kg and signed velocities in m/s; the outputs keep momentum and energy separate.

from math import isfinite

def sticking(m_a, u_a, m_b, u_b):
    """kg, m/s -> v (m/s), Pi, Pf (kg m/s), Ki, Kf (J)."""
    if not all(isfinite(x) for x in (m_a, u_a, m_b, u_b)):
        raise ValueError("Inputs must be finite numbers")
    if m_a <= 0 or m_b <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Masses must be positive")
    p_i = m_a * u_a + m_b * u_b
    v = p_i / (m_a + m_b)
    p_f = (m_a + m_b) * v
    k_i = 0.5 * m_a * u_a**2 + 0.5 * m_b * u_b**2
    k_f = 0.5 * (m_a + m_b) * v**2
    return v, p_i, p_f, k_i, k_f

The finite-value checks reject missing or infinite numerical inputs. A negative velocity is valid; a nonpositive mass is not. The function predicts a common final velocity under the stated sticking and negligible-external-impulse assumptions. It does not predict the contact duration, maximum force, or detailed deformation.

Activity 6.9 · Model comparison (60 minutes of Python)

Use 10 minutes to reproduce Example 6.6 by hand, 15 to read and run the function, 20 to run the supplied head-on, stationary-target and co-moving cases, and 15 to change one input and write a conclusion. For each case print the momentum residual Pf−Pi and kinetic-energy decrease Ki−Kf. Predict the co-moving result before running it. Test a zero mass and explain the error rather than bypassing it. Save the notebook or script with your explanation.

Discussion of Activity 6.9

The head-on example returns (0.70, 0.70, 0.70, 0.725, 0.245), in the documented order. The stationary-target case from Activity 6.7 returns a common +1.0 m/s, zero momentum residual and energy decrease 0.60 J. If both initial velocities are +1.2 m/s, the common velocity remains +1.2 and kinetic energy is unchanged. This is a limiting algebraic check, not an actual catching-up collision. Tiny residuals near zero can arise from floating-point arithmetic; use a stated numerical tolerance, not an assertion of new external physics. Zero mass raises ValueError as intended.

6.10 Evidence and a qualified conclusion

A zero momentum residual in our function verifies that the implementation follows its algebra. It is not experimental evidence for the assumptions: we constructed the final velocity using the conservation equation. To test a physical collision, we would measure both endpoint velocities independently, document mass measurements, choose a common frame and interval, and estimate external impulse and measurement limitations.

The surface in Figure 6.7 makes another limitation tangible. An impact need not leave two intact objects moving together. Material can be ejected. A boundary that includes only the surviving target after impact omits the momentum carried by that material. A simple sticking model can be a useful first comparison, but it cannot silently stand in for an impact with debris.

Activity 6.10 · Write an investigation conclusion (30 minutes)

A report uses the Example 6.6 masses and initial velocities, with a proposed measured common final velocity of +0.68 m/s. Calculate the final momentum and residual. The stated bound on external impulse is 0.005 N s; the report gives no measurement uncertainties. Explain what the discrepancy establishes, what cannot yet be concluded, and what information you would request. Distinguish this record from the constructed Python benchmark.

Discussion of Activity 6.10

The proposed final total is +0.68 kg m/s, so the residual is −0.02 kg m/s. Its magnitude exceeds the stated external-impulse bound by a factor of four. If the stated velocities and masses were exact and the bound reliable, this record would contradict the zero-impulse approximation at that scale. But measurement uncertainty is missing; the numbers alone cannot diagnose friction, a velocity calibration error or a bad impulse bound. Request uncertainties, frame/axis conventions and before/after timing. The Python benchmark demonstrates algebraic consistency; this proposed independently measured outcome tests agreement with a model. It is an invented report for critique, not real experimental data.
