P101 · Unit 6 · Lesson 3

Work and kinetic energy

Knowing how long a force acts helps us predict a momentum change. Sometimes the available information is a distance instead: a load is lifted through a height, or a trolley stops over a measured length of track. Work gives us a way to connect that displacement with a change in motion.

Allow four hours: 75 minutes of explanation, 135 minutes for Activities 6.11–6.15, and 30 minutes for feedback. No Python is required. Keep a force diagram beside each work calculation.

6.11 Force along a displacement

Begin with a particle subject to a constant force F while it moves through displacement s. Only the force component along the displacement contributes to the work. If F and s are the vector magnitudes and θ the angle between them, define

W=F·s=Fscosθ.

This is the dot product from M101 Unit 2 with a physical meaning. Project the force onto the direction of displacement, then multiply that signed component by the displacement magnitude. Equivalently, in rectangular coordinates, W=Fxsx+Fysy. Use components from the same axes. Work is a scalar, so a negative result is not a spatial direction; it describes the sign of an energy transfer by that force.

The unit is the newton metre, or joule. Compare that with impulse's newton second: force multiplied by distance and force multiplied by time answer different questions. The constant-force restriction means the whole vector is unchanged over the chosen motion. Knowing only an average force in time is not enough to replace it in a work calculation for a variable force. Such work will be developed later in the course.

Example 6.8 · Components make the projection explicit

A constant force has components (6.0,8.0) N. A particle's displacement is (3.0,0) m. The work is 6.0(3.0)+8.0(0)=18 J. The force magnitude is 10 N, but using 10(3)=30 J would count the vertical component as though it acted along the horizontal displacement.

If the displacement were instead (0,3.0) m, the same force would do 24 J. The force alone does not determine the work: the displacement matters as well.

Activity 6.11 · Two routes to a dot product (25 minutes)

A constant force is (4.0,−3.0) N and displacement (2.0,1.0) m. Calculate the work from components. Find the magnitudes of both vectors and infer the cosine of the angle between them from the dot product. Sketch the vectors and check whether the angle should be acute or obtuse. Then reverse the displacement and repeat the work calculation.

Discussion of Activity 6.11

W=4(2)−3(1)=5.0 J. The magnitudes are 5.0 N and 5 m, so cosθ=1/5≈0.447 and the angle is acute, about 63.4°. Reversing displacement gives −5.0 J; the angle becomes obtuse. The radical is a magnitude, so take its positive root. No force component has been discarded: each is multiplied by the corresponding displacement component.

6.12 Positive, negative and zero work

When force and displacement point in the same direction, cosθ=1 and the work is positive. Opposite directions give cosθ=−1 and negative work. Perpendicular directions give zero. Zero work therefore does not imply zero force. A force may act while its point of application stays still, or it may be perpendicular to the motion.

The suspended apparatus in Figure 6.9 helps separate holding from moving. In an ideal stationary hold, the lifting force is nonzero but does no mechanical work on the load because its displacement is zero. During a steady upward lift it does positive work; during a steady lowering the same upward force does negative work. “Steady” means the resultant force is zero, not that each force does zero work.

For ideal uniform circular motion, the inward force is perpendicular to the instantaneous motion at every point. It changes the direction of velocity without changing speed and does no work. Do not multiply its initial direction by the displacement across a large arc: that force vector turns, so it is not one constant vector over the arc. The local perpendicularity explains the result. A tangential driving or resistive component would be a different case.

Example 6.9 · A steady vertical lift

A 2.0 kg ideal load rises steadily through 1.5 m. Take g=9.8 m/s² and neglect other forces. Its upward tension is T=mg=19.6 N. The tension does +(19.6)(1.5)=+29.4 J of work, while gravity does −29.4 J. The total is zero, consistent with unchanged speed.

The work of the lifting force is not zero merely because the total is zero. Naming the force prevents us from conflating one contribution with their sum.

Activity 6.12 · Classify before calculating (25 minutes)

For the same load, find the tension work and gravity work when it (a) remains fixed for ten seconds and (b) is lowered steadily by 0.80 m. Then explain the work done by an ideal horizontal track's vertical normal force on a particle travelling horizontally. State one reason why “zero mechanical work on this load” does not mean an operator uses no energy.

Discussion of Activity 6.12

(a) Both works are zero because displacement is zero. Duration does not enter this calculation. (b) Tension work is −15.68 J and gravity work +15.68 J; their sum is zero. The vertical normal force is perpendicular to the horizontal displacement and does zero work. An operator or machine can have internal energy transfers even when the chosen load does not move. The mechanical calculation is about the stated system, not all processes in its surroundings.

6.13 From Newton to kinetic energy

We introduced K=12mv2 in Lesson 2. To see why the square appears, consider one-dimensional motion of a constant-mass particle under a constant resultant force. Unit 4 supplied the relation v2−u2=2as, with signed displacement s and acceleration a. Multiplying by m/2 and using Fnet=ma gives

12mv2−12mu2=mas=Fnets.

The right side is the net work. The left side is final minus initial kinetic energy. This derivation establishes the connection in the case we already know; the work–kinetic-energy theorem applies more generally to particles when work is calculated correctly along the motion. Here we restrict quantitative calculations to constant forces or stated constant-force segments.

Key idea 6.2 · Net work changes kinetic energy

Wnet=ΔK=12mv2−12mu2.

Add the work of all forces on the particle. Positive net work increases speed; negative net work decreases speed. Kinetic energy cannot be negative. A calculation of speed from energy does not by itself supply the direction of velocity.

For a constant vector force in several dimensions, apply the one-dimensional constant-acceleration relation to each component and add. The sum of squared components is speed squared, and the sum of force–displacement component products is the dot product. This connects the component calculation of Section 6.11 to the same energy statement, without introducing calculus.

Example 6.10 · Recover a speed

A 0.80 kg particle initially has speed 2.0 m/s. The net work during a stated motion is +2.0 J. Initially Ki=12(0.80)(2.0)2=1.6 J, so Kf=3.6 J. Therefore

v=2Kfm=2(3.6)0.80=3.0 m/s.

The positive root gives speed. If the question asks for a signed final velocity, additional directional information is required. Squaring has removed that information.

Activity 6.13 · Test an impossible endpoint (25 minutes)

A 0.50 kg particle starts at speed 4.0 m/s. Find its final speed after net work of (a) +5.0 J, (b) −3.0 J and (c) −5.0 J. Explain the different status of part (c). For a constant 2.0 N opposing force on a straight horizontal path, find the stopping distance and explain why the original opposition model cannot simply continue unchanged after stopping.

Discussion of Activity 6.13

Initially Ki=4.0 J. The final energies for (a) and (b) are 9.0 and 1.0 J, giving speeds 6.0 and 2.0 m/s. Part (c) demands −1.0 J, which is impossible for this model. A 2.0 N opposition removes 4.0 J over 2.0 m. After stopping, continued sliding is no longer established; friction or contact conditions must be reconsidered. A negative square-root argument signals an inconsistent proposed endpoint, not a physical negative speed.

6.14 Several forces, one work balance

Make a force inventory before forming the net work. For a particle moving horizontally, gravity and the normal force may do zero work; an angled pull can do positive work; sliding friction can do negative work. The zero contributions are justified by geometry, not omitted because their forces seem less interesting.

If a pull has an upward component, it also changes the normal force and therefore the simple sliding-friction estimate fk=μkN. Reusing N=mg without checking vertical balance would miss that dependency. Conversely, when a problem gives the friction force directly, use the given value without inventing a coefficient or normal-force calculation.

Example 6.11 · Pulling against a specified resistance

A 2.0 kg particle starts from rest and moves 3.0 m to the right on a level track. A constant 10 N pull acts 60° above the horizontal, and the track exerts a specified 2.0 N leftward resistance. The pull does 10(3.0)cos60∘=15 J. Resistance does −6.0 J; gravity and support do zero work. Thus Wnet=9.0 J and

v=2(9.0)2.0=3.0 m/s.

As a cross-check, the horizontal resultant is 3.0 N, giving acceleration 1.5 m/s². The relation v2=2as gives the same speed. The track can maintain contact: N=19.6−10sin60∘≈10.94 N is positive.

Activity 6.14 · Let the force diagram constrain the energy account (30 minutes)

A 4.0 kg block slides 2.0 m right on a horizontal surface, starting at 1.0 m/s. A 20 N pull acts 30° above horizontal. Use g=9.8 m/s² and μk=0.25. Calculate normal force, sliding resistance, each work and final speed. Explain why using N=mg would give the wrong answer. Check contact and state the assumed direction of sliding.

Discussion of Activity 6.14

Vertical balance gives N=39.2−20sin30∘=29.2 N, so fk=7.30 N. Pull work is 40cos30∘≈34.641 J; friction work is −14.60 J. Other works are zero. Net work is about 20.041 J, added to initial energy 2.0 J. Hence v=2(22.041)/4≈3.32 m/s. The positive normal force supports the contact assumption. Motion remains rightward; the rightward resultant increases its speed. The upward pull reduces support and friction, so N=mg would overestimate the resistance.

6.15 Choosing time or distance

Impulse and work are different ways to organise a force's effect. If the question supplies a time interval and asks for a signed velocity change, impulse may be economical. If it supplies a displacement and asks for speed, work may be economical. Newton’s law remains available, especially when acceleration or individual support forces are the unknowns.

Do not choose by spotting one familiar symbol. First ask whether the needed conditions hold: is the force constant, is an average defined in time, are all relevant forces included, and is the object adequately represented as a particle? For extended deforming bodies, the motion of a force's point of application matters; our simple translational particle account does not automatically describe rotational or internal energy.

Activity 6.15 · Solve one motion two ways (30 minutes)

A 1.0 kg particle begins at +2.0 m/s on a straight track and experiences a constant resultant +3.0 N for 2.0 s. Use impulse to find the final velocity. Then find the displacement from constant-acceleration motion and use work to check the final speed. Explain why multiplying the force by 2.0 s does not give joules, and why using only the final speed to calculate displacement would be incorrect.

Discussion of Activity 6.15

Impulse is +6.0 N s, so v=2.0+6.0/1.0=+8.0 m/s. Acceleration is 3.0 m/s²; displacement is 2(2)+12(3)(2)2=10 m. Work is 30 J; initial energy is 2.0 J, so final energy is 32 J and speed 8.0 m/s. N s is momentum's unit, not energy's. The final speed was not maintained for the whole interval; using 8(2) would overestimate displacement.

Work tells us that gravity can increase kinetic energy during a fall and decrease it during a rise. Next we will reorganise this gravitational contribution as a change in stored potential energy. The physics will be the same, but a larger system boundary will make the bookkeeping more useful.
