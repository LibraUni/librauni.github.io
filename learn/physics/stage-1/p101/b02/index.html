P101 · Physics, space & scientific thinking · Block 2

Motion, forces
and conservation

A position–time record can reveal that a vehicle changed its motion. It does not, by itself, explain why. Was the vehicle pulled by a cable, slowed by contact with the ground, or deflected by a barrier? To move from describing a journey to explaining and predicting it, we need to identify how the moving object interacts with its surroundings.

Block 1 taught us to distinguish a measurement from a model and to make a numerical argument reproducible. We retain those habits here. A force law will give us a new kind of model, connecting interactions to changes in motion. Its usefulness will still depend on the choices around it: which object we are studying, which effects we include, what we neglect and what evidence could challenge the prediction.

Following interactions, then following balances

We begin close to hand: a supported object, a pull transmitted by a rope, a surface that resists sliding. Unit 4 develops force diagrams and Newton’s laws, then uses a constant-acceleration model to predict motion along a line and through the air. The central skill is selecting a physical description before choosing an equation. A careful sketch often exposes a mistaken assumption while it is still easy to correct.

Unit 5 asks how an object can change its velocity while keeping the same speed. Circular motion brings direction into the foreground. Gravity then connects our terrestrial examples to idealised satellites and planets. “Inward” will describe the direction of a resultant force, not name an extra interaction to add to a diagram.

Some questions are easier to answer by comparing before and after than by reconstructing every instant. Unit 6 introduces momentum and energy balances. You will choose the system for which a conservation statement is appropriate, account for transfers, and decide what a balance can determine. These methods complement force calculations; they do not remove the need to specify assumptions.

Unit 7 extends energy accounting to heating and cooling. We will distinguish temperature from internal energy and from energy transferred as heat. We also return to motion after M101 has supplied differentiation, connecting a physical rate of change with its mathematical description. A final model comparison brings together measurement judgement, calculations and a report another person can inspect.

Preparation and study

Bring the signed quantities, units, data handling and checking methods from P101 Units 1–3. M101 Units 1–2 supply vector components and projections; these become physical tools here. The opening mechanics needs algebra and elementary trigonometry, not calculus. Unit 7’s derivative work explicitly requires M101 Unit 5. If that mathematics is not yet in place, complete that prerequisite before the derivative lesson; no earlier force lesson depends on it.

The block has 72 study hours, including 10 hours of Python, divided equally among Units 4–7. Introductions, activities, corrections, each unit’s closing exercises and the formative iCMA 02 all belong within those hours. TMA 02 follows the block with its separate six-hour allocation. Python uses the established local tools to examine algebraic predictions and records; a successful calculation is still only one part of a physical argument.

Keep paper beside the text. For each new situation, practise naming the system, drawing the interactions and stating the approximation before calculating. By the end of the block, the question “Does this answer make sense?” should lead to several specific checks: direction, units, a simple limiting case, a balance and, where available, a comparison with observations. We start by deciding exactly what our diagram is a diagram of.
