P101 · Unit 7 · Lesson 3

Motion as a rate of change

An energy balance connects states. A motion function can describe how a state changes between them. Differentiation lets us read the velocity and acceleration implied by that function, including at an instant when the object reverses direction.

Before starting, complete the derivative introduction and polynomial rules in M101 Unit 5. Allow three hours: 40 minutes for reading, 50 minutes for written activities, 60 minutes for Python and 30 minutes for feedback.

7.10 Position, velocity and acceleration

Choose an origin and a positive direction along a straight track. Let x(t) be the signed position at time t. Over a nonzero interval from t to t+h, the average velocity is [x(t+h)−x(t)]/h. You have used this finite comparison since Unit 3. M101 now supplies the limiting operation that turns it into a local rate:

v(t)=limh→0x(t+h)−x(t)h=dxdt.

The derivative gives the instantaneous velocity of the differentiable position model. Its units follow from the quotient: metres divided by seconds gives m/s. It can be positive, negative or zero. Speed is its magnitude |v|; velocity retains the direction information.

Apply the same reasoning to velocity. The instantaneous acceleration is its rate of change,

a(t)=dvdt=d2xdt2.

The second expression means differentiate position twice with respect to time. Acceleration units are m/s2. This is not position divided by the square of elapsed time: it is a local rate of a local rate. An object can have nonzero position and zero velocity, or zero velocity and nonzero acceleration.

A wider view · A short fall, a useful experiment

With downward position positive and a release from rest, an ideal free-fall model is x(t)=12gt2. Differentiation gives v=gt and a=g. At 2.2 s, with g=9.8m/s2, the predicted distance is about 23.7 m, consistent with the facility’s scale.

The experiment and its surroundings fall together, producing near-weightlessness inside. The facility uses a separate drag shield to reduce the influence of air resistance on the experimental package. Gravity still accelerates the falling system relative to the building.

We will write physical model coefficients with units at first. For example, x0+ut+12at2 adds three lengths: x0 has units m, u has units m/s and a has units m/s². Once a numerical convention is stated—time in seconds, position in metres—we can use a shorter numerical function in code.

Example 7.8 · Differentiate before substituting

A track model is

x(t)=1m+(2m/s)t−(0.50m/s2)t2,0≤t≤4s.

Find velocity and acceleration at 1 s. Treat the coefficients as constants and differentiate each term:

v(t)=2m/s−(1.0m/s2)t,a(t)=−1.0m/s2.

Thus v(1s)=+1.0m/s and a=−1.0m/s2. The object moves in the positive direction while its velocity is decreasing. Substituting t=1 into position first would leave the single value 2.5 m, which contains no function left to differentiate.

Activity 7.10 · Read the coefficients (15 minutes)

For y(t)=2m−(3m/s)t+(0.75m/s2)t2, find velocity and acceleration, then evaluate position, velocity and acceleration at 1 s. State the units of each coefficient and explain why the position’s constant term disappears from velocity.

Discussion of Activity 7.10

The velocity is −3m/s+(1.5m/s2)t; acceleration is +1.5m/s2. At 1 s the three values are −0.25 m, −1.5 m/s and +1.5 m/s². Coefficient units make every position term a length. A constant shift of origin changes positions but not their changes, so it does not alter velocity or acceleration.

7.11 Signs, turning and the shape of motion

Return to Example 7.8. Its velocity decreases linearly from +2 m/s at 0 s to zero at 2 s and −2 m/s at 4 s. Before 2 s the position is increasing; after 2 s it is decreasing. The instant v=0 separates the two directions and is a turning point of this motion.

The acceleration remains −1 m/s² throughout. Before the turn, velocity and acceleration have opposite signs and the speed decreases. After the turn, both are negative and the speed increases. “Negative acceleration” therefore does not mean “slowing down”. The sign of the velocity must also be considered.

On a position–time graph, velocity is the tangent slope. A positive slope can become less positive even while the graph continues to rise. At the maximum position the tangent is horizontal; beyond it the slope is negative. On a velocity–time graph, acceleration is the slope. Its graph can stay below zero throughout without implying that the object always moves in the negative direction.

Key idea 7.4 · A value is not a rate

Position gives location; velocity gives its rate of change; acceleration gives the rate of change of velocity. A zero velocity at one instant does not imply zero acceleration. For one-dimensional motion away from a zero of velocity, equal signs of v and a indicate increasing speed; opposite signs indicate decreasing speed.

Example 7.9 · A return with zero average velocity

For Example 7.8, find average velocity from 0 to 4 s, distance travelled and average speed.

Working. The endpoints both have position 1 m, so displacement and average velocity are zero. The object reaches x(2)=3m, moving 2 m out and 2 m back. Its distance is 4 m and average speed is 1 m/s. These are interval quantities. None says that instantaneous velocity vanishes throughout the journey.

A zero derivative is a candidate for a turn, not a guarantee. For the numerical model z(t)=(t−2)3, with position in metres and time in seconds, v=3(t−2)2 is nonnegative on both sides of 2 s. The object momentarily has zero velocity but continues in the same direction. Inspecting the sign on each side supplies the missing physical information.

Activity 7.11 · Explain the turn (20 minutes)

Use Example 7.8 at times 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 s to make a table of position, velocity, acceleration and speed. Identify intervals of increasing and decreasing speed. Sketch a small direction arrow at each nonzero velocity. Then compare with z(t)=(t−2)3: explain why its zero velocity at 2 s is not a reversal.

Discussion of Activity 7.11

Positions are 1, 2.5, 3, 2.5, 1 m; velocities are 2, 1, 0, −1, −2 m/s; accelerations are all −1 m/s²; speeds are 2, 1, 0, 1, 2 m/s. Speed decreases before 2 s and increases afterwards. In the cubic case, 3(t−2)2 stays positive on both sides, so position continues increasing. A sketch must follow velocity’s sign rather than acceleration’s sign.

7.12 Checking a derivative numerically

For a known function, we can compare the hand derivative with a difference quotient at a small but finite step. A forward difference uses [x(t+h)−x(t)]/h. A centred difference uses values on both sides:

vcentre(t;h)=x(t+h)−x(t−h)2h,h>0.

The denominator is the full interval 2h. The semicolon reminds us that this estimate depends on a chosen step. Both sample times must lie inside the model’s applicable interval. At an endpoint, use a one-sided check or an interior time.

Example 7.10 · A special exact check

For the quadratic in Example 7.8, use numerical seconds and metres. Expanding the forward difference gives 2−t−0.5h. At t=1, steps 0.2 s and 0.02 s give 0.9 m/s and 0.99 m/s, approaching the exact 1 m/s.

For the centred difference, the two quadratic terms proportional to h2 cancel when we subtract. The result is exactly 2−t in exact arithmetic for every allowed h. This special cancellation makes the quadratic a useful benchmark, but does not establish exactness for every function.

For a numerical cubic model q(t)=1+2t−0.5t2+0.1t3, the derivative at 1 s is 1.3 m/s. Expanding the centred quotient leaves 1.3+0.1h2 in the numerical unit convention. At h=0.2 it gives 1.304 m/s; at h=0.02 it gives 1.30004 m/s. This difference is caused by using a finite interval on a curved function.

Measured positions create another issue. Suppose each of the two positions has a rounding contribution bounded by ±0.005 m. Their difference can then be shifted by as much as 0.010 m. In a centred quotient with h=0.1s, that contribution can be as large as 0.05 m/s; with h=0.01s, it can be 0.5 m/s. Decreasing the interval magnifies the effect of position rounding. Those bounds concern the stated rounding alone; calibration and timing may add other effects.

Activity 7.12 · What does a smaller step improve? (15 minutes)

Calculate the centred estimate of the cubic at 1 s for h=0.5 s and h=0.1 s using the expression above. Compare with the exact derivative. For position rounding bounded by ±0.002 m per reading, find the maximum rounding contribution to a centred velocity estimate at h=0.1 s. Explain why “always use the smallest possible step” is a poor rule.

Discussion of Activity 7.12

The estimates are 1.325 and 1.301 m/s. Smaller steps reduce this particular finite-step error. Two ±0.002 m rounding contributions can differ by 0.004 m, giving 0.004/(0.2)=0.02m/s. Small intervals magnify such input limitations. For function evaluations in floating-point arithmetic, subtraction of nearly equal values can also become sensitive to rounding. A useful check compares several sensible steps and retains the hand derivative.

7.13 A motion function another person can use

Keep the position model and its hand-derived velocity and acceleration as separate named functions. State the units in the names or documentation, and check the time interval. The following code evaluates the quadratic model; it does not estimate velocity from observations.

from math import isfinite

def motion(t):
    """Input seconds; return x (m), v (m/s), a (m/s**2)."""
    if not isfinite(t) or not 0 <= t <= 4:
        raise ValueError("Use 0 <= time <= 4 seconds")
    x = 1 + 2*t - 0.5*t**2
    v = 2 - t
    a = -1.0
    return x, v, a

def centred_velocity(t, h):
    if not isfinite(h) or h <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Step must be finite and positive")
    x_plus = motion(t+h)[0]
    x_minus = motion(t-h)[0]
    return (x_plus-x_minus) / (2*h)

print(motion(1.0))
print(centred_velocity(1.0, 0.2))

The outputs are (2.5,1.0,−1.0) and approximately 1.0. The index [0] selects the returned position. Calling motion for both shifted times automatically checks that they remain in the interval. An attempted centred check at t=0 fails because one sample would lie before the model’s start.

Activity 7.13 · A reproducible derivative check (60 minutes, Python)

Download the motion notebook or companion script. Reproduce your five-row table; compare the centred velocity at 1 s for steps 0.5, 0.2 and 0.02 s; and test the forbidden endpoint case. Add your own cubic position and hand-derived velocity functions, then repeat the step comparison. End with a labelled position/velocity plot and a short explanation of what changed between the quadratic and cubic checks.

Discussion of Activity 7.13

The quadratic checks agree with 1 m/s apart from floating-point rounding. For the cubic, the derivative function is 2 - t + 0.3*t**2; the three estimates at 1 s are 1.325, 1.304 and 1.30004 m/s. The difference shrinks with step size here. Both position and velocity plots need their own vertical units; use separate panels rather than treating metres and metres per second as one quantity. A correct derivative check establishes consistency of the model and code. Testing the motion itself requires observations.
