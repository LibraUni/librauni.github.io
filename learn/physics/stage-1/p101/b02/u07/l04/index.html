P101 · Unit 7 · Lesson 4

Testing competing motion assumptions

A model can be internally consistent and still miss an observation. We will compare two descriptions of stopping motion against a documented historical record, keeping the calculations transparent and the conclusions tied to what the record actually tells us.

Allow four hours: 60 minutes for reading and examples, 90 minutes for written activities, 60 minutes for Python and 30 minutes for feedback. The historical dataset is used to study model comparison, not to give present-day driving guidance.

7.14 Make the comparison fair

The cars dataset distributed with R contains 50 observations of speed in miles per hour and stopping distance in feet. R’s documentation identifies the observations as dating from the 1920s and cites M. Ezekiel’s Methods of Correlation Analysis (1930). The documentation available with the dataset does not specify each car, road surface, measurement uncertainty or exactly how the start of a stop was identified. Those omissions will matter when we interpret the result.

The local CSV preserves all 50 speed–distance pairs in their source order and adds a row number. The metadata records the source, units, conversion and reuse terms. We will use SI values calculated from

v=0.44704s,d=0.3048D,

where s is the numerical speed in mph, D the numerical distance in feet, v the speed in m/s and d the distance in metres. These are unit conversions, not fitted parameters.

Model A assumes immediate braking with constant acceleration −b, where b>0. Measured from the beginning of braking, position is x(t)=vt−12bt2, giving velocity v−bt. The stop occurs at ts=v/b. Substitution gives

dA=v22b.

This result also follows from Unit 6’s work–energy account: a constant opposing resultant of magnitude mb does work −mbd, removing 12mv2. The mass cancels. The two derivations connect the local description of motion with an endpoint energy balance.

Model B adds a period τ at the initial speed before the same braking phase. The first phase covers distance vτ, so

dB=vτ+v22b.

For the main comparison use b=3.0m/s2 and τ=0.50s. These are author-selected illustrative assumptions, chosen after inspecting the dataset’s scale; they were not measured for these observations or obtained by a formal fit. Both models must be evaluated at every recorded speed, with the same unit conversions. We are comparing these specified accounts, not estimating a historical driver’s reaction time.

Example 7.11 · A hand benchmark before the data

At an initial speed of 6.0 m/s, Model A stops after 6/3=2.0s and covers 62/6=6.0m. Model B adds (6)(0.50)=3.0m, giving 9.0 m in a total 2.5 s. Differentiate the braking position to check the stopping time; use work and energy to check its distance.

For any mass m, the braking work is −3m(6)=−18m joules and the initial kinetic energy is 12m(62)=18m joules. The two cancel. The additional 3 m in Model B belongs to the preceding constant-speed phase.

Activity 7.14 · Predict the difference (20 minutes)

At initial speeds 4 and 8 m/s, calculate both distances. How does doubling speed affect Model A’s distance and the extra term in Model B? Explain why a single global constant-acceleration equation cannot describe both phases of Model B. Identify one missing piece of historical metadata that would affect whether the delay phase belongs in a comparison.

Discussion of Activity 7.14

Model A gives 16/6≈2.67 m and 64/6≈10.67 m. Model B gives about 4.67 m and 14.67 m. The braking distance quadruples while the delay distance doubles. During the delay acceleration is zero; during braking it is −3 m/s². A documented starting event—such as a signal or the onset of braking—would help determine which distance the observations include.

7.15 Read the residuals

As in Unit 3, define a residual as the observed value minus the model’s prediction. Here

ri=di−dmodel(vi).

A positive residual means the recorded stop is longer than predicted; a negative one means shorter. Plot residuals against the recorded initial speed. The horizontal coordinate is speed, not elapsed time: different rows are separate observations, not successive points along one journey.

For example, rows 39–43 all list 20 mph, corresponding to 8.9408 m/s, but their stopping distances are 32, 48, 52, 56 and 64 feet. Thus the observed distances range from 9.7536 m to 19.5072 m at the same listed speed. A model that uses only speed must assign one prediction to all five. The variation calls for information beyond that single recorded input.

Example 7.12 · Same prediction, different residuals

At 20 mph, Model A predicts approximately 13.323 m and Model B approximately 17.793 m. For row 39, with recorded distance 9.7536 m, the residuals are about −3.570 m and −8.040 m. For row 43, with 19.5072 m, they are about +6.184 m and +1.714 m.

Model B is closer on the second row and farther away on the first. Selecting just one of those rows would tell a misleading story about the complete record.

A compact descriptive summary is the mean absolute residual: add the magnitudes of all residuals and divide by their number,

MAR=1n∑i=1n|ri|.

It remains in metres. Absolute values prevent positive and negative discrepancies from cancelling, but the average still hides where the discrepancies occur. Use it alongside the plot and the individual rows. It is a descriptive comparison, not a probability or a formal model-selection test.

Activity 7.15 · What survives averaging? (20 minutes)

Two toy residual lists are (−2,+2,−2,+2) m and (−1,−1,+1,+1) m. Calculate each signed mean and mean absolute residual. Explain why a zero signed mean is insufficient. Then use the five 20 mph observations above to explain why matching their average would still leave important variation unexplained.

Discussion of Activity 7.15

Both signed means are zero, while the mean absolute residuals are 2 m and 1 m. Cancellation hides the discrepancy magnitude. The five observed distances span 9.7536 m; a single average prediction cannot explain their spread. We need the individual points and additional information about conditions, not just one summary number.

7.16 Locate the source of disagreement

Before changing a physical model, check the calculation. A speed in mph treated as m/s changes both distance terms, and the squared term is especially sensitive. An incorrect subtraction order reverses every residual sign. These are implementation errors: hand benchmarks, units and a printed row can expose them.

Next consider what the observations represent. Integer-valued entries do not establish measurement accuracy. The original readings may have been rounded, but the source gives no uncertainty bounds. Different road conditions, different cars or different stopping procedures could contribute to the spread. They are possibilities to investigate, not causes demonstrated by this two-column record.

Finally examine the model assumptions. Constant braking acceleration, a common delay and identical parameters for every observation are strong simplifications. A systematic pattern in residuals can suggest where the description is inadequate, but several physical or measurement effects may produce similar patterns. Improving a description requires an additional observation that distinguishes those possibilities.

Activity 7.16 · Choose a diagnostic check (25 minutes)

Classify each proposed explanation and give a distinguishing check: (a) the code used feet as metres; (b) all recorded speeds share a calibration offset; (c) braking acceleration varies with speed; (d) the dataset mixes different stopping procedures. Which can you check from the supplied file and lesson alone? For the others, specify what further evidence would be needed.

Discussion of Activity 7.16

(a) is a unit/implementation error: compare one converted row and the hand benchmark. (b) is a systematic measurement possibility needing calibration records or an independent speed measurement. (c) concerns the physical model and could be tested with a time-resolved velocity record during braking. (d) concerns provenance and needs experimental notes or a controlled comparison. Only (a) can be settled with the supplied material. A smooth residual pattern does not uniquely identify any of the others.

7.17 A reproducible comparison in Python

Keep the original data untouched. Convert units into new variables, calculate both predictions from the same row, and retain both residuals. This small loop is deliberately readable enough to check by hand. Save the CSV beside the notebook or script.

import csv
from pathlib import Path

with Path("p101-u07-stopping.csv").open(newline="") as file:
    records = list(csv.DictReader(file))

results = []
for row in records:
    speed = float(row["speed_mph"]) * 0.44704
    observed = float(row["distance_ft"]) * 0.3048
    model_a = speed**2 / (2 * 3.0)
    model_b = 0.50 * speed + model_a
    results.append((speed, observed,
                    observed-model_a, observed-model_b))

mar_a = sum(abs(row[2]) for row in results) / len(results)
mar_b = sum(abs(row[3]) for row in results) / len(results)
print("Rows:", len(results))
print("Mean absolute residuals (m):", mar_a, mar_b)

The output is 50 rows and mean absolute residuals approximately 4.61 m for A and 3.34 m for B. Python’s csv.DictReader associates each entry with its column heading. The retained original units in the CSV make the conversions visible. If the file is missing, check the working folder before changing the data path; the download package contains everything needed for this calculation.

For a reproducible plot, put speed on the horizontal axis and observed/predicted distance on the vertical axis. Distinguish observations with markers and the two models with labelled lines. Plot residuals separately with a zero line. Do not connect successive observations as if they formed a continuous trajectory.

Activity 7.17 · Run and audit the comparison (60 minutes, Python)

Download the complete comparison package with notebook, script, CSV, metadata and source licence. Run the notebook from a fresh kernel. Check the 50-row count, the first converted row and the two worked 20 mph rows. Create the two labelled plots. Then change the common braking magnitude to 2.5 m/s² while retaining the 0.50 s delay. Does the ranking by mean absolute residual remain the same? Restore the main parameters before your final run.

Discussion of Activity 7.17

The first row is 1.78816 m/s and 0.6096 m. The main summaries are about 4.6133 m and 3.3358 m. With braking magnitude 2.5 m/s², they become approximately 3.5981 m and 3.6432 m: the ordering reverses slightly. Thus the statement “B is better” needs the chosen parameters and comparison measure. This is a sensitivity check, not a search for best-fit parameters. Keep both versions and explain the change rather than silently choosing the favourable result.

7.18 Write a bounded conclusion

A useful report lets another person recover your question, your inputs and your reasoning. State the dataset and units, the two assumptions, the parameters, the checks and the observed comparison. Then explain what prevents a stronger conclusion. The numerical result is one part of that account.

Example 7.13 · From a result to a defensible claim

“For the 50 historical observations in R’s cars dataset, the specified delay-plus-braking model has a smaller mean absolute distance residual than the immediate-braking model: 3.34 m versus 4.61 m, using b=3.0m/s2 and τ=0.50s. Substantial spread remains at the same listed speed, and changing the assumed braking magnitude can reverse the ranking. The comparison therefore supports this particular numerical description under its chosen assumptions; the sparse metadata does not establish a common reaction time or braking law for the original experiments.”

This conclusion includes the result without turning an illustrative parameter choice into a measured property. A stronger physical test would record the stopping procedure, calibration and velocity through the braking phase, allowing the acceleration assumption to be examined directly.

Activity 7.18 · Prepare the handover (25 minutes)

Write a report of about 150–200 words with the two plots. Include one hand check, the main comparison, the parameter sensitivity and two concrete limitations. End by proposing one additional measurement and explaining which ambiguity it addresses. Keep the notebook runnable from the supplied CSV and record any assistance used.

Discussion and criteria for Activity 7.18

Several reports can be good. Check that yours identifies all 50 source rows, original and converted units, both equations and parameter values, residual sign, an independent arithmetic check and the two summary values. Interpret the same-speed spread and the sensitivity result. Do not invent measurement error bars or a unique cause. A time-resolved velocity record could test constant deceleration; a recorded start signal and brake-onset time could test whether a delay belongs in the stopping interval. The proposed observation should resolve a named ambiguity.

You now have the ingredients of a model comparison that another reader can inspect. The closing lesson brings this judgement together with force, momentum, mechanical energy and thermal reasoning across Block 2.
