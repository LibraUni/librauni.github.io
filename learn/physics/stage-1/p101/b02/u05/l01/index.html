P101 · Unit 5 · Lesson 1

Direction changes in circular motion

Imagine marking one point on a slowly rotating platform and photographing it at equal time intervals. The point can cover equal distances between photographs and still have a different velocity in every photograph. To describe that change, we need arrows as well as a stopwatch.

Allow three hours: 60 minutes for the unit introduction and this explanation, 90 minutes for the five activities, and 30 minutes for checking and correcting your reasoning. Draw the vector constructions by hand. No Python is needed in this lesson.

5.1 Tangent velocity and an inward direction

Choose an observation frame in which the centre of a circular path is fixed. We treat the moving object as a particle: a point whose position is enough for the question. A point on a rotating object may follow a circle even though the whole object is too large to treat as a particle. The radius r is the distance from the centre of the path to the particular point we are tracking, not automatically the size of the apparatus.

At any position, the radius points out from the centre. The tangent is the straight line touching the circle at that position and perpendicular to the radius. Over a sufficiently short interval, the displacement points almost along this tangent. As we shorten the interval, its direction approaches the tangent exactly. The instantaneous velocity points along the tangent in the sense of travel. The arrow does not point towards the position the object will reach a substantial fraction of a turn later.

The diagram uses a fixed pair of page directions, right and up. In counterclockwise motion, velocity points up at the rightmost point, left at the top, down at the leftmost point and right at the bottom. Those are four different vectors even if all four arrows have the same length. Uniform circular motion means motion on a fixed circle at constant speed. Here “uniform” describes the speed, not a constant velocity vector.

We will also use the radial direction, meaning along the line between object and centre. Specify inward or outward whenever the sign matters. Inward is left at the rightmost point and down at the top. Thus “inward” is a useful local direction, but it is not one fixed compass direction throughout the motion. This is why the constant-vector-acceleration equations of Unit 4 cannot describe a complete uniform circle using one unchanged acceleration.

Activity 5.1 · Four positions, one speed

Allow 15 minutes. Sketch a circle and mark its top, bottom, left and right positions. Draw equal-length velocity arrows for clockwise motion, then inward arrows in a different style. State which arrows change if the sense of travel reverses. A student says, “The speedometer reading is steady, so the motion is unaccelerated.” Explain the error without using a formula for circular acceleration.

Discussion of Activity 5.1

For clockwise motion the velocities are right at the top, down at the right, left at the bottom and up at the left. Reversing the travel reverses every velocity arrow; the inward directions stay the same. A speedometer gives a magnitude. Velocity also has direction, and that direction changes, so acceleration need not vanish. Do not yet infer a force from the arrows: the physical interactions will be examined in Lesson 2.

5.2 Timing a revolution

A full circuit is a useful interval because its path length is known. The period T is the time for one revolution. The frequency f is the number of revolutions per unit time. If each revolution takes the same time, counting n complete revolutions during time t gives T=t/n and f=n/t. Hence f=1/T. Frequency in inverse seconds is conventionally expressed in hertz, Hz. A revolution is a count; it does not introduce a new physical dimension.

The circumference is 2πr. For uniform circular motion, speed is distance travelled divided by elapsed time, giving

v=2πrT=2πrf.

The numerator is a path length, not the displacement after one circuit. That displacement is zero because the point returns to its starting position. Average velocity over a circuit is therefore zero; average speed is not. When speed varies around a circle, circumference divided by period still gives average speed, but no longer supplies the speed at every instant.

Notation matters.

We use capital T for a period in this lesson and later use S for rope tension, so that a timing quantity cannot be confused with a force. The frequency f here is not a friction force; friction will be written Ffr.

Example 5.1 · A timing record

A marker 0.30 m from the centre completes 12 revolutions in 24 s at a steady rate. Find its period, frequency and speed.

Divide the whole timing interval by the number of circuits before inserting a time into the speed formula:

T=2412s=2.0s,f=1224s−1=0.50Hz.
v=2π(0.30m)2.0s=0.30πms−1≈0.94ms−1.

As an independent bookkeeping check, the total distance is 12(2π×0.30) m; dividing by 24 s gives the same speed. Using 24 s as the period would incorrectly assign the time for twelve circuits to one circuit.

Sometimes a rate is given in revolutions per minute. Convert the count rate before using seconds elsewhere: 90 rev/min is 90/60=1.5 revolutions per second, not 90 Hz. For describing partial turns, radians are useful. An angle of one radian spans an arc whose length equals the radius; in general, arc length s=rθ when θ is in radians. A full turn is 2π radians. The angular speed ω=2π/T consequently gives v=rω. We will mainly use T or v, but this notation helps you read specifications that use rad/s.

Activity 5.2 · Two marks on one platform

Allow 15 minutes. A platform turns at 30 rev/min. Mark A is at radius 0.20 m and mark B at radius 0.50 m. Calculate their periods and speeds. Explain why they complete a circuit together but have different speeds. Find each mark's displacement and distance travelled after one complete circuit.

Discussion of Activity 5.2

The common frequency is 30/60=0.50 Hz, so both periods are 2.0 s. Speeds are 2π(0.20)/2=0.20π≈0.628 m/s and 2π(0.50)/2=0.50π≈1.571 m/s. B travels 2.5 times as far in the same time. Each displacement is the zero vector. Distances are 0.40π≈1.257 m and π≈3.142 m. Sharing an angular rate does not mean sharing a linear speed.

5.3 Subtracting velocity vectors

From Unit 4, average acceleration over an interval Δt is Δv/Δt, where Δv=v2−v1. Subtract the vectors, not their magnitudes. With their tails drawn at a common origin, the arrow from the tip of the first velocity to the tip of the second is the change. You can verify this construction by adding: v1+Δv=v2.

Example 5.2 · A quarter-turn is a finite interval

A particle moves counterclockwise at 2.0 m/s on a circle of radius 1.0 m, from the rightmost point to the top. Use right-positive x and up-positive y. Find its average acceleration over this quarter-turn.

Initially v1=(0,2) m/s; finally v2=(−2,0) m/s. Therefore Δv=(−2,−2) m/s. The distance travelled is a quarter of 2πr, so Δt=(π/2)/2=π/4 s. Thus

aav=(−8π,−8π)ms−2,|aav|=82πms−2≈3.60ms−2.

The direction is down-left, towards the centre from the arc's midpoint. It is not the inward direction at either endpoint. The value describes a whole interval. We have not yet calculated the instantaneous acceleration at a selected point.

An especially revealing interval is a full revolution. Initial and final velocities then agree, so average acceleration over the whole turn is zero. Yet the velocity has changed throughout the intervening motion. A zero average vector does not tell us that every instantaneous vector was zero. Oppositely directed changes can cancel in the net result.

Activity 5.3 · A half-turn audit

Allow 20 minutes. For the particle in Example 5.2, take the interval from the rightmost point to the leftmost point. Calculate the velocity change, elapsed time and average acceleration. Sketch its direction. Explain why neither subtracting the two speeds nor dividing the circumference by this interval answers the acceleration question.

Hint for Activity 5.3

The final velocity points down. The distance travelled is half a circumference, and the velocity change is final minus initial.

Discussion of Activity 5.3

The velocity changes from (0,2) to (0,−2) m/s, giving (0,−4) m/s. The elapsed time is π/2 s, so average acceleration is (0,−8/π) m/s², about 2.55 m/s² down. This is inward from the top, the midpoint of the travelled semicircle. Subtracting speeds gives zero and discards direction. Dividing a length by a time gives a speed, with units m/s rather than m/s²; using the full circumference also assigns the wrong path length to this half-turn.

5.4 From a short chord to radial acceleration

To find the acceleration at an instant, compare velocities at increasingly close times. There is a helpful geometric relation before we make any short-interval approximation. Join the two positions to the centre. This makes an isosceles triangle with two sides r and a third side equal to the chord length c, the straight-line distance between the positions. Draw the two velocity vectors tail to tail. Their lengths are both v, and the angle between them equals the angle between the radii: rotating both radii through the same right angle preserves that angle.

The two isosceles triangles are therefore similar. Their corresponding side ratios give |Δv|/v=c/r exactly. Divide by the elapsed time:

|Δv|Δt=vrcΔt.

The chord is shorter than the travelled arc for a finite turn. Replacing c/Δt by v is consequently not exact for a quarter-turn or a half-turn. However, as the interval shrinks, the chord-to-arc ratio approaches one. The arc length is vΔt, so c/Δt approaches v. The magnitude of instantaneous acceleration is therefore v2/r. The change vector points inward at the midpoint of the short arc; as the interval shrinks around the chosen instant, this becomes the inward direction at that point. This argument uses a limiting geometric idea, without requiring differentiation.

Key idea 5.1 · Acceleration in uniform circular motion

For a fixed circle of radius r>0 and constant speed v, the instantaneous acceleration is directed towards the centre and has magnitude

ar=v2r=4π2rT2=rω2.

These are equivalent descriptions of one motion. The acceleration vector changes direction around the circle. If speed varies, v2/r remains the inward component for a circular path, but there is also a tangential component associated with the speed change.

Why does inward acceleration not make the radius shrink? Acceleration tells us how velocity changes, not where the object is heading. The current velocity is tangent. Over the next short interval the inward change turns that velocity. Treating the inward acceleration as a velocity would discard the existing tangential motion. The circular path is the combined result of that motion and its continuous change in direction.

Example 5.3 · Check the local value

For the same r=1.0 m, v=2.0 m/s motion, ar=22/1=4.0 m/s². This exceeds the 3.60 m/s² quarter-turn average from Example 5.2. There is no contradiction: the quarter-turn average combines acceleration vectors whose directions change during the interval. Shortening the interval makes their directional cancellation smaller. The units also check: (m/s)2/m=m/s2.

Activity 5.4 · Test the chord approximation

Allow 20 minutes. For the motion above, a total turn angle α in radians takes Δt=rα/v. Bisecting the position triangle gives c=2rsin(α/2). Use these two relations to calculate the average acceleration magnitude for total angles 60°, 20° and 5°. Convert the angle to radians in the timing formula and use a consistent calculator mode for the sine. Compare with 4.0 m/s² and explain the trend.

Discussion of Activity 5.4

The ratio to the instantaneous magnitude is 2sin(α/2)/α. The three results are approximately 3.820, 3.980 and 3.999 m/s². Smaller arcs have chords closer to their arc lengths. The calculation approaches 4.0 from below; it does not prove that chord and arc are equal for a finite interval. Drawing the two close velocity arrows remains useful even when their difference is too small to estimate accurately by eye.

5.5 What is held fixed?

The same formula can produce apparently opposite predictions when different quantities are held fixed. At fixed speed, ar=v2/r says that doubling the radius halves the acceleration. A gentler turn needs less rapid change of direction for the same distance covered each second. At fixed period, ar=4π2r/T2 says that doubling the radius doubles the acceleration. Now the outer point must travel twice as fast to complete its longer circuit in the same time.

The centrifuge photograph provides a useful setting for this distinction. Points held at different radii on one rigid rotating assembly share a period. They do not share a speed. Comparing the acceleration at two radial positions therefore calls for the fixed-period comparison. Comparing the same specimen position at two rotation rates calls for a different comparison. A statement such as “larger circles need less acceleration” is incomplete until we say what else is unchanged.

Example 5.4 · Two ways to double a radius

Begin with radius 2.0 m and speed 4.0 m/s, giving ar=8.0 m/s² and period T=π s. At radius 4.0 m with the same speed, acceleration is 16/4=4.0 m/s² and the period doubles. At radius 4.0 m with the same period, speed is 2π(4)/π=8.0 m/s and acceleration is 64/4=16 m/s². Both answers are correct for their stated controls.

Activity 5.5 · Design a comparison

Allow 20 minutes. Starting from Example 5.4's initial motion, calculate the effect of (a) doubling speed at fixed radius, (b) doubling period at fixed radius, and (c) doubling both radius and period. State a radius, speed, period and acceleration for each case, then write a sentence identifying the controlled quantities. Finally explain why the formula cannot be used at r=0 with a nonzero tangential speed.

Discussion of Activity 5.5

(a) r=2 m, v=8 m/s, T=π/2 s and ar=32 m/s²: acceleration quadruples. (b) r=2 m, T=2π s, v=2 m/s and ar=2 m/s²: acceleration becomes one-quarter. (c) r=4 m, T=2π s, v=4 m/s and ar=4 m/s²: speed stays fixed and acceleration halves. A zero-radius “circle” has no nonzero circumference or defined tangent path for the assumed motion. Division by zero signals that this case lies outside the model. On a rigid platform with finite period, the central point has zero speed and is stationary; it should be described directly, not by substituting 0/0.

We have described what acceleration a circular motion requires. We have not established that a particular object can actually follow it. The next lesson returns to the force inventory: which body pushes or pulls, in which direction, and can that interaction supply the required inward resultant?
