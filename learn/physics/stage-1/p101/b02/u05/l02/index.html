P101 · Unit 5 · Lesson 2

Forces that sustain a circle

Drawing a circle specifies a possible path. It does not explain how the object stays on it. We now ask which interactions can supply the changing velocity established in Lesson 1, and what happens when the required force exceeds what those interactions can provide.

Allow four hours: 75 minutes for explanation and worked examples, 135 minutes for the five activities and 30 minutes for feedback and correction. Throughout the near-surface examples use g=9.8ms−2. The numerical situations are idealised models, not operating instructions for real equipment.

5.6 A radial equation is a force inventory

Begin as in Unit 4: choose the object, identify its interactions and draw the forces on that object. For a constant-mass particle, Newton's second law is ∑F=ma in an inertial frame. We will observe from a frame treated as fixed to the laboratory for these examples. We do not attach the observation axes to a rotating rider and invent extra forces to account for that frame's acceleration.

At the instant under consideration, choose a radial axis positive towards the centre of the proposed circular path. Project each actual force onto that axis. The radial component of Newton's law then reads

∑Fin=mv2r.

The left side is a signed sum: forces pointing inward contribute positively, forces pointing outward negatively, and perpendicular forces contribute zero. The right side follows from the geometry of the motion. The term centripetal force names the required inward resultant, or radial resultant component; it does not name an additional interaction to put beside tension, friction and gravity. If tension alone supplies the inward component, writing “tension plus centripetal force” counts the same contribution twice.

Example 5.5 · A horizontal cord

A 0.40 kg puck moves on a smooth horizontal table, attached to a taut horizontal cord anchored at the centre of its 0.80 m circular path. Its speed is 2.0 m/s. Neglect drag. Find the tension.

The forces are table normal N upward, weight mg downward and cord tension S horizontally inward. No vertical acceleration gives N−mg=0. Only the cord has a radial component, so

S=mv2r=0.402.020.80N=2.0N.

The vertical forces balance while the horizontal resultant is nonzero. Equilibrium in one component is not equilibrium of the whole vector. The force exerted by the puck on the cord is the opposite member of the cord–puck interaction pair and belongs on a different free-body diagram.

Suppose the cord breaks. The inward cord force disappears. At the instant of breaking, the velocity is still tangent: removing a force does not instantaneously replace the velocity with an outward one. If the remaining vertical forces balance and horizontal drag is negligible, the puck subsequently moves on a straight tangent line. Its distance from the old centre increases along that line, but there is no newly created outward push in the laboratory description.

Activity 5.6 · Repair the inventory

Allow 20 minutes. A student's diagram for Example 5.5 contains inward tension, inward “centripetal force”, outward “reaction to tension”, upward normal and downward weight. Redraw it correctly and calculate the acceleration. If speed doubles while radius is maintained, find the new tension. Sketch the path just after a break at the top of a counterclockwise circle, assuming the same ideal table model.

Discussion of Activity 5.6

Keep only tension, normal and weight. The required inward acceleration is v2/r=5.0 m/s². The reaction is a force on the cord, not the puck. Doubling speed quadruples acceleration to 20 m/s² and tension to 8.0 N. At the top, counterclockwise velocity points left; after the break the path is a straight leftward tangent. A radial outward arrow would incorrectly change the initial velocity.

5.7 A horizontal turn and friction capacity

Now remove the cord and place a small block on a rotating horizontal platform. Suppose it stays at a fixed distance from the axis without slipping. The platform's normal force is vertical, so it cannot itself provide the horizontal acceleration. Static friction is the interaction that can turn the block. “Static” means no relative sliding at the contact; the block is certainly moving in the laboratory frame.

For the block, vertical balance still gives N=mg. The horizontal equation demands Ffr=mv2/r. But static friction is responsive only up to a limit: Ffr≤μsN. Combining demand and capacity gives

mv2r≤μsmg,v≤μsgr.

The positive square root gives a speed limit within this contact model. Equality describes impending slip. Below that limit, friction is smaller than its maximum; setting Ffr=μsN at every speed would generally predict the wrong acceleration. The cancellation of m follows from the assumed proportional friction law. It does not say that every real contact behaves independently of load, surface condition or shape.

Example 5.6 · A demand below capacity

A 0.50 kg block follows a circle of radius 0.80 m at 1.2 m/s on the platform. Take μs=0.25. Can static friction maintain this motion?

The demand is Ffr=0.50(1.2)2/0.80=0.90 N. The normal is 0.50(9.8)=4.9 N, so the capacity is 0.25(4.9)=1.225 N. Since 0.90<1.225, the proposed motion is consistent with the model. Actual friction is 0.90 N, not 1.225 N. The maximum speed at this radius is 0.25(9.8)(0.80)=1.40 m/s.

If the platform demands 1.6 m/s, the needed force is 1.60 N, exceeding capacity. The assumption of no slip then fails. We cannot keep imposing the old radius and angular rate on the block; a new sliding-motion calculation would be required.

A particle model of a vehicle turning on a level road has similar horizontal bookkeeping when tyre contact does not slide. The real vehicle introduces further issues, such as load distribution and overturning, which this model omits. The useful general method is to compare an explicitly calculated demand with a justified interaction limit, rather than treating the square-root expression as a universal guarantee.

Activity 5.7 · Choose the right comparison

Allow 25 minutes. Use the block and friction coefficient in Example 5.6. At radius 0.40 m, test motion (a) at the same 1.2 m/s speed and (b) at the same period as the original 0.80 m, 1.2 m/s motion. Calculate the friction demand in both cases and decide whether each is possible. Explain the different conclusions.

Hint for Activity 5.7

At fixed period, halving radius halves speed. Normal force and the assumed friction capacity are unchanged.

Discussion of Activity 5.7

(a) Demand is 0.50(1.2)2/0.40=1.80 N, greater than 1.225 N, so no slip is impossible. (b) Speed becomes 0.60 m/s and demand is 0.50(0.60)2/0.40=0.45 N, below capacity. In the first comparison radius falls at fixed speed; in the second the reduced speed more than offsets the smaller radius. State the control condition before comparing formulas.

5.8 Tension with two components

Not every interaction points exactly inward or exactly vertically. Consider a small bob on a light taut string, moving steadily in a horizontal circle below a fixed pivot. The string makes angle θ with the vertical and sweeps out a cone, so this arrangement is often called a conical pendulum. The bob's height stays constant. The path radius is the horizontal distance to the vertical line through the pivot, not the string length L; geometry gives r=Lsinθ.

Only two forces act in the ideal model: gravity downward and tension S along the string towards the pivot. Resolve tension rather than adding its components as extra forces. Its upward component is Scosθ and its inward component is Ssinθ. Consequently,

Scosθ=mg,Ssinθ=mv2r.

For 0<θ<90∘, the vertical equation gives S=mg/cosθ. Since the cosine is less than one, tension exceeds weight. This is not because the bob accelerates upward: the vertical component balances weight while another component supplies radial acceleration. Dividing the inward equation by the vertical one eliminates the common S and m:

tanθ=v2rg,v=rgtanθ.

Example 5.7 · Geometry selects the speed

A 0.20 kg bob on a 1.0 m string makes a steady horizontal circle with the string at 30° to the vertical. Find its radius, tension and speed.

First find the path radius: r=1.0sin30∘=0.50 m. The vertical condition gives S=1.96/cos30∘≈2.26 N. Then

v=(0.50)(9.8)tan30∘ms−1≈1.68ms−1.

As a check, Ssin30∘≈1.13 N and mv2/r≈1.13 N. Inserting the full 1.0 m string length as the circle radius would spoil this agreement. The geometry and speed are constrained together; they cannot all be assigned independently while retaining the same ideal steady motion.

The photograph invites a force question that the path alone could not answer: how is the specimen supported as it turns? A real mounting may transmit several forces and moments through an extended structure. Our particle models deliberately suppress those details. The conical bob shows why even a simpler support force can exceed weight without producing upward motion: its direction matters. Knowing a magnitude alone is insufficient to decide what acceleration it can supply.

Activity 5.8 · Which radius?

Allow 30 minutes. Keep the 1.0 m string and 0.20 kg mass, but choose a steady angle of 60° to the vertical. Calculate r, S and v. Compare the tension with the 30° case. A student claims that doubling the angle doubles the radius and speed. Use both the numbers and the component equations to assess that claim.

Discussion of Activity 5.8

Now r=sin60∘≈0.866 m, S=1.96/0.50=3.92 N and v=0.866…(9.8)tan60∘=14.7≈3.83 m/s. Neither radius nor speed doubles. Sine, cosine and tangent do not scale linearly with angle. Tension rises because a smaller fraction of it points upward; the vertical component must still equal 1.96 N. At exactly 90° a horizontal string would provide no upward component, so it cannot support this steady horizontal motion under gravity alone.

5.9 Top and bottom of a vertical circle

In a vertical circle the inward direction changes relative to gravity. This makes signs especially important. We will calculate at specified instants using supplied speeds. A freely moving bob generally changes speed between top and bottom; nothing in a radial equation alone determines that whole journey. Do not assume that the two supplied speeds must be equal, or that a feasible force at one point proves completion of the loop.

For a bob on a taut string of length r, tension always points towards the pivot. At the bottom, inward is upward, so tension contributes positively and weight negatively: S−mg=mvb2/r. At the top, inward is downward, so both contribute positively: S+mg=mvt2/r. The two tensions are therefore

Sb=mvb2r+mg,St=mvt2r−mg.

These equations use the same inward-positive convention at both positions. It is the direction of the axis on the page that changes. At the top, gravity helps provide inward acceleration; at the bottom, tension must overcome the outward radial component of gravity as well as provide the required resultant. Gravity has zero tangential component at these two precise positions, although it generally has a tangential component elsewhere on the circle.

Example 5.8 · A tension that cannot exist

A 0.30 kg bob has radius 1.5 m. Calculate the tension at a bottom speed of 6.0 m/s and a top speed of 4.0 m/s. Then test a proposed top speed of 3.0 m/s. Treat these as separate instantaneous tests, not as speeds on one complete trajectory.

At the bottom, Sb=0.30(36)/1.5+2.94=10.14 N. At the top with 4.0 m/s, St=0.30(16)/1.5−2.94=0.26 N. Both demands are nonnegative. At 3.0 m/s, however, St=0.30(9)/1.5−2.94=−1.14 N.

A negative answer is not a compressive tension. The string would have to push outward to reduce the inward resultant to the proposed circular value. A string cannot do that. The taut-circle assumption fails; the bob's subsequent motion must be described with a different force inventory and path. Replacing −1.14 N by its magnitude would conceal the physical inconsistency.

The top condition St≥0 requires vt≥gr. Equality has zero tension: it is the ideal limiting case at that point. A speed satisfying this inequality is a necessary local condition for a taut-string top passage, not a proof that the motion can be reached from any chosen starting state. In the same way, a real string's maximum tension would impose an additional upper demand limit that our ideal string model has not specified.

Activity 5.9 · Diagnose the impossible case

Allow 30 minutes. For Example 5.8, find the limiting top speed and explain why mass cancels. Evaluate the top tension at 5.0 m/s. Then examine a student's equation S−mg=mvt2/r at the top: identify the incompatible direction choices and write a correct outward-positive version. Does reversing the direction of travel reverse the top tension?

Discussion of Activity 5.9

The limiting speed is 9.8(1.5)=14.7≈3.83 m/s. Both gravitational force and required radial resultant are proportional to mass. At 5.0 m/s, S=0.30(25)/1.5−2.94=2.06 N. At the top tension and weight point in the same direction, so their signs must agree in any one radial convention. With outward positive, −S−mg=−mvt2/r. Reversing the tangential velocity leaves vt2 and the inward geometry unchanged, so it does not reverse tension.

5.10 When the prescribed circle fails

A surface, like a string, has a direction restriction. A block resting on the outside of a smooth convex circular hump receives an upward normal at the top, while gravity points downward towards the centre. Taking inward as positive gives mg−N=mv2/r, so N=m(g−v2/r). Remaining in contact requires N≥0. Here the resulting condition is an upper speed limit, v≤gr, whereas the taut-string top condition was a lower limit.

The difference is physical, not an algebraic trick. At the top of the hump gravity is the only inward force; the surface can only subtract from the inward resultant by pushing upward. If the desired circular acceleration exceeds g, the surface would have to pull downward. With no attachment, it cannot. At the top of a string circle, in contrast, tension can add to gravity's inward pull but cannot subtract from it.

Example 5.9 · A convex track

A 2.0 kg model cart passes the top of a smooth circular hump of radius 5.0 m at 6.0 m/s. Treat its size as negligible and calculate the normal force.

The radial demand is mv2/r=2(36)/5=14.4 N. Weight supplies 19.6 N inward, so the surface normal is N=19.6−14.4=5.2 N outward. The track pushes less strongly than when the cart is stationary. The contact threshold is 9.8(5.0)=7.0 m/s. At 8.0 m/s the circular calculation gives N=−6.0 N, which the unconnected surface cannot provide. The path then departs from the track; do not carry the circular acceleration into the detached phase.

Key idea 5.2 · Test a motion, then its conditions

Identify interactions first. Choose a radial sign and use ∑Fin=mv2/r. Check remaining components and the conditions of each interaction: tension S≥0, normal contact N≥0, static friction within its capacity. A failed condition rejects the assumed motion or contact state; it does not authorise changing a negative magnitude to a positive one.

Activity 5.10 · Similar algebra, different physics

Allow 30 minutes. Compare a 0.20 kg bob at the top of a taut-string circle with a 0.20 kg object on the outside top of a smooth circular hump. Both radii are 0.50 m. For each model draw the actual forces and test speeds 1.0 and 3.0 m/s. State which motion is locally consistent, and explain why the same threshold separates opposite allowed ranges.

Discussion of Activity 5.10

The threshold is 4.9≈2.21 m/s. For the string, S=0.20v2/0.50−1.96, giving −1.56 N at 1.0 m/s (impossible taut circle) and 1.64 N at 3.0 m/s (locally feasible). For the hump, N=1.96−0.20v2/0.50, giving 1.56 N at 1.0 m/s (feasible) and −1.64 N at 3.0 m/s (impossible contact). The string adds an inward pull; the surface adds an outward push. Neither answer alone establishes a complete trajectory before or after the top.

We can now ask a different question. If gravity alone supplies the inward resultant, with no cord and no contact, what circular speed does it permit? Answering requires a gravitational force law that remains useful far from the surface, which is the subject of Lesson 3.
