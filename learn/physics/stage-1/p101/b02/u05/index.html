P101 · Block 2 · Unit 5

Circular motion and gravitation

A spacecraft can keep falling without reaching the ground. A sample in a centrifuge can keep accelerating without gaining speed. Both statements become understandable once we take the direction of velocity as seriously as its magnitude.

In Unit 4, a force inventory led to an acceleration, and an acceleration led to a prediction of motion. For a projectile near Earth's surface, the simplest model used one fixed downward direction. We now consider paths that turn far enough for that approximation to become inappropriate. Along a circle, the direction towards the centre changes as the object moves. A constant speed therefore does not make the acceleration vector constant, or zero.

There are two questions to keep separate. First, what acceleration must a chosen motion have? That is a question about geometry and timing. Second, which interactions could supply the required resultant force? A cord, a surface and gravity can all turn a path, but each has different conditions. A cord cannot push when our equation asks for a negative tension. A surface cannot pull an unattached object towards it. A circular path is a possibility to test against the force model, not an instruction that nature has to obey.

Gravitation gives this reasoning a wider reach. We will connect the familiar near-surface weight to an attraction that depends on centre distance. For a small satellite moving around a much more massive, approximately spherical body, the circular model selects a particular speed at each radius. That selection lets us compare hypothetical orbits by hand and in Python. It does not predict every possible orbit, nor does a working program establish that a real satellite follows the ideal model.

Your route through the unit

Lesson 1 develops the changing velocity of a point moving round a circle. Lesson 2 asks what forces can sustain that motion and tests contact limits. Lesson 3 combines gravity with the circular condition. Lesson 4 turns those equations into transparent, checked comparisons. Lesson 5 draws together the reasoning through mixed exercises and a concise reference. This leads into Unit 6, where momentum and energy provide other ways of organising mechanical questions.

Bring the system boundaries, free-body diagrams and vector components from Unit 4, together with the algebra and right-triangle reasoning of M101 Units 1–2. The computing work uses the functions, arrays and labelled plots already introduced in P101; links beside the investigation provide a route back. No differentiation, orbit solver or prior astronomy course is required.

Allow 18 hours, including 3 hours of Python: 3, 4, 4, 4 and 3 hours for Lessons 1–5 respectively. The introduction belongs within Lesson 1's reading time. The final lesson includes the cumulative exercises, their solutions and correction, the reference sheet and the conclusion. Work in shorter sessions when useful; attempt the numbered activities before opening their discussions. There is no extra assessed submission in this unit.

What you should be able to explain

By the end, you should be able to distinguish speed from velocity in a turning path; connect radius, period and inward acceleration; identify the interactions behind a radial force equation; reject a motion that violates a contact condition; calculate and compare ideal circular orbits with stated assumptions; and explain what hand checks, code checks and observations each contribute to a scientific conclusion.
