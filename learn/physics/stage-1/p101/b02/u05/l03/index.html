P101 · Unit 5 · Lesson 3

Gravitation and a circular orbit

A circular orbit has no string holding the satellite in place. Gravity supplies the inward acceleration. To calculate it, we must replace the approximately constant near-surface value of gravity with a model that accounts for distance from the attracting body.

Allow four hours: 60 minutes for explanation, 90 minutes for written activities, 60 minutes for the computational scaling investigation and 30 minutes for feedback and correction. Keep the physical assumptions beside the formulas; they decide where a result can be used.

5.11 One attraction, two interacting bodies

Newtonian gravitation models an attractive interaction between masses. For two point masses M and m separated by distance r, the force magnitude on either is

F=GMmr2.

The force on each points towards the other. Here G is the gravitational constant; we use G=6.67430×10−11m3kg−1s−2, a value tabulated by NASA/JPL. Capital G is not the local gravitational acceleration g. Multiplying these units by two masses and dividing by a squared distance gives kgms−2, or newtons, as a force requires.

The expression applies exactly in Newtonian theory to non-overlapping, spherically symmetric bodies using their centre-to-centre distance. Spherical symmetry means the mass distribution looks the same in every direction from its centre; density may vary with radius. For an irregular body whose size is small compared with the separation, concentrating its mass at one point can be a useful approximation. Neither statement permits putting an arbitrary extended object's surface separation into the denominator.

The equal force magnitudes do not imply equal accelerations. Dividing by the recipient's mass gives am=GM/r2 for the body of mass m and aM=Gm/r2 for the body of mass M. If one body has a much larger mass, the same force changes its velocity much more slowly. The forces do not cancel on either body because they have different recipients. They cancel only as internal forces when we choose the combined system for a suitable total-force calculation.

Example 5.10 · A weak mutual attraction

Two small spherical bodies have masses 12 kg and 18 kg and centre separation 0.60 m. Calculate their mutual force and the acceleration contribution it gives each body. Assume they do not overlap.

F=(6.67430×10−11)(12)(18)0.602N≈4.00×10−8N.

The 12 kg body's acceleration contribution is F/12≈3.34×10−9 m/s² towards the 18 kg body. The 18 kg body's is F/18≈2.22×10−9 m/s² towards the 12 kg body. The lighter body has the larger acceleration. These are contributions from their mutual attraction; in a laboratory, supports and Earth's gravity also belong in the complete inventory. We have not claimed that either body actually accelerates freely at these values.

Activity 5.11 · Change a source, change a recipient

Allow 20 minutes. In Example 5.10, first double only the 18 kg mass. Find the factors by which the force and each body's acceleration contribution change. Next restore the original masses and double their separation. Explain why a factor of two in distance has a different consequence from a factor of two in either mass.

Discussion of Activity 5.11

Doubling the source to 36 kg doubles the force. The 12 kg body's acceleration doubles, while the 36 kg body's acceleration due to the unchanged 12 kg source is unchanged: both its force and its own mass have doubled. Doubling separation divides the force and both accelerations by four, since the denominator contains r2. The law is proportional to each mass separately and inverse-square in separation.

5.12 Field strength and centre distance

For a small test body outside a spherical source of mass M, dividing gravitational force by test mass produces a property of the source and location:

g(r)=GMr2.

This is the magnitude of the gravitational field, directed towards the source. Its units may be written N/kg, force per unit mass, or equivalently m/s², the acceleration of a freely falling test body. An object held at rest at that location can have nonzero gravitational field and zero total acceleration because a support supplies another force. Keep the field separate from the resultant acceleration of a constrained object.

If the body's spherical radius is R and the altitude above its surface is h, then r=R+h. The gravitational force has no singularity at the surface h=0: the centre distance is then R, not zero. For the same source, a useful ratio avoids repeated constants:

g(R+h)g(R)=(RR+h)2.

When h is small compared with R, this ratio is close to one, which explains the usefulness of uniform gravity for short near-surface flights. “Small” depends on the precision needed. At h=0.01R, the ratio is 1/1.012≈0.9803, already about 2% below the surface value. At h=R, the ratio is one-quarter. Doubling altitude from one small height to another does not generally double centre distance or quarter gravity.

Example 5.11 · Mass, weight and a scale reading

Take an ideal planet with surface field 10 m/s². What is the gravitational force on a 60 kg person at altitude equal to the planet's radius? What would an ideal spring scale read if person and scale were in free fall together, with negligible relative motion?

The centre distance doubles, so field becomes 2.5 m/s² and gravitational force is 60(2.5)=150 N. Mass remains 60 kg. In ideal shared free fall no support force is needed between person and scale, so the scale reads zero. That is zero apparent weight, not zero gravitational force. Here we use “weight” for gravitational force; always specify when a measurement instead reports support force.

Activity 5.12 · Altitude is not radius

Allow 20 minutes. For an ideal spherical planet of radius 6000 km, compare the fields at altitudes 0, 600 km and 6000 km as fractions of the surface field. A student predicts a fourfold reduction between altitudes 300 km and 600 km. Calculate the actual ratio and identify the mistaken distance.

Discussion of Activity 5.12

The fractions are 1, (6000/6600)2=100/121≈0.8264, and (6000/12000)2=0.25. Between 300 and 600 km the ratio is (6300/6600)2≈0.9112. The altitude doubles but the centre distance grows only from 6300 to 6600 km. Use matching distance units in a ratio; in a force calculation with SI G, convert kilometres to metres first.

5.13 Selecting a circular orbital speed

Consider a satellite whose mass is negligible compared with the attracting body. Approximate the source as spherical and fixed at the centre of an inertial frame; neglect the forces of other bodies, drag and thrust. Assume the satellite is outside the source and its path is circular. These assumptions describe a model we can solve, not a universal property of satellites.

Gravity is the only force on the satellite, and it points inward. For the proposed radius, the radial equation is

GMmr2=mv2r.

For m>0 and r>0, cancel m and multiply by r. This gives v2=GM/r, hence the nonnegative speed is v=GM/r. It is useful to write μ=GM, called the source's gravitational parameter. Then

v=μr,μ=GM.

Here μ has units m³/s². It is not the static friction coefficient μs from Lesson 2; the subscript and units distinguish them. The formula selects the speed consistent with a circular orbit of that radius. Initial velocity must also be tangent. Merely being at radius r, or having a speed with this magnitude while pointing towards the source, does not establish a circular orbit.

The satellite's mass has cancelled because a larger satellite experiences a proportionally larger gravitational force and has proportionally more inertia. Its gravitational force is not independent of its mass. The central body's mass remains because it controls the attraction. If the two masses are comparable, both move substantially around their common centre of mass; treating one centre as fixed fails. The equal-force law still holds, but the simple one-centre orbital construction needs revision beyond this unit.

An inward force does not need an outward “balancing force” to maintain an orbit. Balance would give zero resultant and straight-line constant velocity in this frame. The actual unbalanced gravity continually changes velocity's direction. Equally, switching gravity off would not leave the satellite tracing the old circle; it would initially continue along the tangent. These are the same distinctions that the cord example made on the table.

Activity 5.13 · Audit three orbit claims

Allow 20 minutes. Explain what is wrong or incomplete in each statement: (a) “Gravity and centripetal force cancel.” (b) “Doubling satellite mass halves orbital speed.” (c) “The right speed at a given radius is sufficient, whatever the direction of travel.” Then state a set of assumptions under which v=GM/r is justified.

Discussion of Activity 5.13

(a) Gravity supplies the inward resultant; centripetal force is not another interaction. (b) Satellite mass cancels from the acceleration equation in the negligible-satellite-mass model. (c) Velocity must be tangent for the proposed circle; an inward velocity already changes the centre distance. State a circular path outside a spherical source, a dominant effectively fixed central body, Newtonian gravity, and negligible drag, thrust and other attractions. Both the size and direction of initial velocity matter.

5.14 Period and a hand benchmark

We can find a period without a new force law. The satellite travels circumference 2πr at constant speed. Using the speed we have just derived,

T=2πrv=2πrrμ=2πr3μ.

The quantity inside the square root has units m³ divided by m³/s², which is s²; the final period has units seconds. This result concerns circular motion under the stated assumptions. It is not a derivation of a general law for elliptical orbits, and we will not use it as one.

Example 5.12 · A transparent numerical orbit

Use the rounded Earth gravitational parameter μ=3.986×1014 m³/s² and a circular radius r=7.000×106 m. Find speed, period and gravitational acceleration. The radius is supplied directly; this is an illustrative orbit, not a prediction of the ISS's current location.

v=3.986×10147.000×106ms−1≈7546ms−1.
T=2π(7.000×106)7546.049…s≈5829s≈97.14min.

Compute field independently from the inverse-square law: g=μ/r2≈8.1347 m/s². The radial acceleration v2/r gives the same value when unrounded speed is used. This check can catch a wrong power or unit conversion. It tests consistency between equations; it does not observe a satellite.

For a hand-friendly software benchmark, choose a hypothetical source with μ=8.0×1012 m³/s² and radius of orbit r=2.0×106 m, outside that source. Then μ/r=4.0×106 m²/s² and v=2000 m/s exactly for these stipulated numbers. The period is 2000π s and the field is 2.0 m/s². A benchmark selected for simple arithmetic is useful precisely because we can predict its result before running the code.

Key idea 5.3 · A circular gravity-only model

For a small satellite outside a dominant spherical source, neglecting other forces and taking the source centre as fixed:

g=μr2,v=μr,T=2πr3μ.

Distance r is measured from the source centre. Specifying a circular motion links its radius and speed; they are not independent controls under gravity alone.

Activity 5.14 · Predict before calculating

Allow 30 minutes. For the hand-friendly source, compare radii r, 4r and 9r with the same μ. Predict the factors for field, speed and period before evaluating them. Next keep radius fixed and multiply the source mass by four, keeping G unchanged. What happens to those three quantities? Explain why a longer orbital circumference does not by itself explain the full period change.

Discussion of Activity 5.14

For radius factors 1, 4 and 9, field factors are 1, 1/16 and 1/81; speed factors are 1, 1/2 and 1/3; period factors are 1, 8 and 27. Thus speeds are 2000, 1000 and approximately 666.7 m/s, and periods are 2000π, 16000π and 54000π s. At fixed radius with four times the source mass, field quadruples, speed doubles and period halves. Increasing orbital radius increases the circumference and reduces the selected speed, making the period grow faster than radius alone.

5.15 An hour of computational scaling

Retrieve the array operations from P101 Unit 1 Lesson 4 if needed. In NumPy, multiplying an array by a number applies the operation to each entry; exponentiation uses **. Square roots can be evaluated element by element with np.sqrt. The following calculation represents three separate circular scenarios. It does not advance a satellite through time.

import numpy as np

mu = 8.0e12                 # m^3/s^2: hypothetical central source
r0 = 2.0e6                  # m: centre distance, outside the source
radius_factor = np.array([1.0, 4.0, 9.0])
radius = r0 * radius_factor
speed = np.sqrt(mu / radius)
period = 2 * np.pi * radius / speed
field = mu / radius**2

print("speed / m/s:", speed)
print("period / s:", period)
print("field / m/s^2:", field)
print("period ratios:", period / period[0])

Each array has the same ordering: its first value belongs to r0, its second to 4r0 and its third to 9r0. The assignment to radius keeps the unit conversion in one visible place. The program stores no units automatically: comments record our convention, while our reasoning has to enforce it. In particular, entering 2000 because the radius is 2000 km would silently represent 2000 metres.

Activity 5.15 · Reproduce, perturb, diagnose

Allow 60 minutes for this Python investigation, including reading the code and checking outputs. Use the notebook or companion script; these are alternative forms of the same task.

  1. Write down Activity 5.14's predictions, then run the code. Explain every output's units and ordering.
  2. Replace the radius factors with 1, 2 and 4. Predict and check the middle value without rounding the first computed result.
  3. Restore 1, 4 and 9 and multiply mu by four. Record the change in each output.
  4. In a separate scratch cell, deliberately enter r0 = 2000.0. Explain the resulting speed and period factors using powers of 1000, then restore metres. Label this as a unit-error test, not a new physical scenario.
  5. Write three sentences distinguishing verification of the program from evidence for a real orbit.
Discussion of Activity 5.15

The original speed array is approximately [2000, 1000, 666.667] m/s and the period ratios are [1, 8, 27]. At factor 2, speed is 2000/2≈1414.21 m/s, period ratio is 22≈2.82843, and field is 0.50 m/s². Quadrupling μ doubles every speed, halves every period and quadruples every field. The metre/kilometre error reduces all radii by 1000: speeds increase by 1000≈31.62, periods decrease by 10001000≈31622.8, and fields increase by one million. These absurdly altered values should trigger an input audit. Matching predicted ratios checks algebra and implementation; real-orbit validation would require suitable observations and an assessment of the circular, central-body and neglected-force assumptions.
