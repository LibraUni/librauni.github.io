P101 · Block 2 · Unit 4 · Lesson 3

Weight, support,
tension and friction

A force diagram tells us which interactions belong in a calculation. Newton’s second law connects their sum to acceleration. Neither step, by itself, tells us how strongly a bench supports a tray or whether the tray will slip when we pull it. We now need models for the interactions themselves.

The models have different logical roles. Gravity supplies a force from mass and gravitational field. A support or taut cord supplies whatever force is required by the motion, within its physical constraints. Dry friction has a limiting rule when surfaces stick and a different approximate rule when they slide. Treating every force as a fixed formula would erase these distinctions.

Allow four hours: 85 minutes for explanation and examples, 125 minutes for the seven activities, and 30 minutes for feedback and correction. Take g=9.8ms−2 in numerical Earth-surface examples unless another value is supplied. Keep extra digits during working and round final calculated values sensibly. The masses, coefficients and force values in the problems are stipulated models, not measured properties of the photographed equipment.

4.13 Weight depends on mass and location

Lesson 1 identified weight as the gravitational force exerted on an object. Near Earth’s surface, over a region small enough that changes in gravity can be neglected, we represent it by

W=mg.

Here g is the local gravitational field expressed as force per unit mass; it points approximately vertically downwards. Its magnitude g can be expressed in newtons per kilogram. Since 1N=1kgms−2, these units are equivalent to metres per second squared. With upward y positive, Wy=−mg, while the weight’s nonnegative magnitude is W=mg. Do not put a minus sign into the magnitude g and then subtract it a second time.

Why can the same quantity describe a field and an acceleration? If gravity is the only significant force on the object, the second law gives ma=mg, hence a=g. We call this free fall. It includes upward motion after release as well as downward motion: gravity does not wait for the object to start descending. If a support or rope also acts, the acceleration need not be g even though the weight remains mg.

The mass is still measured in kilograms. Changing location can change weight without changing the object’s mass. A suitcase carried to a place with weaker gravity is easier to support, but it has not thereby become easier to accelerate horizontally under the same resultant force. Conversely, a force sensor beneath an object responds to contact force; converting that reading to kilograms assumes a gravitational field and a state of motion.

Example 4.12 · The same sample in two gravitational fields

Question. A 5.0 kg sample is taken from a location with g=9.8ms−2 to one with g=1.6ms−2. Find its weight at each location. Compare its acceleration if a horizontal resultant of 10 N is applied at either location.

Working. The gravitational force magnitudes are

W1=(5.0kg)(9.8ms−2)=49N,W2=(5.0kg)(1.6ms−2)=8.0N.

For the stipulated horizontal resultant, ax=10N/(5.0kg)=2.0ms−2 at either location.

Interpretation. Less support is needed in the weaker field, but the inertial mass is unchanged. This comparison holds the horizontal resultant fixed. If a real surface’s friction changes with the support force, an unchanged applied pull need not give the same resultant.

Activity 4.13 · Weight is not the resultant by default

Allow 15 minutes. A 0.40 kg sensor is (a) resting on a bench and (b) in free fall immediately after release. Find its weight in both situations and its acceleration in each idealised model. Neglect air effects. Explain why “the sensor has lost its weight when the support is removed” is incorrect, and state the third-law partner of its weight.

Hint for Activity 4.13

Removing a contact changes the support force, not Earth’s gravitational interaction.

Discussion of Activity 4.13

In both cases W=(0.40)(9.8)=3.92N, approximately 3.9 N down. At rest, the bench’s 3.92 N upward support balances weight and acceleration is zero. In free fall, support is absent, so ay=−9.8ms−2 with upward positive. The gravitational partner is the force of the sensor on Earth, not a force of the bench on the sensor. Weight and support must remain separate entries even when their magnitudes happen to agree.

4.14 Find the normal force from the motion

The normal force is the perpendicular component of a surface’s contact force. Its direction follows the surface, but its magnitude depends on the other forces and the perpendicular acceleration. The familiar equality N=mg is a result for a particular situation: a horizontal support, zero vertical acceleration, and no other vertical forces. Here italic N denotes a force magnitude; upright N is the newton unit.

Suppose a hand partly lifts a box that remains on a bench. The hand supplies some of the upward force needed to balance gravity, so the bench supplies less. If instead the hand presses down, the bench must support the combined downward forces. In either case, write the vertical equation rather than recalling a numerical rule.

Example 4.13 · A hand changes the support

Question. A 5.0 kg box rests on a horizontal bench. A hand pulls vertically upwards with 12 N. Find the normal force. What would it be for a 12 N downward push? Finally, test the assumption of maintained contact if the upward pull is increased to 60 N.

Working. The weight is 49 N. With upward positive and zero vertical acceleration, the upward-pull case gives

N+12N−49N=0,N=37N.

For the downward push, N−12N−49N=0, giving N=61N. If we keep assuming contact and zero vertical acceleration under the 60 N upward pull, the equation instead demands N=−11N.

Interpretation. The last result is not a negative magnitude to accept or replace by its absolute value. An ordinary nonadhesive bench can push outwards, not pull the box down. Contact is lost. Set N=0 and use the new free-body diagram: the resultant is 60−49=11N up, giving ay=2.2ms−2 while the pull continues and the box is free of the bench.

Key idea 4.4 · Contact is a condition to check

For an ordinary nonadhesive surface, the normal magnitude satisfies N≥0. Find it from the perpendicular equation of motion. If an assumed contact requires N<0, that assumption has failed; redraw the system after separation. An adhesive attachment would be a different interaction model.

A moving support makes the same reasoning useful. An object resting relative to the floor of a vertically accelerating lift shares the lift’s vertical acceleration. Described from the approximately inertial ground frame, its equation is N−mg=may. This gives N=m(g+ay). The acceleration’s sign matters; the direction of travel alone does not. A lift moving upwards while slowing has downward acceleration and can give a smaller support force than at rest.

Activity 4.14 · Read a support sensor in a lift

Allow 20 minutes. A 20 kg instrument remains on a horizontal lift floor. Find the normal force when the lift (a) accelerates upwards at 1.2 m/s² and (b) accelerates downwards at 2.0 m/s². In case (b), it is currently moving upwards. Explain why that direction of travel does not change your calculation. What normal force does the ideal equation predict if both floor and instrument enter free fall together?

Hint for Activity 4.14

Use upward positive in the ground frame, then substitute the signed acceleration in N−mg=may.

Discussion of Activity 4.14

The weight is 196 N. In (a), N=20(9.8+1.2)=220N. In (b), ay=−2.0ms−2, so N=20(9.8−2.0)=156N. The lift is slowing on its upward journey, but Newton’s law uses acceleration, not velocity.

For ideal free fall, ay=−g, giving N=0. The instrument still has its 196 N weight; the contact reading vanishes. Zero support is the contact boundary, not evidence that gravity has switched off. This is a model calculation, not a suggested lift experiment.

4.15 A rope pulls along itself

A taut flexible rope exerts a pull along its local direction. Call the magnitude of that pull the tension, T. An ideal slack rope exerts no tension; an ideal flexible rope cannot transmit a compressive push. For a hanging object, tension equals weight only when the other conditions imply zero vertical acceleration.

We often model a rope as light enough to neglect its mass and inextensible, meaning that its length does not change appreciably. These are separate assumptions. Negligible mass lets the tension have the same magnitude along a straight rope with no other loads: a difference between the endpoint pulls would otherwise provide a nonzero resultant on negligible mass. Inextensibility links the motions of its ends while it is taut. Neither assumption follows merely from drawing a line.

The third law pairs the force of the rope on an attached object with the force of that object on the rope. It does not, by itself, make the tensions at two distant ends of a massive rope equal. A rope with appreciable mass can require a force difference to support or accelerate itself. Likewise, friction at a contact can change tension along a rope. We will use a straight, negligible-mass connector here and leave general pulley networks aside.

Example 4.14 · Raising a sample

Question. A 2.0 kg sample hangs from a vertical taut light rope. It accelerates upwards at 1.5 m/s². Find the tension, neglecting air effects. Compare with lowering the sample at constant velocity.

Working. On the sample alone, the rope pulls up and weight acts down. With upward positive,

T−mg=may,T=m(g+ay)=(2.0kg)(9.8+1.5)ms−2=22.6N.

At constant velocity, acceleration is zero even if the velocity points down, so T=mg=19.6N.

Interpretation. The upward acceleration requires tension greater than weight. Constant downward velocity does not require tension less than weight. If the rope became slack, the tension model and motion constraint would change; we would not continue imposing the rope’s previous acceleration on the sample.

Activity 4.15 · Can a rope provide the calculated force?

Allow 15 minutes. A 1.5 kg package is lowered with downward acceleration 2.0 m/s² by a vertical taut light cord. Find its tension. A proposed second motion would accelerate it downwards at 12 m/s² with only cord tension and weight present. Test that proposal. Explain why the third law does not require tension to equal the package’s weight in either calculation.

Hint for Activity 4.15

Keep upward positive. A negative required T would be a downward push by a flexible cord.

Discussion of Activity 4.15

First, T=1.5(9.8−2.0)=11.7N. For the proposal, T=1.5(9.8−12)=−3.3N, which is incompatible with a tension magnitude. With only gravity and an upward cord pull, the downward acceleration cannot exceed g; a slack cord would give free fall, not 12 m/s². An additional downward interaction would be required.

Weight is Earth on package; tension is cord on package. Their recipients are the same, so they are not a third-law pair. Their difference supplies the package’s resultant.

4.16 Static friction adjusts up to a limit

Place a box on a rough horizontal bench and increase a horizontal pull gradually. At first the box can remain at rest. The bench exerts a parallel contact force that balances the pull. That is static friction: the contacting surfaces are not sliding relative to each other. The word static describes the relative state at the contact, not a requirement that the whole system be at rest in every frame.

For a simple dry-contact model, the magnitude fs satisfies

0≤fs≤μsN.

The coefficient μs is dimensionless and refers to the pair of surfaces and their condition. The product μsN is the maximum available static-friction magnitude, not the force that must always act. A box resting on a level bench without any horizontal tendency to slip may have fs=0 despite a nonzero normal force and nonzero coefficient.

To find static friction, first suppose that the surfaces stick. Use the required motion—rest, in our first examples—to find the necessary parallel force and its direction. Then compare its magnitude with μsN. If the required value is below the limit, sticking is possible in the model. If it exceeds the limit, the assumed state fails. Equality is the limiting or impending-slip condition: the model is at the boundary of static support, not a guarantee of a measurable sliding speed.

Example 4.15 · Required force versus available force

Question. A 4.0 kg box rests on a horizontal bench with μs=0.50. A cord pulls horizontally right with 12 N. Find the friction and test whether the box can remain at rest. Then test a 22 N pull.

Working. There are no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration, so N=mg=39.2N. The available limit is

fs,max=0.50(39.2N)=19.6N.

For rest under the 12 N pull, the horizontal sum requires 12 N of friction left. This is within the limit, so it is an admissible static solution. The actual friction is 12 N, not 19.6 N.

Under 22 N, rest would require 22 N left, exceeding 19.6 N. The box cannot remain at rest under the stated model. Once it slides, its friction must be calculated using a sliding model; 19.6 N is not automatically the continuing resistance.

Check. Using the maximum friction at a smaller pull would predict acceleration to the left for a box initially at rest with a rightward pull. That result is a warning that a capacity was mistaken for an actual force.

Direction deserves the same care. Static friction opposes the relative slipping that would otherwise occur at the contact. If a moving conveyor belt carries a box without slip while accelerating right, friction on the box may point right to accelerate it. Without friction the box would fall behind the belt. A “friction always points opposite velocity” rule would fail even though the dry-contact model remains useful.

Activity 4.16 · Two pulls, one contact

Allow 20 minutes. A 2.0 kg block is initially at rest on a horizontal bench with μs=0.40. Two horizontal cords pull it, one 10 N right and one 4 N left. Find the static friction required for rest and test its feasibility. Repeat after the rightward pull becomes 14 N. Finally, explain why the original friction is neither μsN nor 10 N.

Hint for Activity 4.16

Friction responds to the net tendency produced by both pulls. Find the normal separately.

Discussion of Activity 4.16

The normal is 19.6 N, so the static limit is 7.84 N. Initially the two cords give 6 N right; rest requires 6 N of friction left, which is feasible. With the 14 N rightward pull, the cords give 10 N right. Required friction is now 10 N left, beyond the limit, so rest is impossible in this model.

The original 6 N balances both cord contributions together. The 7.84 N capacity is a bound, while 10 N is only one part of the external horizontal inventory. Neither is the actual initial friction.

4.17 Sliding friction uses a different rule

When surfaces slide relative to each other, a commonly useful dry-friction approximation is

fk=μkN,

where fk is the sliding, or kinetic, friction magnitude and μk is its dimensionless coefficient. On the selected object, the direction is opposite its sliding relative to the other surface. Determine that relative motion first; the acceleration can point either way. In many ordinary dry contacts μk is smaller than the limiting static coefficient, which helps explain why starting a box can take a larger pull than sustaining its sliding.

These are empirical models, not exact universal laws like a vector identity. Surface condition, speed, deformation and lubrication can matter. We use supplied coefficients within stated situations rather than infer a universal number from the material’s name. Rolling resistance, a wheel digging into loose soil, and fluid drag are not automatically represented by the same block-sliding formula. Figure 4.13 is useful precisely because it reminds us how much our bench model leaves out.

Example 4.16 · After sliding begins

Question. A 4.0 kg box is already sliding right on a horizontal bench. A horizontal cord pulls right with 18 N and μk=0.30. Find its acceleration. What is the acceleration just after the cord goes slack while the box still slides right?

Working. Vertical balance gives N=39.2N. Sliding friction has magnitude 0.30(39.2)=11.76N and points left. Therefore

ax=18N−11.76N4.0kg=1.56ms−2≈1.6ms−2.

After the pull ends, the resultant is 11.76 N left, so ax=−2.94ms−2, approximately −2.9ms−2.

Interpretation. The friction magnitude is unchanged over these two sliding intervals because the normal and coefficient are unchanged. At zero sliding speed the kinetic rule no longer determines what happens. With no horizontal pull remaining on a level bench, a static solution with zero friction is possible. The model does not make the box accelerate backwards after stopping.

Key idea 4.5 · Decide the contact regime

For sticking, calculate the required friction and test fs≤μsN. For sliding, use fk=μkN opposite relative sliding. At a transition, reconsider the regime. In both cases, determine N from the actual perpendicular forces and motion.

Activity 4.17 · Friction can point with the applied pull

Allow 15 minutes. A 2.0 kg block slides left on a stationary horizontal bench while a cord pulls right with 8 N. Let μk=0.20 and μs=0.50. Find the acceleration while it slides left. Can it remain at rest when it stops, if the 8 N pull is maintained? Explain the friction direction and magnitude on each side of that transition.

Hint for Activity 4.17

While sliding left, both the cord and friction act right. At rest, test the friction required to balance the cord.

Discussion of Activity 4.17

The normal is 19.6 N. While sliding left, friction is 0.20(19.6)=3.92N right. Hence ax=(8+3.92)/2=5.96ms−2 right, reducing the leftward speed.

At rest, equilibrium requires 8 N of friction left. The static limit is 0.50(19.6)=9.8N, so sticking is possible. The friction changes direction and magnitude when the state changes. Continuing the original rightward kinetic force beyond the stopping instant would use the wrong model.

4.18 Choose axes that suit a slope

On an incline, gravity remains vertical while the normal remains perpendicular to the surface. Instead of resolving every contact into horizontal and vertical components, choose one axis down the ramp and another outwards normal to it. A block that stays on a straight, fixed ramp has zero acceleration in the normal direction, even when it accelerates along the slope.

Let the ramp make angle θ with the horizontal, between 0° and 90°. The inward normal makes that same angle with the downward vertical: rotating two perpendicular directions by the same amount preserves their angle. In the right triangle formed by resolving weight, the normal component magnitude is adjacent to θ, and the downslope component is opposite it. Thus the weight components are

W∥=mgsinθ,W⟂=−mgcosθ,

where positive parallel is down the ramp and positive perpendicular is outwards. The minus sign on W⟂ records that weight points into the surface. These are components of one force, not two additional interactions. Use them in place of the original weight vector when summing.

There is a useful check before calculating. At θ=0, a horizontal bench has no downslope weight component and the full weight acts normally inwards. As θ grows, the downslope component increases and the perpendicular one decreases. A formula that gives the full weight along a level bench has interchanged sine and cosine.

With only weight and the ramp’s contact force present, normal balance gives N=mgcosθ. If the block is at rest, parallel balance requires static friction fs=mgsinθ uphill. Sticking is possible only if mgsinθ≤μsmgcosθ. For 0≤θ<90∘ we may divide by positive mgcosθ, obtaining tanθ≤μs. This shortcut belongs to this particular inventory; an extra rope or push can change both the required friction and normal.

Example 4.17 · Test rest before calculating sliding

Question. A 3.0 kg block is placed at rest on a 30° fixed ramp. Let μs=0.40 and μk=0.30. Neglect air effects. Can it remain at rest? If not, find its acceleration as it begins sliding down.

Working. Resolve the 29.4 N weight:

N=29.4cos30∘N≈25.46N,W∥=29.4sin30∘N=14.7N.

Rest needs 14.7 N of friction uphill, but the static limit is 0.40(25.46)N≈10.18N. Rest fails. When the block slides downhill, kinetic friction is 0.30(25.46)N≈7.64N uphill. Using the unrounded values,

a∥=g(sin30∘−μkcos30∘)≈2.35ms−2.

Interpretation. The acceleration is downslope. We used the static coefficient to decide whether rest was possible and the kinetic coefficient for the subsequent sliding acceleration. Substituting one coefficient everywhere would merge two physically different questions.

Activity 4.18 · A changed surface, then a changed pull

Allow 20 minutes. Keep the mass and 30° ramp of Example 4.17, but let μs=0.60. Find the actual static friction when the block is at rest and test the limit. Then a taut cord pulls uphill along the ramp with 18 N. Recalculate the static friction needed for rest, including its direction. Does the extra pull change the normal force in this geometry?

Hint for Activity 4.18

Compare the uphill pull with the downslope weight component before deciding which way slipping would occur.

Discussion of Activity 4.18

The normal remains about 25.46 N, with a static limit about 15.28 N. Without the cord, the actual friction is 14.7 N uphill, which is feasible. With 18 N uphill, the other parallel forces have a 3.3 N uphill sum, so static friction must be 3.3 N downhill. This is also within the limit.

The cord is parallel to the ramp, so it has no perpendicular component and does not change N while contact is maintained. An angled cord could do so. Friction did not stay uphill merely because the block is on a slope: its direction followed the changed tendency to slip.

4.19 A model must satisfy its own conditions

We can now return to the angled cord in Lesson 1 with enough information to calculate. A pull above the horizontal does two things at once: its horizontal component can change motion, while its vertical component reduces the support needed from the bench. Since the dry-friction model depends on normal force, changing the cord angle can change the friction even if the tension magnitude is held fixed.

Example 4.18 · Pulling a sliding box at an angle

Question. A 5.0 kg box slides right on a horizontal bench. A cord pulls with tension 20 N at 30° above the rightward horizontal. Let μk=0.20. Find its acceleration and check whether the assumed contact is possible. Neglect air effects.

Working. The vertical acceleration is zero while contact is maintained. Resolve the cord pull into Tcos30∘ right and Tsin30∘ up:

N+Tsin30∘−mg=0,N=49N−10N=39N.

The positive normal is consistent with contact. Friction is 0.20(39)=7.8N left. Therefore

ax=Tcos30∘−μkNm=20cos30∘−7.85.0ms−2≈1.90ms−2.

Interpretation. A calculation using N=49N would overestimate friction by ignoring the upward cord component. A calculation using the full 20 N as the horizontal pull would make a different error. Both can be avoided by resolving once and keeping the vertical and horizontal equations distinct.

Notice the order of reasoning. We stated the system and sliding direction, chose axes, solved the constrained vertical motion, checked that the resulting support was possible, and only then evaluated friction and horizontal acceleration. This order is a guide rather than a formula checklist: the physical situation determines which constraint and force model are justified.

Our model also has a time boundary. If the cord tension, angle or surface changes, the resultant can change. If sliding stops, static friction may take over. A constant acceleration can be used to predict later motion only over an interval for which the forces and mass really keep it constant. That is the connection to Lesson 4.

Activity 4.19 · One complete force-model decision

Allow 20 minutes. A 4.0 kg crate is initially at rest on a horizontal bench. A cord pulls 16 N at 30° above the rightward horizontal. Take μs=0.50 and μk=0.30. Find the normal, the friction required for rest and the static limit. Decide whether the crate starts to move from rest. Then find its acceleration if it is instead already sliding right under the same pull. Explain why these two initial states can give different answers even with the same mass, coefficients and pull.

Hint for Activity 4.19

The upward cord component reduces N. The horizontal component is the friction required for rest. Do not select μk until sliding is specified.

Discussion of Activity 4.19

Vertical balance gives N=39.2−16sin30∘=31.2N. Rest requires fs=16cos30∘N≈13.86N left. The available static limit is 0.50(31.2)=15.6N, so the initially resting crate can remain at rest.

If it is already sliding right, the friction is 0.30(31.2)=9.36N left, and

ax=16cos30∘−9.364.0ms−2≈1.12ms−2.

The sliding state accelerates right. Static and kinetic models supply different friction values; the physical state must therefore accompany the numerical parameters. The positive normal validates maintained contact in both cases. A complete solution labels the three agents—Earth, cord and bench—and represents the bench’s normal and friction components without adding a duplicate total contact arrow.

Looking ahead

The laws of motion become predictive only when the forces are modelled with enough care. Weight, support, tension and friction do not all answer the same question: some values follow from a field, others from motion constraints, and others from a contact regime with a limit. Checking those roles can be more important than the final arithmetic.

We can now obtain a constant acceleration for suitable intervals. The next lesson develops the algebra that turns such an acceleration, together with initial conditions, into a prediction of velocity and position.
