P101 · Block 2 · Unit 4 · Lesson 5

Projectile motion
and unit synthesis

A ball released with an upward and a horizontal velocity does not need an extra “forward force” to keep travelling sideways. If gravity is the only significant force, its horizontal velocity stays constant while its vertical velocity changes. Combining those two statements gives a curved path.

This final lesson brings the unit’s argument together: choose a system, identify the interactions, state a model, resolve the equations, and check a prediction against its conditions. We will first calculate a trajectory by hand and then evaluate the same algebra in Python. A plot will make the prediction easier to inspect; it will not supply experimental evidence that the assumptions are true.

Allow five hours in total: 35 minutes for the physical explanation, reference and conclusion; 25 minutes for Activity 4.26; 120 minutes for the Python investigation, including its code explanation and checks; 75 minutes for the cumulative unit exercises; and 45 minutes for solutions, correction and revision. The downloads are alternative ways to do the same investigation, not extra assignments. The exercise set covers the whole unit and can be attempted in shorter sessions.

4.26 Two components describe one object

We use projectile for an object whose motion after release is modelled with gravity as the only significant force. A thrown ball is a useful example over a suitable short flight. A powered aircraft, a rocket under thrust or an object still being pushed by a launcher has a different force inventory. Neglecting air resistance is an approximation to justify for the intended use, not a property of every object moving through air.

Choose a horizontal x axis positive to the right and a vertical y axis positive upwards. Assume a constant mass and a uniform downward gravitational field. The free-body diagram has one force, mg, so the component equations are max=0 and may=−mg. Hence ax=0 and ay=−g. The horizontal and vertical calculations have different accelerations, but describe the same object at the same elapsed time t.

Let release be t=0, from position (x0,y0) with velocity components (ux,uy). Applying Lesson 4 separately to each axis gives

x=x0+uxt,vx=ux,
y=y0+uyt−12gt2,vy=uy−gt.

The phrase independent components means that changing ux alone does not enter either vertical equation in this model. It does not mean that we may choose one time for x and another for y when locating the object. Nor is independence a universal rule for every force model: air resistance can depend on the velocity as a whole and couple the component calculations.

If the launch is specified by speed u and angle θ above the rightward horizontal, retrieve the vector triangle from M101: ux=ucosθ and uy=usinθ. These are signed components, while u is a nonnegative magnitude. For our upward-right launches, both components are positive. A horizontal launch has uy=0 but usually has ux≠0; it is not a release from rest.

Example 4.25 · Locate a ball and describe its motion

A model ball is released from (x0,y0)=(0,4.9m) with ux=6.0ms−1 and uy=4.9ms−1. Use g=9.8ms−2. Find its position and velocity after 0.50 s.

The horizontal calculation is x=6(0.50)=3.0m and vx=6.0ms−1. Vertically,

y=4.9+4.9(0.50)−4.9(0.50)2=6.125m,
vy=4.9−9.8(0.50)=0ms−1.

The ball is at its greatest height and still moves right at 6.0 m/s. Its vertical velocity is zero, not its whole velocity. Acceleration remains 9.8 m/s² down. The vertical force does not have to point along the curved path or along the current velocity.

For ux≠0, eliminate time by writing t=(x−x0)/ux. Substitution into the vertical relation gives

y=y0+uyux(x−x0)−g2ux2(x−x0)2.

This quadratic relation describes a parabola. It is a graph of position against position, unlike Lesson 4’s velocity–time graphs. The original equations in t are often more useful because they retain timing and also work for a purely vertical launch, where division by ux would be invalid. In every representation, keep only the portion before the first relevant collision.

4.27 Let the landing condition choose the time

An equation for a trajectory does not by itself state where the flight ends. A floor, a raised platform or a wall supplies an event condition. For a horizontal floor at y=0, release from height h=y0≥0 gives the landing equation

0=h+uyt−12gt2.

Rearrange to 12gt2−uyt−h=0 and use the quadratic formula. With g>0, the two algebraic candidates are

t=uy±uy2+2ghg.

For h>0, the square root is larger than |uy|, so the minus choice is negative and the plus choice is positive. The positive root describes the future crossing of the floor level, provided no obstacle ends the flight earlier. We do not choose a root because “quadratics always use plus”; we choose it because it satisfies the event and the interval. For a raised landing height, two positive crossing times can exist, and a statement such as “on the descent” matters.

Example 4.26 · Finish the trajectory

Find where the ball in Example 4.25 reaches the floor and its velocity just before contact.

Using h=4.9m and uy=4.9ms−1, the landing equation, after expressing numerical values in SI units, is t2−t−1=0. Thus

tf=1+52s≈1.618s.

The other root is approximately −0.618 s, outside the interval after release. Horizontal displacement is xf−x0=uxtf≈9.708m. Just before contact,

vx=6.0ms−1,vy=4.9−9.8(1.618…)≈−10.957ms−1.

The speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector,

|v|=vx2+vy2≈12.49ms−1.

Its direction is approximately tan−1(10.957/6.0)=61.3∘ below the rightward horizontal. We used positive component magnitudes for the acute angle and then stated the downward direction explicitly. Substitution of the unrounded landing time into y(t) gives zero, checking the event condition. The model gives the velocity immediately before contact; stopping or bouncing requires a new contact model.

An equal-height launch and landing is a useful special case. For h=0 and uy>0, factor the landing equation as t(uy−12gt)=0. Release is the root t=0; the later return is tf=2uy/g. Therefore the horizontal range is R=2uxuy/g. If a speed and angle were supplied, this becomes R=2u2sinθcosθ/g. These expressions require the same launch and landing height. For a horizontal launch from a raised bench, using 2uy/g=0 would erase the actual fall time.

Example 4.27 · A horizontal launch is not a zero-duration flight

A ball leaves a horizontal ledge at 3.0 m/s from a height of 4.9 m. Neglect drag and take g=9.8ms−2. Find its flight time and horizontal displacement to a floor at zero height.

Here uy=0, so 0=4.9−4.9t2 gives the future root tf=1.0s. The horizontal displacement is R=3.0(1.0)=3.0m. A second ball released from rest from the same height, at the same time, has exactly the same vertical equation and reaches the floor simultaneously, if neither hits an obstacle first. Its horizontal displacement is zero.

Different horizontal velocities give different landing positions, but not different fall times under these shared vertical conditions. A real experiment would need to control release height, vertical launch velocity and air effects before using agreement or disagreement to assess the model.

4.28 Change one initial condition at a time

We can now make a prediction before drawing another curve. Hold height, vertical launch velocity and gravity fixed; double only ux. The landing equation is unchanged, so flight time is unchanged. Every horizontal coordinate at a given time doubles relative to the launch point. The path becomes wider, but its maximum height does not change. Holding the speed fixed while changing launch angle is a different comparison, because it changes both components.

The exploration below evaluates the same algebra with upward-positive coordinates, a floor at zero height and g=9.8ms−2. It shows the full predicted path immediately; there is no moving animation to pause. The axes keep equal metre scales so that changes in shape are meaningful. The tabulated values remain available without the controls.

Activity 4.26 · Predict, vary, explain

Allow 25 minutes. Start with h=4.9m, uy=4.9ms−1 and ux=6.0ms−1. Predict the effect of changing only ux to 3.0 and then 9.0 m/s. Check the explorer or calculate from the equations. Record the flight time, maximum height and range for all three cases.

Next return to the default and set uy=0 while retaining height 4.9 m. Explain what changes and why. Finally consider h=0, uy=0: does the gravity-only floor model contain a positive-duration airborne interval? Write two sentences separating what these comparisons verify about the equations from what they establish about real balls.

Hint for Activity 4.26

For the first comparison use the same landing time from Example 4.26. At maximum height vy=0, so ttop=uy/g. Without JavaScript, use the default table and multiply its horizontal coordinates by one-half or three-halves.

Discussion of Activity 4.26

All three upward-launch cases have tf≈1.618s and maximum height 6.125 m. Their ranges are approximately 4.854, 9.708 and 14.562 m for horizontal velocities 3, 6 and 9 m/s respectively. No vertical equation contains ux.

With uy=0 and height 4.9 m, flight time becomes 1.0 s, maximum height is the initial 4.9 m, and the range at ux=6 m/s is 6.0 m. With both h=0 and uy=0, the future floor crossing is already at release: the model has no positive-duration airborne interval. A ball on a floor would require a contact model, not a plotted trajectory beneath the floor.

These checks confirm internal relationships of the stipulated gravity-only model and its implementation. They do not measure drag or prove that a particular real launch meets its conditions.

4.29 Make a calculation another person can rerun

The following code explanation and Activity 4.27 belong to the 120-minute Python allocation. Use the NumPy and Matplotlib environment from Unit 3. Download the investigation working folder, or use the notebook or plain Python companion. The folder includes a short README and model metadata. Keep predictions and interpretations alongside the calculations.

We will evaluate the algebraic trajectory directly at chosen times. This is not a step-by-step approximation to an unknown equation of motion. Increasing the number of plotted points makes the joining line smoother; it does not introduce air resistance or make the physical assumptions more realistic.

Separate the event calculation from the position calculation. The first function returns the later floor-crossing time for finite inputs with h≥0, uy≥0 and g>0. This limited input contract matches the investigation; the function does not validate arbitrary user input. The second returns both position components at the same time. Returning two comma-separated values lets the caller assign them to two names in the same order.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def landing_time(h_m, uy_m_s, g_m_s2):
    # Contract: finite h >= 0, uy >= 0, g > 0; floor at y = 0.
    return (uy_m_s + np.sqrt(uy_m_s**2 + 2*g_m_s2*h_m)) / g_m_s2

def position(t_s, h_m, ux_m_s, uy_m_s, g_m_s2):
    # x0 = 0; t is a scalar or NumPy array of elapsed seconds.
    x_m = ux_m_s * t_s
    y_m = h_m + uy_m_s*t_s - 0.5*g_m_s2*t_s**2
    return x_m, y_m

np.sqrt takes a nonnegative square root; **2 squares a value or each entry of an array. Here the radicand is nonnegative under the stated contract. Parameters include units in their names to make a mistaken centimetre or millisecond input easier to notice. For a physical flight, use times from zero to the calculated landing time; the algebra itself will otherwise continue beneath the floor.

Before plotting, choose a hand benchmark that tests something specific. For a horizontal launch from 4.9 m under g=9.8 m/s², the flight time must be 1 s. At 6 m/s horizontally it must land at x=6 m. A zero-time check tests the starting position, and a floor check tests the event substitution. They address different possible faults.

g_m_s2 = 9.8
benchmark_tf_s = landing_time(4.9, 0.0, g_m_s2)
benchmark_x_m, benchmark_y_m = position(benchmark_tf_s, 4.9, 6.0, 0.0, g_m_s2)
print("Benchmark time, x, y:", benchmark_tf_s, benchmark_x_m, benchmark_y_m)

h_m = 4.9
ux_m_s = 6.0
uy_m_s = 4.9
tf_s = landing_time(h_m, uy_m_s, g_m_s2)
time_s = np.linspace(0.0, tf_s, 101)
x_m, y_m = position(time_s, h_m, ux_m_s, uy_m_s, g_m_s2)
print("Initial x, y:", x_m[0], y_m[0])
print("Landing time, x, y:", tf_s, x_m[-1], y_m[-1])
print("Floor check:", abs(y_m[-1]) < 1e-10)

Recall that np.linspace includes both endpoints by default, and index -1 selects the last array entry. Floating-point arithmetic may leave a tiny residual such as 10−15 m instead of exact zero. The final comparison uses a numerical tolerance of 10−10 m for this modest-scale calculation. That tolerance is an implementation check, not a measurement uncertainty or a claim about physical accuracy. The initial output should be (0,4.9) m, and the final values should agree with Example 4.26.

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4), layout="constrained")
ax.plot(x_m, y_m, label="Uniform gravity; no drag")
ax.plot([x_m[0], x_m[-1]], [y_m[0], y_m[-1]], "o", label="Release / landing")
ax.set_xlabel("Horizontal position x / m")
ax.set_ylabel("Height y / m")
ax.set_title("P101 U04: predicted projectile path")
ax.set_aspect("equal", adjustable="box")
ax.grid(True)
ax.legend(loc="lower left")
fig.savefig("projectile-path.png", dpi=160)
plt.show()

The horizontal array is position, not time: this is a path in space. set_aspect("equal", adjustable="box") makes one metre the same displayed length on both axes by adjusting the plotting box. Without equal spatial scales, a path can look steeper or flatter merely because of the plotting choices. The release and landing markers identify the interval. The line between computed points visualises the known continuous model; it is not a set of experimental observations.

If a problem gives speed and launch angle, convert the angle before using NumPy’s trigonometric functions. np.sin and np.cos expect radians. np.deg2rad converts degrees to radians; it does not alter the speed’s unit. For a 10 m/s launch at 30°:

speed_m_s = 10.0
angle_deg = 30.0
angle_rad = np.deg2rad(angle_deg)
ux_from_angle = speed_m_s * np.cos(angle_rad)
uy_from_angle = speed_m_s * np.sin(angle_rad)
print("Launch components:", ux_from_angle, uy_from_angle)

The expected components are approximately 8.660 m/s and 5.000 m/s. Their squared sum should equal u2=100m2s−2. That check alone does not detect every angle error: using 30 as radians still preserves the sine–cosine identity. A known-angle check such as the vertical component at 30° is needed as well. Choose checks for the error you want to expose.

  5. Restart the kernel and run in order, or rerun the companion in a fresh process. Hint for Activity 4.27

Recompute tf_s after changing a vertical input. Recompute the arrays after changing any input. Use a new output filename for each plot. At the top, calculate ttop=uy/g and evaluate the position function there; the sampled time array need not contain the exact top.

Discussion of Activity 4.27

The horizontal benchmark gives 1 s, 6 m and zero height. For the default upward launch, tf≈1.618034 s, range is about 9.708204 m and the maximum height is 6.125 m at 0.50 s. Changing only ux changes the range in direct proportion but leaves flight time and maximum height unchanged.

Both 21 and 101 points include the same endpoints, so their landing results agree. The coarser joining line is less smooth; neither is a new physical model. Find the top from the analytic event rather than assuming the largest sampled height is exactly the maximum.

For the 10 m/s, 30° launch from 4.9 m, the correct vertical component is 5 m/s, giving tf≈1.632839 s and range about 14.140797 m. Omitting degree conversion gives 10sin(30)≈−9.880 m/s, immediately contradicting an upward 30° launch and violating this investigation’s nonnegative-uy contract. The squared-component check still returns approximately 100; it cannot distinguish the intended angle from the wrong one.

A satisfactory report lets another reader reproduce the inputs and result, explains why its checks discriminate between plausible errors, and separates implementation evidence from physical validation. A possible conclusion is: “The plotted endpoints and benchmark agree with the uniform-gravity equations after correcting the angle conversion. The range comparison follows the model’s component independence; no measured trajectory was supplied, so the size of real air effects remains untested.” Your own report should refer to your actual checks and corrections.

4.30 Exercises for Unit 4

These twelve core exercises cover the unit from force inventories to checked trajectories. Allow 75 minutes for attempts, using the individual estimates as a guide rather than a deadline. Use the unit’s 45-minute closing feedback and revision allocation to compare methods, diagnose errors and revisit a representative problem. Make an attempt before opening a solution. Draw a labelled force diagram whenever it supports the reasoning, keep intermediate values unrounded, and use g=9.8ms−2 unless stated otherwise.

Exercise 4.1 · Choose the system 5 minutes

A book rests on a tray, and a hand holds the tray at rest. Air effects are negligible. Draw separate free-body diagrams for (a) the book and (b) the book plus tray. Name the agent and recipient of every force. Identify one force that is external in (a) but internal in (b), and explain why no “force of rest” belongs in either diagram.

Hint for Exercise 4.1

Cross the boundary only once for each interaction; include gravity on every massive part.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.1

On the book alone: Earth on book down, tray on book up. On book plus tray: Earth on book down, Earth on tray down, and hand on tray up. The two gravitational contributions may be represented by their combined weight if labelled accordingly. The contact pair between book and tray is internal to the combined system and is omitted from its external inventory.

Tray on book changes status when the boundary expands. Its partner, book on tray, acts on a different recipient; the pair cancels in the combined force sum. Rest describes motion, not an interaction with an agent. A correct diagram uses force arrows only and does not add both the combined weight and its two separate contributions.

Exercise 4.2 · Motion does not supply a force arrow 4 minutes

A puck is moving left on a level surface. Its only horizontal forces are 5 N right and 2 N left, and its mass is 1.5 kg. Find its acceleration and state whether its speed is initially increasing or decreasing. What horizontal resultant is required for a subsequent interval of constant leftward velocity?

Worked solution to Exercise 4.2

Choose right positive. The resultant is 5−2=3N, so ax=3/1.5=2.0ms−2 right. Its initial velocity is leftward, so its speed initially decreases. Constant leftward velocity requires zero acceleration and hence zero horizontal resultant. It does not require a persistent leftward resultant. The word “initially” matters because the later motion depends on whether the forces continue through a stop.

Exercise 4.3 · Find the balancing interaction 6 minutes

A small ring is in translational equilibrium under three in-plane forces. Two are (6,−2) N and (−1,14) N in right/up component order. Find the third force’s components and magnitude. A cord providing that third force must point into which quadrant? Would a cord pulling directly down be sufficient?

Hint for Exercise 4.3

Set each component sum to zero before calculating a magnitude.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.3

The two known forces sum to (5,12) N. The third must therefore be (−5,−12) N, with magnitude (−5)2+(−12)2N=13N. It points down-left, in the third quadrant. A directly downward cord could balance the vertical sum but would leave 5 N right unbalanced. Magnitude alone does not establish equilibrium; the direction must also be possible for the actual interaction.

Exercise 4.4 · Equal forces, unequal changes 5 minutes

Two gliders, A of mass 1 kg and B of mass 4 kg, interact briefly on a level air track. During a specified instant, A exerts 8 N right on B and no other horizontal forces act. Find each horizontal acceleration. A student says the forces “cancel, so neither glider accelerates”. Repair the statement by naming the relevant system in each claim.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.4

By the third law, B exerts 8 N left on A. Thus aA=−8/1=−8ms−2 and aB=+8/4=+2ms−2. The force magnitudes agree; the accelerations do not because the masses differ.

The pair cancels in the external-force sum for A plus B because it is internal to that system. It does not cancel on either glider’s own diagram. The gliders do not share one common acceleration, so the common-translation shortcut used for connected carts cannot assign a single acceleration to both. No additional “reaction force” should be drawn on the wrong recipient.

Exercise 4.5 · Support and tension are conditional 5 minutes

A 2 kg package is (a) resting on a level bench while a hand pulls vertically up with 6 N, and (b) hanging from a vertical cord while accelerating down at 1.0 m/s². Find the bench normal in (a) and cord tension in (b). Which stated interaction model fails if the hand’s upward pull in (a) is increased to 25 N while rest on the bench is still assumed?

Worked solution to Exercise 4.5

Weight is 19.6 N. In (a), N+6−19.6=0, giving N=13.6N. In (b), upward positive gives T−19.6=2(−1), so T=17.6N. Neither force must equal weight merely because it points upwards.

For the proposed 25 N pull with maintained rest, N=19.6−25=−5.4N. A nonadhesive bench cannot provide that downward normal force. The maintained-contact assumption fails; after separation N=0 and the package accelerates upwards while the pull continues.

Exercise 4.6 · A connector disappears from one equation 6 minutes

Carts A and B of masses 3 kg and 1 kg are joined by a straight taut light inextensible horizontal cord. On a level track with negligible horizontal resistance, a 12 N external pull acts right on A; A is to the right of B. Find their common acceleration and the tension. Check the result using A alone. At the stated instant both carts move left: does this change the calculated acceleration?

Hint for Exercise 4.6

First select both carts and the negligible-mass connector. Then select B alone.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.6

For the combined translating system, internal cord interactions cancel, leaving 12=(3+1)a and a=3.0ms−2 right. For B alone the cord supplies its horizontal resultant, so T=(1)(3)=3.0N. On A, 12−T=12−3=9N, giving aA=9/3=3.0ms−2 as required.

The current leftward velocity does not alter these force equations. Both carts are initially slowing. The shared acceleration relies on the taut inextensible connector and geometry; the tension is external for either cart alone but internal to the combined system.

Exercise 4.7 · Resolve before choosing friction 7 minutes

A 2 kg block is initially at rest on a level bench. A cord pulls with 8 N at 30° above the rightward horizontal. The coefficients are μs=0.40 and μk=0.20. Decide whether rest is possible; if it is not, find the initial rightward sliding acceleration. Explain why using N=mg would give the wrong test.

Hint for Exercise 4.7

The cord’s vertical component changes the normal and therefore both friction limits.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.7

Vertical balance during contact gives N=19.6−8sin30∘=15.6N, positive as required. The horizontal pull is 8cos30∘≈6.928N. Rest needs that much friction left, but the static limit is 0.40(15.6)=6.24N. Rest is impossible.

As the block slides right, friction is 0.20(15.6)=3.12N left, so ax=(6.928…−3.12)/2≈1.904ms−2. Using N=mg ignores the upward cord component and would overestimate the contact capacity, even predicting a feasible static state here. The order of the model checks changes the physical conclusion.

Exercise 4.8 · A slope and two possible states 7 minutes

A 2 kg block lies on a straight 30° ramp, with μs=0.70 and μk=0.20. Can it remain at rest? If instead it is already sliding down at 2.0 m/s, find its speed after a further 3.0 m down the ramp. Assume the ramp is long enough and the coefficients remain applicable.

Hint for Exercise 4.8

Use parallel and perpendicular axes. The initial state determines which coefficient is relevant.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.8

The normal is N=19.6cos30∘≈16.974N. Rest requires friction 19.6sin30∘=9.8N uphill. The static limit is about 11.882 N, so rest is possible.

During downhill sliding, a∥=g(sin30∘−0.20cos30∘)≈3.203ms−2 downhill. The acceleration is constant in this model, so

v=+(2.0ms−1)2+2a∥(3.0m)≈4.82ms−1.

The positive root denotes downhill motion. There is no contradiction between remaining at rest and accelerating when already sliding: the two regimes supply different friction forces.

Exercise 4.9 · Read the turn, not just the endpoint 6 minutes

A particle has u=4.0ms−1 and constant a=−2.0ms−2 for 3.0 s. Sketch its velocity–time graph. Find its final velocity, displacement and distance travelled. Explain what additional assumption would be needed if the question supplied only the initial and final velocities and duration.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.9

The graph is a straight line from (0,4) to (3,−2), crossing zero at 2 s. Final velocity is v=4−2(3)=−2.0ms−1. Displacement is s=4(3)−12(2)(3)2=3.0m. The positive triangle contributes 4 m and the negative triangle −1 m, so distance is 5 m.

Two velocity endpoints and a duration establish average acceleration but not the path between them. Constant acceleration throughout is the additional assumption that justifies the straight graph and these area calculations.

Exercise 4.10 · At the top and back again 7 minutes

A ball is released straight up at 14.7 m/s. Take its release height as zero and neglect air effects. Find the time and height of the top, the later time of return to release height, and its velocity at that return. State its acceleration at the top and total distance travelled by the return. Does choosing a different position origin change these physical results?

Worked solution to Exercise 4.10

At the top, 0=14.7−9.8t, so t=1.5s. The rise is 14.7(1.5)−4.9(1.5)2=11.025m, about 11.0 m. The later equal-height return is at 2(14.7)/9.8=3.0s, with vy=14.7−9.8(3)=−14.7ms−1.

Acceleration at the top is still −9.8ms−2. The distance travelled is twice the rise, 22.05 m; the displacement at return is zero. Changing the position origin changes the coordinates assigned to release and top by the same constant, not their difference, the velocities or the times.

Exercise 4.11 · One flight, two components 8 minutes

A ball is launched at 10 m/s, 30° above the rightward horizontal, and lands at the same height. Neglect drag. Find the launch components, later landing time and range. At the top, is the ball at rest or in equilibrium? Explain why the equal-height time would be inappropriate if the same launch began 4.9 m above the landing floor.

Hint for Exercise 4.11

Vertical velocity, whole velocity and acceleration must be distinguished at the top.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.11

The components are ux=10cos30∘≈8.660ms−1 and uy=10sin30∘=5.0ms−1. Equal-height return gives tf=2(5)/9.8≈1.0204s, and range R=uxtf≈8.837m.

At the top, vy=0 but vx≈8.660ms−1. The ball is neither at rest nor in equilibrium: it still has downward gravitational resultant and acceleration. From a height of 4.9 m, the landing condition is 0=4.9+5t−4.9t2, not 0=5t−4.9t2. The former has a later positive root, about 1.633 s. The same-height result would stop the prediction at a height above the floor.

Exercise 4.12 · Audit a plausible-looking plot 9 minutes

A student intends to plot a horizontal launch from 4.9 m at 6 m/s under g=9.8 m/s². Their code contains:

time_s = np.linspace(0.0, 3.0, 101)
x_m = 6.0 * time_s
y_m = 4.9 - 9.8 * time_s**2

Find two independent modelling or implementation faults. Give the corrected expressions and interval. Identify one hand benchmark that exposes the position fault and one simple check that would miss it. Explain why changing 101 points to 1001 does not repair either fault, and name one kind of evidence needed before using the model for a real ball.

Worked solution to Exercise 4.12

The vertical expression omits one-half: it should be y_m = 4.9 - 0.5 * 9.8 * time_s**2. The intended floor crossing is at tf=2(4.9)/9.8=1.0s, so the flight interval should use np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 101). A three-second graph extends far beyond the first contact with the floor.

The hand benchmark y(1s)=0 exposes the missing factor: the wrong expression gives −4.9 m. The zero-time check y(0)=4.9 m passes for both expressions and therefore misses this fault. More samples only draw the wrong expression more densely over the wrong interval.

Physical validation needs measured motion under documented release conditions and an assessment of discrepancies, including air effects and measurement limitations. An initial-height photograph alone or agreement between two copies of the same formula is not enough. This question is a reasoning audit; running the faulty code is not an extra required Python assignment.

Imports: import numpy as np; import matplotlib.pyplot as plt. Finite SI inputs; h ≥ 0, uy ≥ 0, g > 0; floor y = 0. Numerical tolerance is not measurement uncertainty.

theta = np.deg2rad(angle_deg)

Consult · sampled algebraic path

tf = (uy + np.sqrt(uy**2 + 2*g*h)) / g

x = ux*t
y = h + uy*t - 0.5*g*t**2

4.32 Unit conclusion

We began by asking how a physical situation becomes a model that can explain and predict motion. The first decisive step was a boundary: selecting the object or system on which the external forces act. That choice made it possible to distinguish an interaction pair from a balance, and an internal force from a contribution to the system’s resultant.

Newton’s laws connected the resultant to a change of velocity, while the force models supplied the missing physical detail. A bench cannot provide any normal force we happen to calculate; a rope cannot push; static friction is not always at its limit. Checking those conditions determines whether an equation describes a possible state. The same care carried into prediction: a constant acceleration belongs to an interval, and an event such as stopping or landing can end that interval.

The projectile calculation brought the whole sequence together. One gravity-only inventory produced two component equations with one shared time. The hand calculation, graph and Python function offered different ways to inspect the same prediction. Their agreement is useful evidence that the calculation is implemented consistently; a comparison with measured motion is still needed to judge the physical approximation.

Before leaving the unit, choose one exercise where your first reasoning failed and write a corrected explanation without copying the solution. Locate the first wrong decision—boundary, force direction, model condition, sign, event or code—not merely the last wrong number. You now have a basis for studying motion whose direction keeps changing, where a steady speed need not mean zero acceleration. That is the next curriculum question; this unit’s work is complete once you have consolidated the reasoning and practice here.
