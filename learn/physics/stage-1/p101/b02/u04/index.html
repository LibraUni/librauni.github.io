P101 · Block 2 · Motion, forces and conservation

Unit 4
Interactions, diagrams
and motion

Imagine pulling a loaded tray across a bench with a cord. You can watch its position change and time the journey, as in Unit 3. But suppose you want to predict what happens when the cord is released, the load is increased or the surface is changed. A description fitted to the old motion may no longer be enough. We need a model of the interactions that matter.

Even this small scene contains choices. Are we studying the tray alone, the load alone, or both together? Does the cord pull horizontally? Is sliding resistance important? Is the object still touching the bench? Those questions determine the forces we include, and the answers cannot be recovered merely by rearranging a formula. This unit develops a way of making the choices explicit.

We will begin by turning a scene into a free-body diagram: a deliberately spare picture of a selected object or system and the external forces acting on it. Each arrow must have a physical explanation. A diagram that includes an invented “force of motion”, or a force acting on the wrong object, can lead to polished arithmetic and an incorrect conclusion.

Once the force inventory is reliable, Newton’s laws connect the resultant force with changes in velocity. We will distinguish remaining at rest from moving with constant velocity, and distinguish forces that balance on one object from the two forces belonging to an interaction between two objects. Weight, support, tension and friction then become quantitative models with conditions, rather than labels attached by habit.

From a force model to a path

Acceleration describes how velocity changes. We first develop its physical meaning and then concentrate on the case in which it is constant. In that restricted but useful setting, algebra connects elapsed time, velocity and displacement. We will explain where the equations come from, what their symbols refer to and how to check them at the starting time or when acceleration is zero.

Projectile motion brings the strands together. Under a model of uniform gravity with air resistance neglected, horizontal and vertical changes can be calculated separately and then combined into one path. A short Python investigation will evaluate and plot those algebraic predictions. It will include an independent hand calculation and a clear account of the assumptions; a smooth computed curve cannot establish that a real object follows it.

By the end of Unit 4

You should be able to select a system and justify a force diagram; apply Newton’s laws with consistent directions and units; model common contact and gravitational forces within stated limits; and construct, check and interpret a constant-acceleration or projectile prediction.

The route through the unit

  1. Systems, interactions and force diagrams. Decide which forces belong in the model.
  2. Newton’s laws and equilibrium. Connect the resultant with changing velocity.
  3. Weight, support, tension and friction. Determine forces using explicit physical assumptions.
  4. Constant-acceleration motion. Build and select algebraic predictions.
  5. Projectile motion and unit synthesis. Combine components, investigate a trajectory in Python and consolidate the whole unit.

Allow 18 hours, including 2 hours of Python. The lessons have planned allocations of 3, 3, 4, 3 and 5 hours. The first includes this introduction and the Block 2 opening. The final lesson reserves time for a substantial mixed exercise set, feedback, a short conclusion and matching web and printable reference resources. These are parts of the unit allocation, not extra work to add afterwards. Timings are estimates; record questions that need another pass rather than racing a clock.

You need P101 Units 1–3 and the vector arithmetic and projections of M101 Units 1–2. In particular, be ready to add signed components and distinguish a vector from its magnitude. The first lesson recalls these methods briefly in the new force context. No derivatives, integrals or numerical motion solver are assumed. Paper, a pencil and an ordinary scientific calculator are enough for Lessons 1–4; the established Python tools return in Lesson 5.

Our opening goal is modest but consequential: make a force diagram whose every arrow can be defended. The laws and calculations that follow will have a much firmer foundation once that habit is in place.
