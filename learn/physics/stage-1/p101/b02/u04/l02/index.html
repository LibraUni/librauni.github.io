P101 · Block 2 · Unit 4 · Lesson 2

Newton’s laws
and equilibrium

When the cord becomes slack, the pull on the tray disappears. Its velocity does not disappear with it. Lesson 1 left us with that distinction and a force diagram; we now need to connect the diagram to what changes in the motion.

Newton’s laws make that connection. They do not replace the work of choosing a system and identifying its interactions. They tell us what a correctly constructed resultant implies, and allow us to infer a missing force when the motion is known. We will also examine a different kind of relationship: the two forces that arise when two objects interact.

Allow three hours: 60 minutes for reading and worked examples, 90 minutes for the six activities, and 30 minutes for feedback and correction. Use the signed velocities of P101 Unit 3 and the vector methods of M101 Units 1–2. No calculus or programming is needed. Numerical situations are invented teaching models unless a caption says otherwise.

4.7 Acceleration describes a change in velocity

Imagine a small cart moving along a straight track. At one instant its velocity is 2 metres per second to the right; two seconds later it is 6 metres per second to the right. The cart has not merely changed position. Its velocity has changed as well. Choose rightwards as positive, so the two velocities are +2ms−1 and +6ms−1. Subtract the earlier value from the later one:

Δvx=6ms−1−2ms−1=4ms−1.

The symbol Δ means a change, and the subscript x identifies the signed component along the track. Dividing by the elapsed time gives the average acceleration over that interval:

a¯x=ΔvxΔt=4ms−12s=2ms−2.

The bar distinguishes an interval average. One metre per second squared means a velocity change of one metre per second for each second, when the acceleration is constant. It does not mean that the cart moves a distance of one metre in every square second. The units come from dividing velocity by time.

An interval average does not reveal every change within the interval. The cart might accelerate unevenly and still have the same two endpoint velocities. Acceleration at an instant describes the local rate at which velocity is changing. We can investigate it by considering velocity changes over progressively shorter intervals around that time. Later mathematics makes this precise using a derivative; we do not need that machinery for the constant-acceleration cases here. If acceleration really is constant throughout an interval, its instantaneous and average values agree, and we write ax without a bar.

Example 4.6 · Negative acceleration, two different stories

Question. In one trial, a cart’s velocity changes uniformly from +5 to +1ms−1 in 2 s. In another, it changes uniformly from −1 to −5ms−1 in 2 s. Rightwards is positive. Compare the accelerations and the changes in speed.

Working. Keep the signs when subtracting:

ax,1=(1−5)ms−12s=−2ms−2,ax,2=[−5−(−1)]ms−12s=−2ms−2.

The first cart moves rightwards and slows from 5 to 1 m/s. The second moves leftwards and speeds up from 1 to 5 m/s. Both accelerations point left.

Interpretation. A negative component identifies a direction relative to an axis. It does not, by itself, mean slowing down. Along a straight line, acceleration opposite to a nonzero velocity reduces speed locally; acceleration in the same direction increases it. If a cart reverses, examine the intervals on each side of the reversal separately.

Velocity is a vector, so its direction matters as much as its magnitude. An object can change direction while keeping the same speed; its velocity then changes and it accelerates. We will calculate that kind of motion in the next unit. For now, remember that acceleration means change in velocity, not simply “going faster”. In more than one dimension the average-acceleration definition is the vector relation a¯=Δv/Δt.

Activity 4.7 · Read the change, not just the final sign

Allow 10 minutes. With east positive, a marker’s velocity changes uniformly from −6 to −2ms−1 in 2 s. Find its acceleration and describe its speed change. A second record has equal initial and final velocities separated by 10 s. Does that establish zero acceleration throughout those 10 s? Explain.

Hint for Activity 4.7

Subtract the initial velocity, including its minus sign. For the second record, imagine a cart that first speeds up and then slows down again.

Discussion of Activity 4.7

The first change is [−2−(−6)]ms−1=+4ms−1, so ax=+2ms−2 eastwards. The marker is moving westwards but slowing: its speed falls from 6 to 2 m/s. Positive acceleration need not mean increasing speed.

Equal endpoint velocities give zero average acceleration. They do not exclude nonzero accelerations between the endpoints. A statement about constant velocity throughout an interval is stronger than two matching readings.

4.8 What happens when the resultant is zero?

On a rough bench, a released tray slows down. It is tempting to conclude that motion requires a continuing pull. But the force inventory includes bench friction while the tray slides. The slowing occurs while an interaction remains. If the resistance is reduced, a tray can travel farther before stopping; the useful idealisation is to neglect the resistance altogether, rather than turn the observed slowing into a universal rule.

Newton’s first law states that an object with zero resultant external force maintains a constant velocity in an inertial reference frame. Constant velocity includes rest, but also motion at any unchanged speed in an unchanged direction. If the tray initially moves rightwards at 1 m/s and its resultant subsequently remains zero, it continues with that velocity in the model. The initial velocity is information about its history, not something supplied by the zero-force condition.

A reference frame is a way of assigning positions and times relative to chosen reference objects and axes. The frame qualification is essential. A nearly free puck can move steadily relative to a laboratory while appearing to accelerate backwards relative to a cart that is speeding up beside it. There need be no new backward interaction on the puck. The observer’s frame is accelerating. We call a frame inertial when the first-law description holds for freely moving test objects; frames moving at constant velocity relative to it, with fixed relative axis directions, are also inertial.

For the short, local laboratory motions in this unit, we treat axes fixed to the ground as approximately inertial. This neglects effects of Earth’s rotation and orbital motion at the accuracy required here. An accelerating lift or vehicle is not automatically an inertial frame. We will describe objects in those vehicles using the ground frame, keeping the ordinary external-force inventory. We are not adding a course on rotating frames or fictitious forces.

Key idea 4.3 · A condition on change

In an inertial frame, zero resultant external force means unchanged velocity. It does not determine the value of that velocity. A zero velocity at one instant, by contrast, does not establish zero resultant.

Example 4.7 · Two trials, the same force diagram

Question. A capsule is supported on an ideal smooth horizontal platform. In trial A it is initially at rest; in trial B it is initially moving left at 0.8 m/s. In both trials, weight and normal support balance, and there are no horizontal forces. What follows?

Reasoning. The resultant is zero in both trials. The ground is our approximately inertial frame, so each capsule keeps its own initial velocity. A stays at rest; B continues left at 0.8 m/s while the model remains applicable.

Check. The identical diagrams imply identical acceleration, not identical velocity. A platform edge or a later collision would end the stated interval and require a new model.

A wider view · A photograph is not a motion record

The image offers an unusual setting for a familiar evidence problem. We need successive observations and a specified frame to describe motion. Nor does “floating” establish an absence of gravity: the devices and station are in orbital free fall. A full account of their relative motion requires more than our straight-track model. No force value in this lesson is inferred from this photograph.

Activity 4.8 · What is actually established?

Allow 10 minutes. For each statement, say what it establishes about the resultant force in an inertial frame: (a) a cart remains at rest for a whole interval; (b) a cart travels north at an unchanged velocity for a whole interval; (c) a ball’s velocity is zero at one instant; (d) a cart appears to accelerate relative to an accelerating camera. State any missing information.

Hint for Activity 4.8

Distinguish an interval from an instant and an inertial frame from an accelerating viewpoint.

Discussion of Activity 4.8

In (a) and (b), velocity is constant, so the resultant is zero throughout the interval in the Newtonian model. In (c), one velocity value does not specify its rate of change; the resultant may be nonzero. A vertically thrown ball momentarily stops rising while gravity continues acting. In (d), the stated relative acceleration alone is insufficient: the camera’s acceleration must be accounted for before using the inertial-frame law. A good explanation names the frame rather than inventing a force to match the picture.

4.9 From a force sum to an acceleration

The first law identifies a particularly important case. Newton’s second law supplies a quantitative relationship when the resultant is nonzero as well. For an object of constant mass, treated as a particle in an inertial frame,

Fnet=∑F=ma.

The summation sign ∑ tells us to add all the external forces on this one system. It is the sum already constructed in Lesson 1. The mass m is a positive scalar measured in kilograms; the acceleration a is a vector. Multiplying by mass changes the size of the acceleration vector, not its direction. Consequently acceleration points along the resultant, although velocity need not.

Mass measures an object’s inertia: its resistance to a change in velocity under a given resultant. At the same resultant, doubling the mass halves the acceleration. At the same mass, doubling the resultant doubles the acceleration. This is an experimentally supported relationship within Newtonian mechanics, not a rule inferred from the appearance of an arrow. We restrict attention to constant mass and ordinary nonrelativistic motions; an object exchanging substantial mass with its surroundings needs a more careful treatment.

The SI force unit is chosen so that a resultant of one newton gives a one-kilogram mass an acceleration of one metre per second squared:

1N=1kgms−2.

This identity also checks a calculation: newtons divided by kilograms must produce acceleration units. Mass is not a force, and its kilogram value must not be inserted as a weight in a force sum. Lesson 3 will relate mass to gravitational force.

For fixed perpendicular axes, the vector law becomes a separate equation for each component:

Fnet,x=max,Fnet,y=may.

These equations describe the same object at the same time. A zero vertical acceleration can coexist with a nonzero horizontal one. Begin with the diagram, write the signed force sum, and only then substitute values. The expression max is the value of the sum; it is not an extra “acceleration force” to put on the diagram.

Example 4.8 · A tray pulled more strongly than it is resisted

Question. A 2.5 kg tray slides right on a horizontal bench. A cord pulls right with 8 N; the bench’s sliding friction is 3 N left. The vertical forces balance. Find the acceleration. What changes immediately after the cord becomes slack, while the tray is still sliding right and the same friction model applies?

Method. Choose the tray and rightwards positive. While the cord pulls,

8N−3N=(2.5kg)ax,ax=5N2.5kg=2ms−2.

After the pull ends, the force sum is −3N, giving ax=−1.2ms−2. Its velocity is still rightwards just after release, but it now decreases.

Interpretation. The change of acceleration follows the change of force inventory. Neither calculation specifies the velocity without initial motion information. Once the tray stops, we cannot automatically continue a leftward sliding-friction force; its direction and regime must be reconsidered. We have not predicted perpetual acceleration to the left.

Activity 4.9 · Match the system, sum and mass

Allow 15 minutes. A 4 kg instrument sled has two horizontal forces, 10 N east and 6 N west, with balanced vertical forces. Find its acceleration. Find the eastward force needed for an acceleration of 2 m/s² east if the westward resistance remains 6 N. Finally, explain what happens to the acceleration if the original resultant is applied to an 8 kg sled. In each case distinguish the applied force from the resultant.

Hint for Activity 4.9

For the required pull P, write P−6N=max before rearranging.

Discussion of Activity 4.9

The original resultant is 4 N east, so the 4 kg sled accelerates at 1 m/s² east. To obtain 2 m/s², the resultant must be 4×2=8N east. The applied force is therefore 8+6=14N east. Using 8 N for the pull would neglect the resistance.

The original 4 N resultant on 8 kg gives 0.5 m/s² east. That comparison holds the resultant fixed. Merely saying “the same pull” would not suffice if other forces changed with the added mass.

4.10 Equilibrium is a vector condition

A sample suspended at rest provides a way to infer forces without watching a changing velocity. In our particle model, its acceleration is zero, so its external force vectors sum to zero. We call this translational equilibrium. The same condition applies to an object moving with constant velocity. For an extended object, zero resultant alone does not establish that it will not rotate; moments and rotational equilibrium lie beyond this unit.

Resolving an equilibrium problem gives ∑Fx=0 and ∑Fy=0 separately. A large upward force cannot cancel a rightward force merely because their magnitudes match. The forces must close as a vector sum. There may be more than two, and none need equal the weight individually.

Example 4.9 · Finding a supporting pull

Question. A small stationary calibration bead has weight 12 N downwards. One cord pulls horizontally left with 5 N. A second cord pulls upwards and rightwards. Neglect other forces and find that second pull.

Working. Select the bead. Let the second pull have components Tx right and Ty up. Since acceleration is zero,

Tx−5N=0,Ty−12N=0.

Thus its components are (5,12)N. Its magnitude and direction follow from the right triangle:

T=(5N)2+(12N)2=13N,θ=tan−1(125)≈67.4∘.

Interpretation. The cord must lie about 67.4° above the rightward horizontal in this configuration. Its 13 N magnitude exceeds the 12 N weight because it must also balance the other cord’s horizontal pull. We have inferred what is required for the stated rest condition; we have not established that an arbitrary cord angle or strength can supply it.

This example makes an important distinction between an equation and a physical possibility. Solving the component equations gives a required force. We must still check whether the proposed interaction can provide that direction and magnitude. A flexible cord pulls along itself; it cannot push. A bench can support a resting object but cannot pull it down after contact is lost. Those feasibility checks become central in Lesson 3.

Activity 4.10 · An equilibrium claim to test

Allow 15 minutes. A bead has weight 8 N down. A horizontal cord pulls 6 N left, and another cord points upwards and right at 45° to the horizontal. A report claims that this bead is at rest with only those three forces. Determine the components and direction the second cord would actually need. Explain whether adjusting only its tension could make the claimed 45° configuration work.

Hint for Activity 4.10

At 45°, the two components of a tension have equal magnitudes. Compare that requirement with the two balances.

Discussion of Activity 4.10

Balance requires (Tx,Ty)=(6,8)N, so T=10N and the angle is tan−1(8/6)≈53.1∘ above the rightward horizontal. A 45° cord has equal horizontal and vertical components at every tension. It cannot simultaneously provide 6 N horizontally and 8 N vertically. The report is inconsistent with its force inventory; either the geometry, another force, or the rest claim must change. A complete answer explains that inconsistency rather than accepting the calculated magnitude as proof of equilibrium.

4.11 An interaction has two recipients

Press your hand against a wall. The wall pushes on the hand while the hand pushes on the wall. These are two descriptions of the same contact with the recipients exchanged. Newton’s third law states that, for the Newtonian interactions considered here, the forces two bodies exert on each other are simultaneous, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction:

FAonB=−FBonA.

The order of the labels is part of the statement. The left-hand force acts on B; the right-hand force acts on A. Neither one waits for the other to finish. The usual phrase “action and reaction” must not suggest a time delay or a weaker response. It also does not say that either body has zero resultant, because each may interact with other objects.

Return to the book resting on the tray. The upward force of the tray on the book and the downward force of Earth on the book may balance. They are not a third-law pair: both act on the book and have different agents. The partner of “tray on book” is “book on tray”. The partner of “Earth on book” is “book on Earth”. The last force exists even though Earth’s acceleration due to that book is far too small to notice in this model.

Example 4.10 · Equal contact forces, unequal accelerations

Question. Two carts A and B, masses 2 kg and 5 kg, touch on a smooth horizontal track. During a short interaction, A pushes B right with 10 N. There are no other horizontal forces. Find both horizontal accelerations.

Working. The third law gives 10 N left for B on A. It gives the forces, not the accelerations. With rightwards positive, apply the second law separately:

aA=−10N2kg=−5ms−2,aB=10N5kg=2ms−2.

Interpretation. A has the larger acceleration magnitude because it has less mass. The greater mass of B does not make its force on A greater. These results describe the stipulated instant while contact lasts; they do not determine later velocities without an interaction duration and initial conditions.

Activity 4.11 · Pair by names before comparing numbers

Allow 15 minutes. A package rests on a shelf. List the third-law partner of each force on the package and say which body receives it. Explain why the two forces on the package balance. Then consider a hand pushing cart A right with 9 N while A pushes cart B right with 4 N. What force does B exert on A, and what is A’s horizontal resultant if there are no other horizontal interactions?

Hint for Activity 4.11

Exchange agent and recipient to find a partner. Use only forces received by A in A’s sum.

Discussion of Activity 4.11

The shelf-on-package support is paired with package-on-shelf contact, acting on the shelf. Earth-on-package weight is paired with package-on-Earth gravity, acting on Earth. Support and weight balance on the package because its acceleration is zero, by the second law; their equality is not a third-law assertion.

B pushes A left with 4 N. A receives 9 N right from the hand and 4 N left from B, so its resultant is 5 N right. The hand’s 9 N force is paired with A’s 9 N force on the hand, not with the 4 N contact between the carts. Each interaction needs its own names.

4.12 Choose a useful system, then check it

If we include both objects in an interaction inside one system, the two third-law forces are internal. In the sum of the equations for the parts, those opposite vectors cancel. That cancellation does not mean that the parts cannot move relative to each other or deform. Internal forces can matter greatly for those questions. It means that they do not contribute to the total external-force sum.

For the following example the two carts remain together and have the same acceleration. Adding their individual second-law equations then gives Fexternal,x=(mA+mB)ax. This is a useful result for a group translating together. Do not assume a common acceleration for arbitrary separate objects merely because you have drawn one boundary around them. A more general treatment uses the centre of mass, which is not needed here.

Example 4.11 · Two carts move together

Question. Cart A of mass 2 kg is behind cart B of mass 3 kg on a smooth horizontal track. A hand pushes A right with 15 N. The carts remain in contact and accelerate together without turning. Find their acceleration and the contact force between them. Vertical forces balance; neglect air effects.

Method. For A and B together, the only external horizontal force is the hand’s 15 N. The contact between carts is internal. Therefore

ax=15N(2+3)kg=3ms−2.

Now select B alone. Its sole horizontal force is A’s push, C, so C=(3kg)(3ms−2)=9N right. B pushes A left with 9 N. Check A separately: 15−9=6N right, equal to (2kg)(3ms−2).

Interpretation. The combined system made the acceleration easy to find; the individual system exposed the contact force. The 15 N hand force is not transmitted as a 15 N force on B: part of its effect is to accelerate A. Both individual equations reproduce the same acceleration.

A reliable calculation therefore has a chain of reasons: this system, these interactions, this frame, these signed sums, and this acceleration. A numerical answer with the right units can still fail at the first link. Conversely, an awkward or impossible result can reveal that an assumed contact or equilibrium condition is inconsistent. In the next lesson we will make such checks using models for weight, normal support, tension and friction.

Activity 4.12 · Audit a pair of carts

Allow 25 minutes. Repeat the geometry of Example 4.11 with A of mass 3 kg, B of mass 2 kg and a hand force of 10 N right. The carts initially move left but remain in contact; the horizontal track is smooth. A draft claims: “The two contact forces cancel on A, so A receives the full 10 N and B has no force. Since the hand pushes right, both carts must already be moving right.” Draw the combined and individual diagrams, calculate the acceleration and contact forces, and repair every part of that argument. Explain how A’s acceleration can be checked without relying on the combined diagram.

Hint for Activity 4.12

Find the common acceleration using total mass. Then use B alone to find the contact. A’s velocity and acceleration can point in opposite directions.

Discussion of Activity 4.12

The combined horizontal resultant is 10 N right on 5 kg, so ax=2ms−2. B requires C=(2kg)(2ms−2)=4N right from A. Its partner is 4 N left from B on A. On A alone the sum is 10−4=6N, and 6N/(3kg)=2ms−2, agreeing with the common acceleration.

Each individual diagram includes weight and normal support in balance, although they do not enter the horizontal calculation. The combined diagram excludes both internal contact arrows. The draft cancels forces with different recipients on A’s diagram and then leaves B without the force needed to accelerate it. It also confuses acceleration with velocity. While both carts still move left, their rightward acceleration reduces their speed. A later reversal requires time and initial-velocity information, which have not been supplied.

A complete repair names recipients, preserves the third-law equality, checks both individual equations and states the initial leftward motion separately. Redraw an incorrect diagram before editing its algebra.

Looking ahead

The force inventory now has a quantitative role: its sum determines acceleration for the stated Newtonian model. Zero resultant preserves a velocity; unequal forces can change that velocity; and a third-law pair belongs to two recipients. System choice lets us expose or remove an internal contact without changing the physics.

So far, forces such as 3 N of sliding resistance have been supplied. Lesson 3 asks how to determine such forces from a physical model, and how to recognise when a proposed model cannot support the claimed motion.
