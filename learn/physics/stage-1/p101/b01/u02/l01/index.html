P101 · Unit 2 · Lesson 1

What exactly are
we measuring?

A ruler can give a clear reading to a question that has not been clearly asked. Our first task is to make the question precise enough that another observer could carry out the same measurement.

In this lesson you will move from a physical description to an instrument reading, then from a reading to a preserved notebook record. We will use modest quantities—lengths on a desk and elapsed times—because they let us examine the decisions without needing new physical laws. The reasoning will matter just as much when the instrument is a telescope or a laboratory sensor.

Allow 4 hours, including 1 hour of Python: about 60 minutes for the unit introduction and this teaching, 90 for written and practical activities, 60 for the notebook, and 30 for feedback and correction. Activity times are guides for first attempts, not deadlines. Keep a notebook and pencil nearby; use a ruler if available. Download the learner notebook or its plain Python companion.

2.1 A quantity needs a definition

Suppose a small book is to fit into a rigid sleeve. “The length of the book” sounds adequate until you notice that its cover projects beyond its pages. A measurement of the page block could be carefully made and still fail to answer the practical question. The sleeve must accommodate the outside of the closed cover.

The quantity intended to be measured is called the measurand. The book is the object; the distance between specified points on its closed cover is a measurand. Naming the object alone is not enough. A book also has a width, a thickness and many possible distances between points. Even its thickness can change when the covers are pressed together.

An operational definition tells us how to identify the quantity in practice. For the sleeve question, we might specify the straight-line distance between the outer cover edges along the middle of the closed book's long side, with the book resting flat and no extra pressure applied. “Along the middle” matters if the edges are not parallel. “Closed” and “no extra pressure” help another observer reproduce the state of the object.

Not every measurement needs a paragraph of conditions. The useful details are those that could change the answer at the level we care about. The colour of the desk is unlikely to matter here; whether the book is open clearly does. The aim is to expose consequential choices, not to make the record ceremonially elaborate.

Example 2.1 · The same object, different questions

A note reads “Measure this book so I can cut a protective cover.” Would a single outer-length reading be enough?

Begin with what the proposed measurement is meant to decide.

A protective wrap must accommodate more than one direction: both covers and the spine contribute to the sheet it needs. The outer length of one closed cover is a well-defined quantity, but it does not determine the complete wrap. We would first specify the required dimensions and allowances, then measure them separately. A good instrument cannot compensate for choosing too few quantities.

This does not make the sleeve definition wrong. It shows that a definition serves a question. We should retain the question beside the measurement instead of treating “book size” as a universal quantity.

Definition 2.1 · Specify before reading

A useful measurement description identifies the quantity, the object or event, the relevant endpoints or conditions, and the procedure by which a reading will be obtained. A unit gives a value its scale; the definition gives it its subject.

Activity 2.1 · Repair the question

Allow 12 minutes. A request says “Compare the size of two mugs.” Write two different measurable interpretations. For one, specify endpoints or conditions and explain a situation in which it would be the useful choice. Then explain why two careful measurements in centimetres might still be incomparable. A sketch may help; do not make measurements yet.

Hint for Activity 2.1

Fitting a mug under a shelf and passing it through a narrow opening ask different questions. Decide whether the handle belongs in your definition.

Discussion of Activity 2.1

One interpretation is vertical height from a flat supporting surface to the highest point of the rim, with the mug upright. Another is maximum outside width including the handle, with the direction chosen to capture that span. Height is useful for shelf clearance; outside width can matter for a narrow opening. Other clearly specified quantities are valid. Comparing one mug's rim diameter with another's handle-inclusive width does not compare the same quantity, even though both values use centimetres. State which orientation and points you mean, rather than relying on “size”.

Having agreed on the quantity, we next need a way to connect its endpoints to a scale.

2.2 From scale readings to a length

A ruler supplies marked positions. A length between two points is the difference between their positions on that scale. Starting at zero makes the subtraction easy to overlook: a right-hand reading of 12 cm, with the left endpoint at 0 cm, gives a length of 12 cm. But the physical edge of a ruler need not be its zero mark, and an object need not begin at zero.

Let rA denote the scale reading at the left endpoint A and rB the reading at the right endpoint B. With the ruler increasing from A towards B, the length is

L=rB−rA.

This is the coordinate-difference idea from Unit 1, now attached to observed endpoints. Both readings must come from the same scale placement and use consistent units. If the object or ruler moves between the two readings, the subtraction no longer compares the intended positions.

Example 2.2 · A worn zero end

A ruler's zero end is damaged, so an observer uses two intact marks. The endpoint readings are 2.0 cm and 14.6 cm. Find the length and explain whether using a nonzero start is itself a fault.

Use the interval between readings, keeping the common unit.

L=14.6cm−2.0cm=12.6cm

The nonzero start is acceptable if the remaining scale is sound, both endpoints are read properly and the ruler spans the object. Calling 14.6 cm the length would include the unused 2.0 cm interval. Subtraction avoids that mistake; it does not prove that the spacing of the marks is correct or that the ruler is aligned.

2.2.1 The geometry of a reading

Place the ruler parallel to the line whose length you intend to measure. A diagonal across a rectangle measures a different, longer distance than its side. If a ruler is angled slightly away from the intended line, there may no longer be an unambiguous alignment between its marks and the chosen endpoints. Resolve that geometry before writing a number.

Bring the scale close to the points being read. When a mark and an endpoint lie at different depths, moving your eye sideways changes their apparent alignment. This effect is parallax. Looking approximately straight down at the reading position and reducing the separation between the scale and endpoint help. On a long object, move your head to look above each endpoint; looking from one fixed oblique position can favour one end over the other.

Accuracy concerns how close a measurement is to the value of the quantity we intended to measure. Agreement with a suitable reference can help us assess it; agreement between our own readings alone is not enough.

Check the instrument's range too. A 15 cm ruler cannot span a 20 cm object in one placement. Measuring in stages is possible, but joins and repositioning introduce additional decisions. For this lesson, choose an object the available ruler can span; a simple method will make its limitations easier to inspect.

Activity 2.2 · Recover the interval

Allow 12 minutes. A card gives endpoint readings 3.4 cm and 15.9 cm. A second placement gives 1.0 cm and 13.5 cm. Calculate each length and express it in millimetres. Explain why the endpoint readings changed while the lengths agree. Finally, explain why this agreement alone cannot establish that the ruler is accurate.

Hint for Activity 2.2

Compare differences, not the two right-hand readings. Ask what fault could affect both placements in the same way.

Discussion of Activity 2.2

Both subtractions give 12.5 cm, or 125 mm. The second pair is shifted down by 2.4 cm; a common shift cancels in the difference. This is consistent with moving the ruler relative to an unchanged card. It does not establish the scale's accuracy: incorrect spacing between the marks could affect both results. The stipulated values also tell us nothing about the actual alignment or endpoint choices unless the procedure records them.

2.3 What the last digit can tell us

Imagine a ruler whose adjacent marks are 1 mm apart. That spacing tells you something about how finely the scale is divided. It does not say that every measurement made with the ruler is within 1 mm of the intended length. A thick endpoint, a tilted ruler or a mistaken choice of endpoints could matter more than the spacing.

Resolution concerns the smallest change that can be distinguished in an indication. For a simple digital timer, the displayed increment is a useful starting point: a display that advances through 2.31 s, 2.32 s and 2.33 s has steps of 0.01 s. It does not follow that a person starting and stopping it has timed the intended physical events to within 0.01 s. The display and the procedure contribute different information.

On an analogue scale, you may be able to estimate between graduations. If an edge lies roughly halfway between 126 mm and 127 mm, writing “about 126.5 mm” can describe your estimate. It does not turn the ruler into an instrument with 0.1 mm graduations. Whether such interpolation is useful depends on the widths of the marks, the clarity of the endpoint and the viewing conditions.

For the ordinary-ruler activities here, record to the nearest millimetre and state that convention. If the endpoint is genuinely unclear, note that difficulty beside the reading. The nearest-millimetre convention makes the exercise consistent; it is not a universal rule forbidding more careful interpolation in other circumstances.

Example 2.3 · A finer display

A handheld timer displays 8.37 s. Its display changes in steps of 0.01 s. A report concludes: “The event lasted 8.37 s, accurate to 0.01 s.” What has been established?

The display establishes an indicated interval and its displayed increment. To judge the measurement, we still need to know which events were used to start and stop the timer, how the observer recognised them, and whether the timer itself was behaving correctly. A delayed button press can shift an endpoint even when the electronics resolve small time steps. Retain the displayed reading, but replace the accuracy claim with a record of the increment and the manual timing method. A numerical accuracy bound requires more evidence.

Distinction 2.2 · Detail is not a guarantee

Record the scale graduation or display increment separately from the measurement's limitations. Neither the last digit nor half a scale division is automatically a bound on every source of measurement uncertainty.

Later lessons will develop precision and justified reporting in more detail. For now, the useful habit is to preserve what the instrument indicated while being honest about what that indication can establish. Adding zeros to a calculation changes its appearance; it cannot improve the reading that supplied it.

Activity 2.3 · Diagnose three claims

Allow 10 minutes. Explain the problem with each statement and write a better one: (a) “My ruler has 1 mm marks, so alignment cannot cause a larger error”; (b) “Python printed 0.126, so the length is known exactly”; (c) “Five identical timer readings prove that the start event was correctly identified.”

Hint for Activity 2.3

Separate the instrument indication, the calculation and the physical procedure.

Discussion of Activity 2.3

(a) Mark spacing does not control alignment; state the 1 mm graduation and describe how the ruler was aligned. (b) The number is a representation of an input or calculation; record its unit and source, and do not infer exact physical knowledge. (c) The same event could have been consistently misidentified, or differences could be too small to change the display. Report that the recorded indications agree, then check the event definition separately. None of these repairs requires inventing a numerical uncertainty.

2.4 Make a repeat a fresh measurement

Suppose you place a ruler beside a card, read 126 mm, and write the number five times. You now have five entries but one observation. Even looking at the unchanged arrangement five times mainly probes your reading of that arrangement. It does not reveal how much repositioning the ruler might matter.

A repeated measurement needs a stated repeat procedure. For our card example, lift and replace the ruler between trials, realign it with the same chosen edge, and make a fresh reading. Keep the card, edge, instrument and reading convention the same. Resetting the placement varies one part of the process while holding the intended quantity fixed. Changing to another edge halfway through would change the measurand instead.

There is no requirement that fresh readings differ. If five independently obtained indications are identical, preserve them. Do not manufacture variation. Conversely, a different reading is not automatically a mistake to remove. Record what happened first; Lesson 2 will begin to distinguish variation from effects that persist through every repeat.

2.4.1 Timing requires two events

The same logic applies to time. “Time a cart crossing a strip” leaves open whether the start is the front of the cart reaching the near line, the rear leaving it, or the moment a person releases the cart. These are different events. A workable definition might use the front edge first crossing line A as the start and that same front edge first crossing line B as the finish, with motion from A towards B.

Example 2.4 · Two late presses

In Figure 2.5 the physical interval is 4.00 s − 1.00 s = 3.00 s. The timer starts at 1.18 s and stops at 4.27 s on the same reference clock. What interval does it indicate?

T=4.27s−1.18s=3.09s

The finish press is 0.27 s late and the start press 0.18 s late, so the indicated interval exceeds the physical interval by 0.09 s. If the delays were exactly equal, they would cancel in this difference. Being late at both ends does not guarantee equal delays, and a single manual timing does not tell us what either delay was. We can calculate the discrepancy here only because the example supplies the physical times independently.

Activity 2.4 · Specify the repeat

Allow 16 minutes. Write a procedure for repeating either the card-length measurement or the cart timing. Identify what stays fixed, what is reset, and precisely which points or events are read. Explain what would be lost if you copied the first result into later rows. Then use the times in Example 2.4 but change the start press to 1.27 s: what interval is indicated, and does that make every later timing trustworthy?

Hint for Activity 2.4

Another person should be able to follow your procedure without choosing new endpoints. In the numerical part, compare the two delays.

Discussion of Activity 2.4

For length, keep the same flat card and marked edge, use the same ruler and unit, replace and realign the ruler, view the endpoints from above, then record the fresh reading before the next trial. For timing, retain the same front-edge crossings and direction, reset the timer, and make a fresh run under the stated starting conditions; this can vary the cart's motion as well as the timing process. Copied rows provide no new observations. With a start press at 1.27 s, the indicated interval is 4.27 − 1.27 = 3.00 s. The equal 0.27 s delays cancel for this case. That equality is supplied, not guaranteed for other runs.

A method makes repeated readings comparable. A record must now keep that method attached to the values.

2.5 Keep the evidence before calculating

A list such as 126, 127, 126, 125, 126 could be lengths, times or counts. Even with “mm” added, it does not identify an object, an edge or a method. The information accompanying the values is often called metadata: data about the record. Here that means such things as the object and endpoint description, instrument, reading convention, date, observer label and any events noticed during the trials.

The word raw means that we retain readings as recorded, before later conversion or summarising. It does not mean that the numbers are free of judgement. Reading an analogue scale already involves decisions. Preserving raw readings lets us revisit those decisions rather than reconstruct the observations from a final average.

Example 2.5 · A record with a declared origin

The following is a simulated teaching record, constructed for this lesson. No card was actually measured to produce these numbers. Its stipulated method is: a flat card; one identified long edge between corners A and B; a ruler with 1 mm graduations, spanning the card; A aligned to the zero mark; fresh ruler placement for each trial; reading from above to the nearest millimetre. The observer records B's scale reading.

Simulated record P101-U02-L01-A · trial order preserved

2127Ruler replaced
3126Ruler replaced
4125Corner B looked less distinct

Because A is stipulated to be aligned to zero on each placement, the B readings also supply the estimated lengths in this exercise. If A were not at zero, we would preserve both endpoint readings for every trial and calculate their differences. The note on trial 4 identifies something to investigate; it does not establish that 125 mm is wrong or justify silently deleting it.

This table supports practice in handling a record. Its simulated origin must remain visible if it is copied into a notebook or report. It provides no empirical evidence about a real card or anyone's measuring skill.

For your own work, add method notes before measuring and trial notes as events happen. If you discover a transcription error, retain a trace of the correction and the reason. Do not overwrite an uncomfortable result merely to make the sequence look tidier. Lesson 4 will develop the treatment of missing and invalid readings; at this stage, a clear note is better than an invented replacement.

Activity 2.5 · What can this file tell us?

Allow 10 minutes. Someone sends a file containing only values = [126, 127, 126, 125, 126]. List four questions you would need answered before interpreting it physically. If they send Example 2.5 with the file, what becomes clearer? What still cannot be concluded?

Hint for Activity 2.5

Think about quantity, unit, origin and method. Distinguish understanding a teaching record from verifying an experiment.

Discussion of Activity 2.5

Ask what quantity was measured, in which unit, on what object or event, with which instrument and procedure, and whether the values are observations, predictions or invented data. Example 2.5 supplies those meanings and a trial note. It also establishes that the record is simulated. We still cannot conclude that a real object has that length, that a physical ruler was accurate, or that an observer performed the method. The unusual fourth reading remains part of the constructed record.

2.5.1 A conversion that leaves the readings intact

Enter the simulated readings as a list, retaining their order. Use a name that states the unit. A comment records the origin for anyone reading the program; the longer method belongs in a Markdown cell of the notebook.

# Simulated teaching record P101-U02-L01-A, not observations.
raw_mm = [126, 127, 126, 125, 126]
print("Raw readings / mm:", raw_mm)
print("Number of readings:", len(raw_mm))

All five values are integers because this exercise records whole millimetres. That choice does not say that the physical lengths are exact integers. The len call counts entries; it cannot tell whether the entries came from five fresh observations or five copies.

To express the values in metres, divide each by 1000. Store the converted values in a new list. The function below contains only the arithmetic already used in Unit 1; its name states the conversion direction.

def millimetres_to_metres(value_mm):
    return value_mm / 1000.0

converted_m = []
for value_mm in raw_mm:
    converted_m.append(millimetres_to_metres(value_mm))

print("Converted values / m:", converted_m)
print("Raw readings / mm:", raw_mm)

The converted list should display [0.126, 0.127, 0.126, 0.125, 0.126], and the raw list should remain [126, 127, 126, 125, 126]. The 0.125 m entry must remain in the fourth position. A conversion that silently sorts the data can preserve every value while losing its connection to the trial notes.

Check the processing against arithmetic you can do independently. A one-metre input is a useful test because 1000 mm must become 1 m. Zero must remain zero. For this dataset, 125 mm should become one eighth of a metre, 0.125 m. These checks probe the calculation; the method notes probe the meaning of the input.

print(millimetres_to_metres(1000))  # Expected: 1.0
print(millimetres_to_metres(0))     # Expected: 0.0
print(millimetres_to_metres(125))   # Expected: 0.125

The comments say what to expect; they do not make the checks pass. Read the output and explain any disagreement. A short printed decimal is also not a promise that a computer stores every decimal fraction exactly. Unit 1 introduced finite numerical representation. Here, ordinary floating-point arithmetic is adequate for the conversion, but the displayed digits should never be treated as extra measurement evidence.

Activity 2.6 · Build an inspectable notebook

Allow 60 minutes, including prediction, coding, running and interpretation. Use the learner notebook or Python companion. Keep the simulated record as a practice case even if you later add your own observations.

  1. 10 minutes: write a short record description: source label, quantity, unit, endpoint convention, instrument graduation and the trial-4 note. Predict the converted values on paper.
  2. 20 minutes: enter the raw list and implement the conversion using a function and a loop. Preserve the raw list and its order; make the unit clear in names and output.
  3. 15 minutes: run the three independent checks above. Try the conversion loop with an empty raw list, then restore the five readings. Explain why an empty output does not establish a zero-length object.
  4. 15 minutes: restart the notebook kernel and run all cells in order. Compare both lists with the original table. Explain what the conversion checks establish, what the source label prevents you from claiming, and what remains unknown about measurement quality.
Hint for Activity 2.6

Initialise the converted list before the loop, not inside it. Keep the raw list as an input. Put the method in a Markdown cell or plain-text notes, not only in a variable name.

Discussion of Activity 2.6

The three code blocks above form a worked conversion. A complete response also preserves the meaning of the input. Expected outputs are the five converted values in the original order and check values 1.0, 0.0 and 0.125. With an empty list the loop has no iterations, giving an empty converted list, not a measurement of zero. Restore the original five values before saving the main run.

The method note should say that the values are simulated, A is aligned to zero, B is read to the nearest millimetre after repositioning, and the ruler graduations are 1 mm. Preserve the fourth trial's endpoint note. A clean run checks that the saved cells reconstruct the result without hidden earlier state. It does not turn the simulated record into an experiment, establish instrument accuracy or show that the chosen endpoints answer every question about the card.

2.6 A measurement someone else could repeat

You now have the pieces of a measurement account: an intended quantity, a procedure that connects it to an instrument, raw readings with context, and a visible processing step. The final activity brings those pieces together while the physical task is still small enough to inspect closely.

A useful test is to set your instructions aside briefly, then read them as if you had not seen the apparatus. Could you find the same endpoints? Would you know whether to align with the ruler's edge or its zero mark? Does “repeat” say what to reset? Where would you record an unusual event? Repair a gap in the instructions rather than relying on your memory to fill it.

Activity 2.7 · Write, try and audit a procedure

Allow 30 minutes for one route. Use about 10 minutes to specify the method, 10 to obtain or inspect the record, and 10 to audit the account. The two routes have different evidence: identify the one you use.

Route A · Your own observations

Choose a flat card, closed small notebook or similarly stable object that your ruler can span on a desk. No cutting or special equipment is needed. Identify one length and its endpoints with a sketch. Record the instrument's graduation, the reading convention, how you avoid an oblique view and what you reset between trials. Take five fresh readings, recording each before making the next. Keep the same edge and conditions. If you use a nonzero start, retain both endpoint readings and their difference for every trial. Note difficulties as they occur; do not round all the readings to match one another. If no suitable instrument is available, use Route B.

Route B · Audit a simulated record

Use Example 2.5, explicitly labelled simulated. Rewrite its method as instructions that another person could follow, add an endpoint sketch, and transcribe the table with trial order and note intact. Then audit this proposed report: “The card is exactly 126 mm long because four repeats confirmed the first reading. The 125 mm entry was obviously a mistake, so it was removed.” Identify each claim the record does not support, and write a replacement paragraph. This route practises method and data reasoning; it does not demonstrate instrument handling.

For either route

Finish with a short account containing the question, endpoint definition, instrument and procedure, unaltered readings with units, and two specific limitations. Do not calculate a mean or choose a final “best” value yet. If you transfer your own values into Python, retain the simulated practice case separately and keep each dataset's source label. The existing notebook structure is sufficient; additional coding is not required here.

Hint for Activity 2.7

A person following your account needs to distinguish a real observation from a teaching value and an endpoint reading from a calculated length. “Human error” alone is too vague: name the action or feature that could matter.

Discussion of Activity 2.7

For Route A, a sound account might specify the distance along one marked edge of a flat card, a millimetre ruler placed beside that edge with A at the zero mark, nearest-millimetre readings taken from above at B, and a fresh placement before each trial. Include your actual values, even if identical. Thick corners, imperfect alignment and uncertainty about placing A at zero are concrete limitations. Do not copy the simulated numbers as your own observations.

For Route B, only trials 1, 3 and 5 have the value 126; the two other entries are 127 and 125. Repeats do not establish an exact value, and the trial-4 note is a reason to investigate its endpoint, not proof that it should be discarded. A suitable replacement is: “This simulated record contains five nearest-millimetre readings of a specified card edge, with fresh ruler placement stipulated for each trial. All five entries are retained. Trial 4 notes a less distinct endpoint. The record does not establish an exact length or the accuracy of a real measurement.”

For both routes, judge the account by whether its choices are explicit and its claims fit its evidence. Several procedures can be reasonable; a plausible final number alone does not make an incomplete procedure adequate.

Use the feedback

Set aside the lesson's 30 feedback minutes to revisit your attempts. When an answer differs, locate the decision that changed it: the quantity, the endpoint, the scale subtraction, the repeat procedure or the interpretation of an output. Rewrite the affected sentence or calculation, then try that part again before opening its discussion.

If your method leaves an endpoint ambiguous, revisit Section 2.1 and amend the sketch. If a right-hand scale reading was mistaken for a length, trace both endpoints through Section 2.2. If the notebook loses values, check where the list is initialised and whether each converted entry still matches its trial. Your corrections should identify the cause, not simply replace an answer.

We began with a bare request to measure a book. We can now specify the question, obtain a reading by a stated method and preserve its meaning through a calculation. One question remains deliberately open: when properly recorded readings differ, how should we interpret that difference? Lesson 2 will investigate variation and effects that shift repeated readings together. Keep the record and the procedural notes; they are the starting point for that distinction.

Further reading and context

The required teaching is self-contained. Optional sources, about 5–10 minutes in total:

  • NIST: checking a tape measure gives the context for Figure 2.4; its optical method is optional background.
  • OpenStax College Physics 2e, Section 1.3 offers a broader introduction to measurement limitations. Its significant-figure calculations and uncertainty methods are beyond the present lesson.
