P101 · Unit 2 · Lesson 4

Precision, provenance
and awkward data

A computer can print twelve decimal places without knowing anything about the ruler that supplied its numbers. It can also produce a beautifully consistent answer after an inconvenient reading has quietly disappeared. Neither a long decimal nor a tidy result is enough. A useful measurement report must explain what its numbers mean and leave a route back to the evidence.

Lesson 3 gave us the arithmetic mean, range and mean absolute deviation about the mean. Here we place those calculations inside a complete record: the quantity intended, the readings attempted, the decisions made and the digits finally displayed. The central question is not “How can I make this list look better?” but “What account could another reader check?”

Allow 5 hours, including 2 hours of Python: 60 minutes for explanation and worked examples, 90 for written activities, 120 for the notebook and 30 for feedback and corrections. All numerical records below are simulated. Download the learner notebook or plain Python companion. No external Python package or apparatus is needed. Lists, loops, functions and the summaries from Lesson 3 are prerequisites; new record structures and formatting are explained here.

2.19 Digits are part of a claim

Suppose a ruler has 1 mm divisions and the procedure specifies recording to the nearest millimetre. An observer writes 126 mm. Typing 126.000 mm into a report does not recover three extra observed digits. It changes the appearance of the claim while adding no evidence. Conversely, changing units to 0.126 m does not lose information: the position of the decimal point has changed because the unit has changed.

Keep three ideas separate. The recording increment describes the step used in recording a reading. Instrument resolution concerns the smallest change that can be distinguished in its indication; the division spacing alone is not a complete account of performance. Working digits are digits retained during calculation to avoid unnecessary numerical loss. Reported digits are those chosen for the final description. Arithmetic may produce more working digits than the original reading contains, without increasing the instrument’s resolution or proving a more accurate physical result.

A decimal-place count says how many digits appear to the right of the decimal point. A significant-digit count starts at the first nonzero digit and continues through the final digit intentionally reported. Leading zeros locate the decimal point; zeros between significant digits count, and trailing zeros after the decimal point can express a reporting choice. These conventions help communicate precision, but they cannot certify that the underlying measurement deserves it.

Example 2.17 · The same length in different units

The expression 0.01260 m has five decimal places and four significant digits: 1, 2, 6 and the final 0. Written as 12.60 mm, it has two decimal places and the same four significant digits. The exact unit conversion has not supplied a new observation. In contrast, 0.0126 m expresses three significant digits.

An integer such as 130 mm can be ambiguous about whether the final zero is intended as significant. Scientific notation makes the intention explicit:

1.3×102mm=130mm

The coefficient 1.3 expresses two significant digits; a coefficient of 1.30 would express three. Both have the same numerical value. Neither notation establishes the uncertainty without information about the measurement.

In this unit, “precision” often describes closeness among repeated indications, as in Lesson 2. People also use it informally for the number of displayed digits. Always make the intended meaning clear. A set can be tightly grouped yet systematically displaced, and a screen can show many digits while the readings vary widely. Avoid treating any of these properties as a synonym for accuracy.

Activity 2.20 · Read the claim behind the digits

Allow 12 minutes. A nearest-millimetre record contains 126 mm. Compare reports A: 0.126 m; B: 126.000 mm; C: “approximately 126 mm, recorded to the nearest millimetre.” Which preserves the original unit-conversion information, which adds unsupported apparent detail, and which makes the procedure visible? Count significant digits and decimal places in 0.00470 m and 4.70 mm. Explain why neither count establishes accuracy.

Hint for Activity 2.20

Separate a change of unit from a change in the final written digit. Look for method information as well as numerals.

Discussion of Activity 2.20

A is the exact conversion of the written 126 mm and preserves its three significant digits. B adds three decimal places without observational support. C states both an approximate physical result and the recording rule, although a full report still needs the object, method and provenance. Both 0.00470 m and 4.70 mm express three significant digits; they have five and two decimal places respectively. Those digits describe the representation. They do not check a ruler’s calibration, endpoint choice or reading procedure.

2.20 Round once, at the end

Rounding replaces a value by a nearby value on a chosen numerical grid. First choose the grid: whole millimetres, tenths of a millimetre, or some other stated increment. Then choose the nearest grid value. When the value is exactly halfway between two possibilities, a tie rule is needed. In this lesson we use nearest, with exact ties to even: choose the candidate whose final retained digit is even. Other conventions exist, so name the convention when a tie matters.

Example 2.18 · One rounding step or two?

To one decimal place, 126.346 becomes 126.3, while 126.36 becomes 126.4. For exact decimal ties, 126.25 becomes 126.2 and 126.35 becomes 126.4: the retained tenths digits 2 and 4 are even.

Now consider the constructed arithmetic value 126.251. Directly rounding it to one decimal place gives 126.3 because it is above the midpoint 126.25. But rounding first to two decimal places gives 126.25; rounding that new value to one decimal place then gives 126.2. The first rounding has erased the information that distinguished the original from an exact tie. Keep the working value and round directly to the final display.

Rounding is not a correction for systematic error. It also does not turn a displayed increment into a complete uncertainty estimate. If a reading was rounded to the nearest millimetre, we know something about that numerical recording operation. We have not thereby bounded errors from alignment, an indistinct endpoint, calibration or changes in the object. Do not attach a universal “half a division” uncertainty merely because the ruler has divisions.

The mean requires a further distinction. Five integer-millimetre entries can have a fractional-millimetre arithmetic mean. That fraction is a valid description of the list, even though no individual indication was recorded at that increment. There is no rule here that every descriptive mean must be rounded to a whole millimetre, nor that every calculator digit should be published. State what was averaged and avoid presenting extra arithmetic detail as demonstrated submillimetre accuracy.

Example 2.19 · A fractional mean with a modest claim

For the simulated list 126, 127, 127, 128, 129 mm, the sum is 637 mm and the count is exactly five:

x¯=637mm5=127.4mm

The absolute distances from 127.4 mm are 1.4, 0.4, 0.4, 0.6 and 1.6 mm. Their sum is 4.4 mm, so the mean absolute deviation about the mean is:

D=4.4mm5=0.88mm

A compact descriptive report may show mean 127.4 mm, range 3 mm and D about 0.9 mm, explicitly stating five nearest-millimetre readings. Keep 0.88 in the working calculation. A brief physical description could instead say “about 127 mm”, alongside the full record. These serve different purposes; neither establishes a numerical uncertainty in the true length. The exact count five is not a one-significant-digit measurement that limits the mean.

Activity 2.21 · Choose and explain the rounding

Allow 18 minutes. Using the stated tie rule, round the exact decimal numbers 18.246, 18.250 and 18.350 to one decimal place. Round 0.004786 to three significant digits. Show how rounding 18.251 first to two decimal places can change its later one-decimal result. Finally, repair “Our ruler is accurate to 0.1 mm because five whole-millimetre readings averaged to 127.4 mm.”

Hint for Activity 2.21

For significant digits, begin counting at the first nonzero digit. For the final sentence, distinguish the list’s arithmetic from instrument performance.

Discussion of Activity 2.21

The one-decimal results are 18.2, 18.2 and 18.4. The last two are exact ties and select an even retained digit. Three significant digits gives 0.00479. Direct rounding of 18.251 gives 18.3; the two-step route gives 18.25 then 18.2. A repair is: “The arithmetic mean of these five nearest-millimetre readings is 127.4 mm. Its fractional digit comes from averaging and does not establish 0.1 mm accuracy.” A defensible account retains the procedure and spread rather than inventing an uncertainty from the mean’s digits.

2.21 Missing, mistaken or merely unusual?

Before calculating a mean, decide what its list represents. Three situations that may look inconvenient in a spreadsheet require different reasoning. A missing reading supplies no numerical observation. A documented transcription error supplies evidence for a correction to the entered data. An unusual reading supplies a question to investigate, but its size alone does not identify an error. A fourth situation, a measurement of the wrong quantity, may be perfectly real while being unsuitable for the stated comparison.

Consider simulated record P101-U02-L04-A, a new teaching case rather than a revision of an earlier lesson’s data. Seven attempts concern the A–B edge of one fixed flat card. The ruler has 1 mm divisions, the procedure records to the nearest millimetre from above, and each attempt starts with a fresh placement. The following source facts are stipulated for this exercise. “Entered value” means the first electronic transcription; it is not automatically the original instrument reading.

Attempt 3 has no reading. A blank or a dedicated missing marker can represent that fact; zero cannot, because zero is itself a possible numerical reading. Attempt 4 has two records at different stages: the paper source says 126 mm and its transcription says 162 mm. Preserve the entered 162, identify the paper source and create a corrected value of 126 in a derived record. If the paper entry were illegible, guessing 126 because neighbouring values are nearby would not be the same operation.

Attempt 7 measured C–D, a different edge. Exclude it from a summary specifically of A–B and record that reason. Keep it in the source ledger: exclusion from one calculation is not deletion from history. Attempts 5 and 6 remain included. An indistinct corner is a relevant caution, and 136 mm is conspicuous, but neither supplied note proves a numerical correction or establishes that those readings measured a different quantity.

Example 2.20 · Count attempts and usable readings separately

The included attempt IDs are 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6, with processed values 126, 127, 126, 125 and 136 mm. There were seven attempts, one missing reading and one excluded wrong-edge reading. One included transcription was corrected using its stipulated paper source. The correction is not an additional reading.

x¯=640mm5=128mm

The observed endpoints are 125 and 136 mm, giving range 11 mm. Distances from the mean are 2, 1, 2, 3 and 8 mm, giving D = 3.2 mm. A denominator of seven would count attempts that supplied no eligible A–B reading. Substituting zero for the missing value while otherwise retaining these five would give a denominator of six and a mean about 106.7 mm—an artefact of invented data.

There is no automatic “bad value” threshold in this decision. A range check can flag a value for inspection, but the flag is not a diagnosis. In a real investigation, inspect the original record, the quantity measured and the procedure. If validity remains uncertain, report that uncertainty in the account and seek another observation under a specified procedure. Do not overwrite the old attempt with the new one.

Activity 2.22 · Make decisions from evidence

Allow 20 minutes. Write a decision for each of the seven attempts, including the evidence used. Recalculate the mean, range and D from the included values. Then suppose the paper source for attempt 4 was unavailable: explain why “correct to 126” would no longer be justified. Do not invent a replacement. Finally, say whether a recorded zero in an otherwise valid record should be treated as missing just because it is zero.

Hint for Activity 2.22

A correction needs evidence for the replacement, not merely evidence that an entry looks odd. State the number of included observations before dividing.

Discussion of Activity 2.22

Keep 1, 2, 5 and 6; correct 4 to 126 using the stipulated paper entry; exclude 3 from numerical summaries because no reading exists; exclude 7 from this A–B calculation because the note identifies C–D. Five values sum to 640 mm, giving mean 128 mm. Their range is 11 mm and their absolute deviations total 16 mm, giving D = 3.2 mm. Without the paper source, 162 remains an unresolved entered value: request the source or report a clearly labelled provisional analysis, with any inclusion choice and its limitation made explicit. There is no justified replacement of 126. A valid numerical zero remains a reading; missingness is a separate state.

2.22 Keep the route back to the source

Provenance is the account of where data came from and what happened to it. It includes the source, the circumstances of acquisition and the transformations leading to a result. A filename alone rarely supplies enough. An entry such as “126” could describe a length in millimetres, an angle in degrees, an identifier or a count. Its surrounding information makes the numerical operation meaningful.

The photograph places physical specimens beside a written record and digital images. A number exported from that setting would need a link back to the relevant specimen and method. Our ruler exercise is smaller, but the logical need is the same. A reader who receives only the final mean cannot reconstruct which edge was measured, which attempt was interrupted or why an electronic entry changed.

Information describing a dataset is often called metadata. For this exercise, retain the record ID, simulated status, A–B endpoint definition, unit, instrument divisions, nearest-millimetre rule, fresh-placement procedure and chronological attempt IDs. In real work also retain the acquisition date or sequence, observer or method identifier, relevant conditions and the instrument identification needed to understand the comparison. Record what is actually known; a template is not permission to invent a temperature or calibration date.

Example 2.21 · A small correction note that can be checked

A useful decision entry says: “Record P101-U02-L04-A, attempt 4: first transcription 162 mm; use 126 mm in processed version 1 because the supplied paper-log entry for attempt 4 clearly reads 126 mm.” It identifies the changed field, both values, the relevant source and the derived version.

“Fixed typo” is insufficient: it does not say what changed or how the replacement was known. “Cleaned the data” is weaker still. For attempt 7, a separate note should state exclusion from the A–B summary because the source records C–D. Neither note requires changing the source ledger.

A reproducible calculation allows another reader to repeat the documented operations from the same inputs. It is an important check, but repeating the same arithmetic is not an independent measurement. Two people can reproduce an answer from an incorrect source. Provenance makes assumptions and decisions available for challenge; it does not make them automatically true.

Use the ledger rather than filling gaps with plausible laboratory details. Distinguish what identifies a reading from what describes the calculation.

Discussion of Activity 2.23

Needed information includes the quantity/endpoints, units, source and simulated status, acquisition procedure, included attempt IDs and processing decisions. A suitable note is: “P101-U02-L04-A, simulated card A–B length, mm; 1 mm ruler divisions, nearest-mm recording from above, fresh placement each attempt. Processed version 1 includes attempts 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 in that order. Attempt 4 uses the supplied paper entry 126; the entered 162 is retained in the source transcription. Attempt 3 was interrupted; attempt 7 recorded C–D and is excluded from this A–B summary.” Do not invent missing dates or conditions. Reproducing the mean checks the documented calculation, not the physical endpoint choice, instrument condition or fidelity of an uninspected original record.

2.23 Prepare records without losing them

The computer now needs more than a bare list of numbers. We want each entered value to remain attached to an attempt ID and note. Python’s dictionary stores values under named keys. In {"trial": 1, "entered_mm": 126}, braces enclose the dictionary, each colon joins a key to its value, and commas separate the pairs. The keys here are text strings. Access record["trial"] to retrieve the value stored under that key; unlike a list index, this key is a name rather than a position.

A list of dictionaries keeps several such records in order. We use None, Python’s special “no value” object, for the missing entry. It is neither zero nor the text string "None". The test value is None asks whether that missing marker is present. Do not use a numerical zero as the marker or a test that discards every value considered false: both can lose legitimate zero readings.

# P101-U02-L04-A. All source facts are simulated and stipulated.
source_records = [
    {"trial": 1, "entered_mm": 126, "note": "Fresh placement"},
    {"trial": 2, "entered_mm": 127, "note": "Fresh placement"},
    {"trial": 3, "entered_mm": None, "note": "Interrupted; no reading"},
    {"trial": 4, "entered_mm": 162, "note": "Paper log says 126 mm"},
    {"trial": 5, "entered_mm": 125, "note": "Corner B less distinct"},
    {"trial": 6, "entered_mm": 136, "note": "No fault documented"},
    {"trial": 7, "entered_mm": 130, "note": "Measured C-D, not A-B"},
]

# Each trial ID has an explicit reviewed decision.
decisions = {
    1: {"action": "keep", "reason": "Specified A-B procedure"},
    2: {"action": "keep", "reason": "Specified A-B procedure"},
    3: {"action": "exclude", "reason": "No reading obtained"},
    4: {"action": "correct", "replacement_mm": 126,
        "reason": "Supplied paper-log entry for attempt 4 says 126 mm"},
    5: {"action": "keep", "reason": "Note does not establish invalidity"},
    6: {"action": "keep", "reason": "Unusual alone is not invalid"},
    7: {"action": "exclude", "reason": "Source identifies wrong edge C-D"},
}
print("First entered value / mm:", source_records[0]["entered_mm"])
print("Attempt 4 decision:", decisions[4]["action"])

There are two kinds of lookup in the final lines. source_records[0] selects the first dictionary in the ordered list, then ["entered_mm"] selects a field inside it. In decisions[4], the integer 4 is a dictionary key identifying attempt 4; it is not the fifth list position. The nested lookup then selects that decision’s action. Trace these differences on paper before running the code.

The function below reads both structures and creates a new list. Its input contract is deliberately narrow: source entries have unique trial IDs, an entered value that is a finite real number in millimetres or None, and a note; each decision has an action and nonempty reason, with a finite numerical replacement for a correction. This is a teaching function for reviewed records, not a general importer of arbitrary files. It checks several common logical mistakes but cannot verify that a written reason is true.

def prepare_readings(source_records, decisions):
    processed = []
    seen_trials = []
    for record in source_records:
        trial = record["trial"]
        if trial in seen_trials:
            raise ValueError("Duplicate trial ID")
        seen_trials.append(trial)
        if trial not in decisions:
            raise ValueError("Every trial needs a decision")
        decision = decisions[trial]
        action = decision["action"]
        reason = decision["reason"]
        if reason == "":
            raise ValueError("Every decision needs a reason")
        if action == "keep":
            value = record["entered_mm"]
        elif action == "correct":
            value = decision["replacement_mm"]
        elif action == "exclude":
            continue
        else:
            raise ValueError("Unknown action")
        if value is None:
            raise ValueError("An included reading cannot be missing")
        processed.append({"trial": trial, "value_mm": value,
                          "action": action, "reason": reason})
    return processed

processed = prepare_readings(source_records, decisions)
values_mm = []
for record in processed:
    values_mm.append(record["value_mm"])
print("Included values / mm:", values_mm)
print("Original attempt 4 entry / mm:", source_records[3]["entered_mm"])

The membership test trial in seen_trials checks whether the ID has already occurred; not in reverses the question. Membership in the dictionary decisions checks its keys. The added elif clauses mean “otherwise, if”: only the first matching branch runs. continue moves straight to the next loop iteration, so an excluded entry never reaches the append operation. else catches an unrecognised action. These branches implement the reviewed decision; they do not infer one from the reading’s size.

Each successful pass constructs a fresh dictionary in processed. There is no assignment to a field in source_records, so the original 162 and the missing marker remain there. The result should contain 126, 127, 126, 125, 136 in that order; the final printed source entry should still be 162. Excluded attempts are absent from the processed list but remain visible in the source and decision records. Keep all three structures together.

Use the three summary functions from Lesson 3, included in the notebook, on values_mm. If no values remain, a report should say that no eligible readings exist. It should not announce a zero mean. A displayed result can then be shortened with format(number, ".1f"): this returns text with one digit after the decimal point. The original number remains available for later calculations. Formatting is the final presentation step, not a new measurement.

if len(values_mm) == 0:
    print("No eligible readings; no numerical summary")
else:
    mean_mm = mean_value(values_mm)
    spread_mm = mean_absolute_deviation(values_mm)
    print("Attempts:", len(source_records))
    print("Included readings:", len(values_mm))
    print("Descriptive mean / mm:", format(mean_mm, ".1f"))
    print("Range / mm:", range_value(values_mm))
    print("Mean absolute deviation / mm:", format(spread_mm, ".1f"))

The output displays mean 128.0 mm and D 3.2 mm for five included readings out of seven attempts. Here one-decimal formatting is a stated descriptive display, not a claim that the ruler resolved tenths. You could print the mean as 128 mm in the written report. The code still needs the surrounding metadata and decision record; these print lines alone are not a complete scientific account.

Example 2.22 · Check a missing marker and a real zero

For a separate simulated record with two eligible numerical readings, 0 and 2 mm, the mean is 1 mm, range 2 mm and D = 1 mm. If a test accidentally rejects zero, only 2 remains, producing mean 2 mm and range zero. That can look reassuring while being wrong. With our function, a zero marked keep passes the is None check. A missing entry marked keep raises an error instead.

If every reviewed decision is exclude, the processed list is empty. That list accurately represents the selection; the reporting guard must then suppress the numerical summaries. This is different from calling the Lesson 3 mean function on an empty list, which deliberately raises ValueError.

One numerical subtlety remains. Python stores ordinary decimal-looking float values approximately in binary. Its round uses nearest with ties to even for exact ties, so round(2.5) is 2 and round(3.5) is 4. A decimal literal such as 2.675 need not be stored as an exact halfway value; round(2.675, 2) gives 2.67. This is a representation issue, not evidence about an instrument. Use exact written decimals for the hand-rounding activities, preserve working values in the code and do not use a float tie as a test of a measurement’s validity.

Activity 2.24 · Build an auditable calculation

Allow 120 minutes in the notebook. Spend about 40 minutes transcribing the ledger, predicting included IDs and tracing dictionary lookups; 40 minutes building and running the preparation and summary functions; 20 minutes on the boundary checks below; and 20 minutes writing the report and restarting the notebook.

Check the seven-attempt case against your hand calculation. Then create separate small records for a retained zero, an excluded missing reading and an all-excluded set. In scratch calls, try keeping a missing reading, duplicating an ID, omitting a decision and using an unknown action. Predict each outcome first. Record the results, remove deliberate failing calls from the final run, and restart and run all cells in order. Keep the main source record unchanged.

Hint for Activity 2.24

Use a new list for every check. Print source attempt 4 after processing. The calculation tests cannot establish the truth of the supplied source notes.

Discussion of Activity 2.24

The main processed IDs are 1, 2, 4, 5, 6; values are 126, 127, 126, 125, 136. Count 5, mean 128 mm, range 11 mm and D = 3.2 mm match the hand result. Source attempt 4 remains 162. A retained numerical zero is included. A missing reading explicitly excluded contributes neither a value nor a count to the summaries. An all-excluded record produces an empty processed list and the guarded report prints no numerical summary.

Keeping a missing marker, repeating an ID, supplying no decision for an ID or using an unknown action should each raise ValueError with a relevant message. A correction to None is also refused. Those refusals expose record-preparation mistakes; they do not prove all possible inputs safe. Check the stated finite-number input contract, units and source evidence separately. A good final notebook preserves predictions, observed check results, metadata and a written interpretation, not just printed numbers.

2.24 A report that can be checked

We can now audit a report at two levels. First, is the arithmetic correct for the stated included values? Second, does the report give those values and calculations a defensible physical meaning? The first question catches wrong sums, counts and formulas. The second catches an invented correction, a missing unit, a disguised omission or a claim of accuracy that the method does not support.

A short report does not have to repeat every line of the notebook. It should identify the record and quantity, state the units and procedure, distinguish attempts from included readings, explain material processing decisions and label the summaries. Link or attach the ledger so that the summary can be checked. End with the limits that matter for the intended conclusion and a next check that addresses one of them.

Activity 2.25 · Repair a misleading report

Allow 22 minutes. Rewrite this account in about 180–250 words: “We took seven good measurements. The exact length is 128.000 mm. The strange readings were cleaned automatically, the missing reading was treated as zero, and D proves the true length is within 3.2 mm of the mean.” Use record P101-U02-L04-A, the documented decisions and your checked summaries. Explain your display choice and propose a next observation without rewriting the old record.

Hint for Activity 2.25

Every factual claim should have a route back to the ledger or calculation. D is a description of the included readings, not a guaranteed error bound.

Discussion of Activity 2.25

A suitable report is: “Record P101-U02-L04-A is a simulated study of the A–B edge of one fixed card. The stipulated procedure uses a ruler with 1 mm divisions, reads from above to the nearest millimetre and starts each attempt with a fresh placement. Seven attempts produced five values included in this summary. Attempt 3 was interrupted before a reading existed; attempt 7 measured C–D and was excluded from the A–B calculation. Attempt 4’s entered 162 mm was replaced by 126 mm only in the processed record, using the supplied paper-log entry. The original transcription and reasons remain available.

The included values, in attempt order, are 126, 127, 126, 125 and 136 mm. Their arithmetic mean is 128 mm, range 11 mm and mean absolute deviation about the mean 3.2 mm. These are descriptive summaries; the displayed digits do not establish an exact true length or a complete measurement uncertainty. The unusual 136 mm remains included because no fault was documented. The indistinct-corner note also remains visible. I would inspect the original endpoint procedure and take additional clearly identified A–B readings, retaining them as new observations rather than overwriting earlier attempts. This could investigate the unusual value, but would not by itself certify the ruler’s calibration.”

The whole-millimetre display of the mean is sufficient here because the calculated mean is an integer; the working record remains available. D uses a decimal to describe the average distance in this list. Neither choice is a new claim about instrument resolution.

Use the feedback

Use the allocated 30 feedback minutes to revisit the step behind any disagreement. A wrong rounded digit may come from choosing the wrong place, applying a different tie rule or rounding twice. A surprising mean may come from counting attempts instead of included readings. A clean-looking list may conceal a lost zero or an unsupported replacement. Trace the result back through the layers in Figure 2.19 before changing a formula.

You now have the ingredients for a small, inspectable measurement account: a defined quantity, a procedure, preserved observations, explicit decisions and labelled summaries with an appropriate display. The final lesson of Unit 2 will bring these together in an investigation and the unit’s cumulative practice. Keep this notebook and the original source ledger: the point of a summary is to help a reader understand the evidence, not to make that evidence disappear.

Further reading and context

  • NIST handbook: detection of outliers, opening discussion, distinguishes investigation from automatic rejection. Formal statistical tests later on that page are beyond this lesson.
  • Python tutorial: dictionaries and built-in functions document dictionary keys, format and round. This lesson uses only the explained subset.
  • International vocabulary of metrology: resolution supplies the formal definition behind our distinction between indication and display.
