P101 · Unit 2 · Lesson 2

Variation, bias and
a fair comparison

Five readings can agree closely and still answer the question badly. Five readings can also differ without any one of them being a blunder. To interpret a measurement record, we need to ask both how the readings differ from one another and what could affect them together.

Lesson 1 established a quantity, a repeat procedure and a preserved record. We now use those foundations to investigate variation. Our aim is to choose a useful next check: repeat the observation, change a particular part of the method, or compare with a suitable reference. These choices answer different questions. More data are useful when we know which question they address.

Allow 4 hours, including 1 hour of Python: 60 minutes for the teaching, 90 for written activities, 60 for the notebook and 30 for feedback and corrections. All numerical records in this lesson are explicitly simulated. No measuring equipment is required. Use a pencil and notebook, and download the learner notebook or plain Python companion. Keep your Lesson 1 record available; no previous personal result is assumed.

2.7 What varies when we repeat?

Recall the simulated card record P101-U02-L01-A: 126, 127, 126, 125 and 126 mm, in that order. The ruler was lifted and replaced each time, the same edge was used, and readings were recorded to the nearest millimetre. The note beside trial 4 said that corner B looked less distinct. The numbers were not five copies of one observation: the stipulated reset made each placement a fresh attempt.

Several small influences can change between attempts even when we intend to follow the same method. We might align the ruler slightly differently, judge the centre of a broad mark differently, or view the endpoint from a slightly different position. A change in the object itself is another possibility. The numerical list alone cannot tell us which influence was responsible. The procedure and the notes are part of the evidence for an explanation.

Repeatability concerns agreement under a specified set of closely matched conditions: the same procedure, observer, equipment and location, with repeats made over a short time. These conditions make the comparison informative. If another observer measures a different card tomorrow, a disagreement might be useful, but it is no longer a simple test of the repeatability of today’s method on this card.

We use random variation for variation whose individual changes are unpredictable under the stated conditions. Calling an effect random does not mean it has no physical cause. It means that, for this measurement process, we cannot predict the next contribution in detail. Nor does it mean successive readings must alternate above and below some central value. A short run can contain several increases or decreases by chance.

A consistent movement with time deserves a different question. A sensor might warm up, a sample might cool, or a support might slowly move. Such a change is often called drift. Before describing a changing record as random scatter, inspect its order and the conditions. Sorting the values can hide the very clue that would prompt you to investigate drift. Five readings are usually too little evidence to establish a mechanism; “consistent with” is often a better phrase than “proves”.

Example 2.6 · The order contains information

Compare two simulated sequences in millimetres: A is 126, 127, 126, 125, 126; B is 124, 125, 126, 127, 128. What would you investigate first?

A moves up and down without a steady progression. B increases on every trial. For B, inspect elapsed time, temperature and any gradual movement before treating the changes as interchangeable repeats. You have not established drift merely by noticing the pattern: it could occur by chance, and a recording error is also possible. The pattern makes a time-related check worth doing. For A, inspect placement and endpoint notes, including the trial-4 difficulty. Neither sequence licenses deleting a reading simply because it is inconvenient.

Activity 2.8 · What did the repeats test?

Allow 12 minutes. A student obtains five identical readings without moving the ruler. A second student lifts and replaces it between five readings, obtaining slightly different values. Explain what each procedure tests. Does the first establish a better measurement? Then explain what extra record you would request if the second student says the values “kept getting larger”.

Hint for Activity 2.8

Ask which part of the operation was allowed to vary. A list in trial order and a sorted list answer different questions.

Discussion of Activity 2.8

The unchanged placement mainly tests repeated reading of one arrangement, including the display or reading convention. Fresh placements also expose placement variability. Identical readings in the first procedure do not establish better accuracy or better performance under repositioning; the ruler could be consistently misaligned. Request the actual ordered readings, times or trial sequence, and notes about conditions and movement. These help investigate a possible trend. A coarse display can also hide small changes, so retain the graduation or display increment.

Repeatability therefore describes an aspect of a method under conditions. To judge how well that method answers the physical question, we need another distinction: close agreement within a set can coexist with a shift of the entire set.

2.8 Scatter and a common shift

Imagine a simple direct-reading length gauge. Unlike a ruler used to subtract two coordinates, this instrument displays a length as one indication. For a teaching model, suppose every indication contains an added 2 mm. Repeating the measurement does not remove that addition. Each fresh reading passes through the same instrument and acquires the same shift.

A systematic effect contributes an error that stays the same or changes predictably across repeated measurements. A constant offset is one example; a scale factor that makes larger lengths increasingly too large is another. A numerical estimate of a systematic error is called bias. We do not know such an estimate merely because a set of readings looks tidy. It needs evidence about the method or a suitable reference.

Read Figure 2.8 by following each value horizontally. A value of 49 mm becomes 51 mm; 50 mm becomes 52 mm; 51 mm becomes 53 mm. The repeated values remain repeated. The separation between any two values stays the same because both receive the same addition. No amount of examining those separations alone can reveal the common 2 mm shift.

Precision concerns agreement among repeated measured values under stated conditions. Repeatability is precision under repeatability conditions. Accuracy concerns closeness to the value of the intended quantity. These are useful descriptions, not interchangeable claims. A tightly grouped set can be displaced from a suitable reference. Conversely, a variable set can include an individual reading close to the reference without the method being reliably close on each occasion.

Example 2.7 · A shift in an indication or in both coordinates?

A direct-reading gauge indicates 52 mm when the stipulated input length is 50 mm. In a separate ruler calculation, both endpoint coordinates have an added 2 mm: 12 mm is written instead of 10 mm, and 62 mm instead of 60 mm. Compare the consequences.

52mm−50mm=+2mm

The direct indication is 2 mm high. For the ruler, the measured length is instead a difference:

(60+2)−(10+2)=50

All numbers in the second calculation are in millimetres. The common coordinate addition cancels. This does not mean every ruler fault cancels: incorrect mark spacing changes intervals, and using the physical end as though it were the zero mark can change the procedure. Trace the suspected effect through the actual measurement calculation before naming its consequence.

This also explains why “human error” is an unhelpful diagnosis. An observer who consistently chooses the wrong endpoint may shift every result. An observer who places the ruler differently on each attempt may introduce variable effects. A transcription mistake is a separate event needing evidence and a documented correction. The identity of the person or instrument does not by itself classify the effect.

Activity 2.9 · Choose the check

Allow 14 minutes. For each case, propose a useful check and state what it could reveal: (a) five close readings, all above an independently supplied reference; (b) readings vary after each replacement of a ruler; (c) both ruler endpoint coordinates have the same added constant; (d) a value was typed as 162 although the original paper record says 126. Explain why “take more repeats and keep the closest ones” is not a sound common remedy.

Hint for Activity 2.9

Follow the procedure. A comparison with a reference, a placement check and a transcription check test different possibilities.

Discussion of Activity 2.9

(a) Check the reference’s suitability and uncertainty, units, instrument setup and definition of the quantity; a common offset is a candidate, not a proven cause. (b) Inspect and standardise alignment and endpoints, preserving all fresh readings. (c) Subtract both coordinates: the common additive constant cancels, although other effects can remain. (d) Correct the transcription against the original, keeping a note of what changed and why. Selecting close readings after seeing the results suppresses evidence of variation and can make a poor method appear better. More repeats alone cannot diagnose a shared fault.

2.9 Change the method fairly

Suppose two reading methods disagree. Method A views a ruler obliquely; method B views it from above. If the same person uses A on one card in the morning and a different person uses B on another card in the afternoon, what produced the difference? Viewing direction, card, observer and time all changed together. There is no clear way to assign the result to the viewing direction alone.

A controlled comparison holds relevant conditions alike while deliberately changing the factor under investigation. Here that factor is viewing direction. The same defined card edge, ruler, observer and reading convention should be used for both methods. The ruler should be replaced between fresh trials in both. Record which method produced each value and retain the trial order. “Everything else the same” is an intention that needs concrete instructions.

Time is particularly easy to overlook. Performing every A trial before every B trial makes method and time move together. If conditions drift during the work, the later method may appear different for that reason. Alternating methods brings their trials closer in time. Reversing which method is first in some pairs can also reduce a simple order imbalance. These arrangements help with specific risks; they do not make all conditions identical or guarantee an unbiased result.

Example 2.8 · Repair a comparison plan

A proposed plan is “Measure card X five times with A before lunch; measure card Y five times with B after lunch; choose the more consistent method.”

Begin by fixing the question: how does viewing direction affect readings of the same edge? Use one stable card and mark the intended endpoints. Use the same ruler and observer. Define both viewing positions and use the same nearest-millimetre convention. Make fresh placements, recording the value before the next trial. A possible order for four paired comparisons is A–B, B–A, A–B, B–A; retain that order in the record. Stop and note any changed condition instead of silently continuing under the old label.

The revised plan better separates viewing direction from object and gross timing differences. It still needs inspection: a fixed oblique position may be hard to reproduce, and an observer may be influenced by remembered readings. Recording immediately, avoiding deliberate matching, and retaining procedural notes make those limitations visible. The plan is improved, not rendered perfect.

The photograph shows why “keep the conditions alike” is substantive work. In this NIST study, comparisons in air and vacuum addressed how exposure to air affected mass standards. The differing surface areas helped make that question investigable. We do not need the apparatus’s detailed physics here. Notice the design decision: choose objects and conditions so that a particular effect can be examined, then document the limits of that comparison.

Activity 2.10 · Design before collecting

Allow 18 minutes. Write a plan to compare reading a ruler from above with reading it from one specified oblique position. State the measurand, the changed factor, at least four conditions held alike, the reset procedure and a trial order. Explain one limitation the ordering helps with and one it does not remove. Finally, say what extra evidence you would need to claim that one method is more accurate. This is a design activity; carrying it out is not required.

Hint for Activity 2.10

Use the same quantity for both methods. Agreement between the two methods cannot detect a fault they share.

Discussion of Activity 2.10

A suitable plan specifies a marked edge on one flat card; changes viewing direction only; retains the card, ruler, observer, nearest-millimetre convention and supporting surface; and replaces and realigns the ruler for each trial. A–B then B–A pairs limit a simple association between method and being first or later. They do not remove a shared ruler-scale fault, unclear endpoints or all time-dependent effects. Record actual order and deviations. To investigate accuracy, compare with an independent suitable reference for the same defined length, taking its uncertainty into account. Another uncontrolled set from the same ruler would not by itself supply that evidence.

Fair comparisons help explain disagreements between procedures. A reference adds a different kind of information: a value supported outside the repeated readings we are trying to assess.

2.10 What a reference adds

A reference is not simply the number we hoped to obtain. It is a value supported by a stated basis: for example, a calibrated standard with an assigned value and uncertainty appropriate to the comparison. A package label, an unverified household object or a second instrument that has never been checked may be useful clues, but they do not automatically provide a reference of the required quality.

Calibration establishes a relationship between reference values and instrument indications under specified conditions, including their uncertainties, so that indications can be used to obtain measurement results. It is distinct from adjustment, which changes how an instrument responds. A calibration can reveal a correction to apply without changing the instrument. Adjustment may be followed by calibration; turning a zero control alone does not establish performance across the instrument’s range.

For arithmetic practice, record P101-U02-L02-A stipulates a reference value of 50 mm and direct gauge indications 52, 53, 51, 52 and 52 mm. These are invented values, recorded in trial order. The displayed increment is stipulated as 1 mm, with a fresh placement of the same reference for each trial. We temporarily treat 50 mm as exact within this teaching calculation. An actual reference would carry uncertainty, and this exercise is not a calibration certificate.

Example 2.9 · A signed discrepancy and a proposed correction

For the first indication, subtract the reference from the indication:

d=52mm−50mm=+2mm

The positive sign means the indication is above the reference. All five discrepancies are +2, +3, +1, +2 and +2 mm. These are individual differences, not five separate estimates of an exactly known constant bias.

Now suppose independent information supplies a constant-offset model of +2 mm for these conditions. To compensate for that stipulated offset, subtract 2 mm from each indication. The correction is −2 mm. The derived values are 50, 51, 49, 50 and 50 mm. Their variation remains: a common correction shifts the set without making its members equal. Keep both the original indications and the model used to derive the corrected values.

The phrase “independent information supplies” is doing essential work. We did not prove an exact 2 mm offset by choosing a convenient centre of five readings. The correction is conditional on a model supplied for this exercise. In real work, the evidence for that model, its uncertainty and its applicable conditions belong in the record. Applying a correction does not make a result exact; uncertainty about the correction can remain.

Example 2.10 · One reference does not establish the rule

Two simplified instrument models both indicate 52 mm for an input of 50 mm. Model A adds 2 mm. Model B multiplies the input by 1.04. What do they predict for a 100 mm input?

yA=100mm+2mm=102mm
yB=1.04×100mm=104mm

The factor 1.04 has no unit: it scales the length. The models agree at 50 mm but disagree at 100 mm. A suitable second reference can help distinguish them. Subtracting 2 mm from Model B’s 104 mm indication gives 102 mm, still 2 mm above the 100 mm input. A correction supported at one value cannot simply be assumed valid everywhere. Real instruments can depart from both simple models, so two reference checks would not establish an exact response over an entire range either.

Activity 2.11 · State the sign and the limit

Allow 16 minutes. (a) A simulated indication is 98 mm for a stipulated 100 mm reference. Find indication minus reference. If a constant offset of that amount were independently established, what correction would compensate for it? (b) Explain why five repeats at 100 mm would not establish that correction at 200 mm. (c) A colleague says “I adjusted the zero, so calibration is complete.” Write a two-sentence reply.

Hint for Activity 2.11

A negative discrepancy means the indication is low. Distinguish checking one point from determining a response across a range.

Discussion of Activity 2.11

(a) The discrepancy is −2 mm, so a compensating correction would be +2 mm under the stated constant-offset assumption. (b) Repeats probe behaviour at the tested value and conditions. A scale error or other response could give a different discrepancy at 200 mm; another suitable reference and a justified model are needed. (c) Adjusting the zero changes the instrument’s response at one setting. Calibration requires a supported relationship to references, with relevant conditions and uncertainties, and zero adjustment alone does not provide it.

2.11 Make the calculation inspectable

Python can repeat the subtraction accurately while leaving the physical question unanswered. We therefore give the calculation a narrow job: form signed discrepancies from a preserved list, then apply a separately stated hypothetical correction. The input label, units and assumptions make the resulting lists interpretable. A list called “answers” would hide too much.

Use the simulated gauge record from Section 2.10. The function takes one reading and one reference value, both in millimetres, and returns their signed difference in millimetres. The loop calls it once for each reading. As in Lesson 1, initialise a new list before the loop and append one result on each pass. This preserves trial order and leaves the raw list intact.

# P101-U02-L02-A: simulated direct-gauge indications.
# Stipulated reference; not an actual calibration.
raw_mm = [52, 53, 51, 52, 52]
reference_mm = 50

def discrepancy_mm(reading_mm, reference_mm):
    return reading_mm - reference_mm

differences_mm = []
for reading_mm in raw_mm:
    differences_mm.append(discrepancy_mm(reading_mm, reference_mm))

print("Raw / mm:", raw_mm)
print("Indication minus reference / mm:", differences_mm)

The discrepancy list should be [2, 3, 1, 2, 2]. Check the second and third entries by hand: 53 minus 50 is 3, while 51 minus 50 is 1. A computer commonly prints a positive number without a plus sign; it still has positive sign. For a reading below the reference, this same subtraction must return a negative value.

The next block asks a different question: what would follow if a common +2 mm offset were justified? The variable name deliberately labels the offset as assumed. We subtract that signed offset, which is equivalent to adding the opposite-signed correction. Do not derive this assumption from the mere fact that it produces a pleasing list.

# Supplied teaching assumption, not inferred calibration evidence.
assumed_offset_mm = 2
corrected_mm = []
for reading_mm in raw_mm:
    corrected_mm.append(reading_mm - assumed_offset_mm)

print("Derived values under the offset model / mm:", corrected_mm)
print("Preserved raw values / mm:", raw_mm)

The derived list is [50, 51, 49, 50, 50]. The raw list must still be [52, 53, 51, 52, 52]. Subtracting from a loop variable and appending to a new list avoids overwriting the evidence. Keeping only corrected values would make it difficult to revisit the calculation if the assumed offset later changed.

Activity 2.12 · Predict, run, check, interpret

Allow 60 minutes using the notebook or Python companion: 10 to predict results, 20 to run and extend the loops, 15 for checks, and 15 to explain the result. Run the two worked blocks from a clean session. Then create a separate practice record with readings 48, 50 and 52 mm against a 50 mm reference. Predict the signs before computing its discrepancies. Keep record A intact.

Write a function that accepts a list of readings and a signed assumed offset, returning a new corrected list. Use only a function, a loop, subtraction and append. Check an empty list, zero offset, and a negative offset of −2 mm. For each, predict the result and check that the input list was preserved. Restart and run all cells in order. Finish with three sentences distinguishing a discrepancy, the supplied offset model and evidence for a real calibration.

Hint for Activity 2.12

Inside the function, start a new empty result list. After the loop, return it. Subtracting a negative offset adds its magnitude. An empty input should give an empty output without manufacturing an observation.

Discussion of Activity 2.12

The practice discrepancies are −2, 0 and +2 mm. Zero means agreement with the stipulated reference for that entry, not proof of exact measurement. An empty list should return []; a zero offset should leave the numerical values unchanged in a new list; subtracting −2 from 48, 50 and 52 should produce 50, 52 and 54. None of these checks needs a mean or a statistics library.

One possible function is:

def apply_offset(readings_mm, offset_mm):
    result_mm = []
    for reading_mm in readings_mm:
        result_mm.append(reading_mm - offset_mm)
    return result_mm

Check it against your predictions, then inspect the input again. A useful explanation says that a discrepancy compares one indication with a reference, the supplied model stipulates a common offset, and the numerical exercise neither establishes that model for a real gauge nor quantifies its uncertainty. A correct program can apply an unsupported model perfectly; interpretation remains your responsibility.

If your values are right but the record’s source or units have disappeared, the work is incomplete. If the program runs only after executing cells out of order, a hidden dependency remains. Correcting these issues now will make the summaries in Lesson 3 much easier to trust.

2.12 A conclusion the evidence can support

We can now read a short comparison without demanding that it deliver one universal verdict. Ask whether both methods address the same quantity, which conditions were controlled, what changed across trials, and what the reference can establish. Then distinguish an observation about the supplied record from a claim about the methods in general.

The following record, P101-U02-L02-B, is simulated. Two direct-reading methods measure the same stipulated 50 mm reference. One observer uses the same setting and a fresh placement on every trial. Each method has a stipulated 1 mm displayed increment. The chronological order is A1, B1, B2, A2, A3, B3, B4, A4, A5, B5. No change of conditions is recorded. The table groups entries by their within-method trial number; it must not replace the stated chronological order. No offset correction has been applied. The operational details distinguishing A from B were not supplied. This is a gap in the record: the values alone cannot identify which procedural difference caused their behaviour.

Method A’s entries lie between 51 and 53 mm; Method B’s lie between 48 and 52 mm. That is a direct description of the listed values, not a formal uncertainty interval. A’s entries are more tightly grouped in this record, yet all are above the reference. B includes values on both sides and one equal to it. That single equality does not establish a superior method, and five entries do not establish long-term performance.

A useful next step depends on what you need to know. To investigate the high indications from A, check setup, reference suitability and possible systematic effects. To investigate B’s variability, inspect its placement and reading procedure. To examine performance across different lengths, introduce suitable references at other lengths. Taking twenty more readings at the same point would enlarge the record but would still leave some of these questions unanswered.

Activity 2.13 · Write a bounded investigation report

Allow 30 minutes. Use about 8 minutes to inspect record B, 12 to write and 10 to audit. Write 250–350 words covering the question and simulated provenance, what the comparison holds alike, two observations directly supported by the values, and two claims the record does not support. Identify the missing method information you would request. Propose one next check for A and one for B, explaining what each could reveal.

Audit this proposed conclusion in your report: “A is more accurate because its readings agree better. Subtract 2 mm from all future A readings. B’s 48 mm value is an outlier, so discard it.” Use no mean, formal spread measure or numerical uncertainty estimate. Keep every entry unless there is independent evidence for a documented correction or exclusion.

Hint for Activity 2.13

Each proposed claim crosses a different gap: agreement versus reference, one-point evidence versus a general correction, and an unusual entry versus a demonstrated fault.

Discussion of Activity 2.13

A sound report identifies the record as a teaching simulation of two methods applied to one stipulated reference. It records the common quantity, observer, displayed increment, reset and supplied order. It describes A’s tighter grouping and consistently high indications, and B’s values on both sides of the reference. “A agrees better with itself in these five entries” is supported; “A is more accurate” does not follow.

A universal −2 mm correction is not justified. A one-point comparison does not establish a constant-offset response at other values, conditions or times. Request the full operational descriptions of A and B before attributing their difference to a particular cause. A useful next check is a suitable reference at a second length, accompanied by setup and uncertainty information. For B, inspect placement consistency and the note-taking procedure, then repeat a clearly specified comparison. The 48 mm entry differs from the others but no procedural fault is documented, so it should remain. Do not label it invalid because removing it makes the record look cleaner.

Several report structures and next checks are reasonable. The criterion is whether a check addresses a stated uncertainty in the explanation. A report that merely lists “more data” has not yet identified what information is missing.

Use the feedback

Use the allocated 30 feedback minutes across your attempts. If repeatability was treated as accuracy, revisit the translated dots in Figure 2.8 and state what stayed unchanged. If a correction had the wrong sign, test it on one indication you know is high or low. If your comparison changes several things together, underline each changed condition and decide which one the investigation is meant to test. Rewrite the claim or method before trying the relevant part again.

We have moved from preserving readings to judging what they can establish. Repetition exposes variability under a stated procedure; a controlled comparison examines a chosen difference; a reference can reveal discrepancies that repetitions alone cannot. None substitutes for the others. Lesson 3 will introduce numerical summaries of repeated readings. Keep the raw order, method notes and assumptions beside those summaries: a compact calculation should help us see the evidence, not erase its meaning.

Further reading and context

The required lesson is self-contained. Optional reading, about 5–10 minutes:

  • JCGM: precision, systematic error, random error and calibration provide formal terminology; some notes assume later statistical ideas.
  • NIST engineering statistics handbook: calibration sets the reference comparison in a broader measurement process. Its advanced subsections are not required.
  • OpenStax College Physics 2e, Section 1.3 offers an introductory comparison of accuracy and precision. Significant figures and uncertainty calculations are beyond this lesson.
