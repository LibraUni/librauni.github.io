Lesson 2
Position, time and a first model

Knowing where something is does not tell us where it will be. To make a prediction, we need a description of how its position changes. In this lesson we will build the simplest useful version of that description, make its assumptions explicit, and turn it into a calculation we can run.

Lesson 1 connected numbers with physical quantities. We now apply that care to a moving object. We will choose what to track, specify where position is measured from, and distinguish a clock reading from a duration. These decisions give meaning to an equation; they also determine what its inputs must mean when we write it in Python.

Allow about three hours, including one hour of notebook work. Begin with paper and a calculator. Before Section 1.11, prepare the environment described in the practical orientation; its setup time belongs to the separate orientation allocation. If you already code, use the notebook tasks to inspect the physical reasoning and checks. You do not need to wait for a particular M101 lesson.

1.7 Choosing what a position means

Consider the crawler in Figure 1.8. If someone reports that it has reached a marker, do they mean that its front, its centre or its rear has reached it? The distinction could matter even if the machine were moving very slowly. A useful description begins by deciding which point on the object our position refers to.

We will use a much smaller imagined vehicle: a trolley moving along a straight laboratory track. Fix a visible mark on its frame and follow that mark. For the question “Where is the mark along the track?”, the trolley’s width, wheel rotation and colour can be left out. That simplification does not claim those features are unimportant for every question. It makes a particular question manageable.

Choose a fixed marker beside the track as the origin, assign it position zero, and choose a positive direction along the track. A position x is then a signed length from the origin to the tracked mark. With positive to the right, x = −2 m means two metres to the left of the origin. The minus sign tells us which side; it does not mean that the trolley has a negative physical size.

Our reference markers remain fixed to the laboratory for the whole calculation. A position stated relative to a moving vehicle would be a different description. For now we need only this one fixed track and one coordinate, so no two-dimensional vector machinery is required.

To find the change in position, subtract the earlier position from the later one. We write Δx = x − x0, where the Greek letter Δ (“delta”) means a change and the subscript 0 identifies the starting value. Here x0 is a name for the initial position; it need not have the value zero. Along this line, the signed change is also called the displacement.

Example 1.6 · Crossing the origin

A trolley mark starts at −2 m and finishes at +4 m. Find the change in position and explain its sign.

Solution

Use final minus initial, retaining the negative starting value:

Δx = 4 m − (−2 m) = +6 m.

The mark has changed position by six metres in the positive direction. It began two metres left of zero and finished four metres right of zero. Adding those two lengths checks the magnitude for this direct movement. Neither the initial nor final coordinate by itself gives the change.

Activity 1.8 · One movement, two origins

Use the movement in Example 1.6. A second observer puts their origin 3 m to the right of O, keeping metres and positive to the right. Sketch both origins and the two trolley positions. Find the second observer’s starting and finishing coordinates, then their change in position. Explain what changed and what did not.

Hint for Activity 1.8

The new origin has old coordinate +3 m. Subtract 3 m from each old position.

Discussion of Activity 1.8

The starting coordinate becomes −2 − 3 = −5 m; the finishing coordinate becomes 4 − 3 = +1 m. Their difference is 1 − (−5) = +6 m. Both positions changed because their reference changed. The displacement did not: the same 3 m was subtracted from both endpoints. Neither the trolley nor its movement was altered by relabelling the scale.

1.8 Two readings, one interval

A laboratory clock may already be running when we begin watching the trolley. Suppose we record the initial position when the clock reads 12 s, then look again when it reads 20 s. The trolley has had eight seconds to move between our observations. Using 20 s as the duration would incorrectly include time before the initial position was recorded.

Let t0 be the clock reading paired with x0, and let t be the later reading. The elapsed time is Δt = t − t0. The pairing matters: the initial position and initial time describe the same event. You cannot freely combine a position measured at one moment with a start time from another.

If we deliberately reset the clock at the initial event, then t0 = 0 and Δt = t. That convenient special case is common in examples, but it is a choice, not a general rule. We will retain both readings in our first program so that the subtraction remains visible.

Activity 1.9 · A clock that was not reset

A camera records the trolley at 35 s and again at 47 s on the same continuous clock. Find the elapsed time. Another clock labels those same events 135 s and 147 s. What duration does it give? Explain why adding 100 s to both readings has no effect on the answer.

Discussion of Activity 1.9

The intervals are 47 − 35 = 12 s and 147 − 135 = 12 s. The extra 100 s cancels in the subtraction. Each pair must use the same clock convention: subtracting 35 s from 147 s would mix two systems and give a spurious 112 s.

We assume an ordinary laboratory clock with consistent seconds and no reset during the interval. Questions about synchronising distant clocks or relativistic time are outside this model. Even within the laboratory, record both the unit and the event associated with each reading.

1.9 From a steady change to an equation

Now make a physical assumption: over the interval of interest, the tracked mark changes position by the same amount in every second. For example, it might advance by 0.75 m towards positive x each second. In two seconds its change would be 1.50 m, and in eight seconds it would be 6.00 m. The amount of change is proportional to the elapsed time.

Call the constant signed rate v. A positive v means that the position coordinate increases; a negative v means that it decreases. This is the one-dimensional constant velocity of our model. We will develop the fuller distinction between speed, velocity and averages in Unit 3. Here v is a signed rate along the chosen axis, with units such as metres per second.

Multiplying a rate by a duration gives the change accumulated during that duration. Thus Δx = vΔt. But a change does not tell us the final position until we know where the movement started. Add that change to x0:

x = x0 + vΔt = x0 + v(t − t0).

This equation collects three different ingredients: an initial condition, a rule for change and the elapsed time over which that rule is applied. The initial condition anchors the prediction to a particular event. The constant rate describes our chosen behaviour. The equation does not explain what forces make the trolley move this way; it describes the assumed motion.

Key idea 1.4 · A constant-rate position model

For motion along a fixed axis with constant signed rate v, the predicted position is x = x0 + v(t − t0). The initial position x0 belongs to time t0. Use compatible units and apply the model only over an interval where its assumptions are reasonable.

Example 1.7 · Keeping the starting event intact

At clock reading 12 s, the trolley mark is at −2 m. Assume its constant signed rate is +0.75 m/s. Predict its position at 20 s.

Solution

First find the duration: Δt = 20 s − 12 s = 8 s. The predicted change is (0.75 m/s)(8 s) = +6 m. Add this to the initial position:

x = −2 m + 6 m = +4 m.

The seconds cancel in the product, leaving metres, which can be added to −2 m. The result lies to the right of the origin, as our sketch suggests. Using 20 s as the interval would predict +13 m; the arithmetic would be correct for the wrong duration.

The expression also works for a fraction of a second. The model assumes a constant rate throughout the interval, not a sequence of sudden jumps at the times shown in Figure 1.11. At t = 14.5 s, for example, 2.5 s has elapsed and the prediction is −2 + 0.75 × 2.5 = −0.125 m. The mark is still slightly left of the origin.

Activity 1.10 · Predict before calculating

A tracked mark is at +5 m when the clock reads 4 s, with constant signed rate −0.50 m/s. Before calculating, decide whether its coordinate at 10 s should be greater or less than +5 m. Find the elapsed time, change and final position. Does the negative rate require the final position to be negative?

Discussion of Activity 1.10

The coordinate should decrease. The elapsed time is 6 s, the change is −3 m, and the final position is +2 m. A negative rate tells us the direction of change, not which side of the origin the object occupies. The mark moves left while remaining right of the origin throughout this interval.

If two observed positions are compatible with a steady-motion assumption, their change divided by their time interval gives a candidate rate. That calculation alone cannot establish that the motion was steady between the observations. A trolley could stop and restart yet arrive at the same endpoint. More observations can test the assumption; they cannot be replaced by the neatness of the formula.

1.10 Asking whether a prediction makes sense

A calculation becomes more trustworthy when we can challenge it in ways that do not simply repeat the same arithmetic. We can check its units, its behaviour at the starting event, and the effect of choosing no motion. These checks are useful before writing code because they establish what any correct implementation of this model must do.

Example 1.8 · Testing a proposed formula

Someone proposes x = x0 + vt for data whose initial position was recorded at t0 = 12 s. What does the formula predict at that initial event?

Solution

Using −2 m and 0.75 m/s gives −2 + 0.75 × 12 = +7 m. But the stipulated initial position is −2 m. The formula fails before we move beyond the starting event. Its units are consistent, so a dimensional check alone would miss the error. The missing subtraction of t0 is the problem. If the time variable had instead meant elapsed time from the start, the same-looking expression would have been appropriate.

Passing these checks establishes some consistency of the calculation. It does not establish that the trolley really maintains a constant rate. Nor does the model know about the ends of the track. If an obstacle intervenes, a numerical prediction beyond it may be perfectly computable and physically inappropriate.

Activity 1.11 · A prediction beyond the track

For Example 1.7, suppose the model is intended for a clear track from −3 m to +6 m. Calculate the prediction at 28 s. Explain why that result needs qualification. Find the clock reading at which the model reaches +6 m; distinguish this boundary time from a prediction of what happens at the end stop.

Hint for Activity 1.11

To reach +6 m from −2 m, the mark needs a change of 8 m. Divide by its rate, then add the starting clock reading.

Discussion of Activity 1.11

At 28 s, 16 s has elapsed, giving −2 + 0.75 × 16 = +10 m. That is outside the stated track. Reaching +6 m requires 8/0.75 = 10⅔ s after the starting event, so the boundary time is 22⅔ s on the clock. The model may describe the approach to that boundary. It gives no account of impact, stopping or reversal there. Those would require a different description.

We should also distinguish a model parameter from evidence used to test it. If we choose v to make the model agree with two endpoints, agreement at those same endpoints is built in. An additional observation at another time supplies a new comparison. Unit 2 will examine how measurement limitations affect such comparisons.

Activity 1.12 · What have two endpoints established?

Two stipulated observations give x = −1 m at 2 s and x = +5 m at 8 s. Find a constant rate that connects them, and predict the position at 5 s. A further stipulated observation gives +1 m at 5 s. Can the constant-rate model match all three values exactly? Does the discrepancy by itself identify its cause?

Discussion of Activity 1.12

The change is 6 m in 6 s, giving +1 m/s. At 5 s, three seconds have elapsed since the starting event, so the prediction is −1 + 1 × 3 = +2 m. This differs from +1 m, so one constant rate cannot match all three stipulated values exactly. For real measurements we would examine both the motion assumption and the measurement process before deciding why. The discrepancy alone does not identify a cause.

1.11 Giving the calculation to Python

We have deliberately done enough work by hand to know what our first program should mean and what it should return. Python will now evaluate the same model. Its immediate value is not that multiplying 0.75 by 8 is difficult. It is that a written calculation can be inspected, repeated and varied without reconstructing it from memory.

Download the Lesson 2 notebook and save a working copy in your P101 folder. The plain Python companion contains the same runnable examples for a Python editor. All required explanations and discussions are also here; the notebook tasks below form the lesson’s one-hour programming allocation.

First enter a numeric expression in a code cell. Python uses * for multiplication, ordinary - for subtraction and parentheses to group an operation. Do not type metre or second symbols into the expression: for this calculation we have agreed to use numerical values in metres and seconds.

Example 1.9 · The model as an expression

-2.0 + 0.75 * (20.0 - 12.0)
Reading the calculation

The parentheses make the time subtraction happen first. Multiplication then produces the numerical value of the change in metres, and addition includes the initial position. Running this expression in a notebook cell displays 4.0. Interpret that as +4 m in our stated coordinate system. Python has returned a number; we supply its physical meaning.

A value such as 4.0 is a normal floating-point result. The decimal point does not imply an extra measurement digit or improved physical precision. We will develop numerical types and representation more carefully in Lesson 3; here the important distinction is between a machine’s numerical output and our justified physical statement.

Activity 1.13 · Parentheses carry meaning

Predict the results of the following two expressions before running either. Then run them in separate code cells and explain why only the first implements Example 1.7.

-2.0 + 0.75 * (20.0 - 12.0)
-2.0 + 0.75 * 20.0 - 12.0
Discussion of Activity 1.13

The first gives 4.0; the second gives 1.0, because multiplication is carried out before the later subtraction: −2 + 15 − 12 = 1. In the second expression, the initial clock reading is subtracted as a bare number after the multiplication. That is not the physical operation we intended. Python accepts the arithmetic because ordinary numbers have no attached unit constraints.

We can make the inputs easier to inspect by assigning names to their numerical values. In Python, an assignment such as x0_m = -2.0 evaluates the expression on the right and associates its value with the name on the left. It does not state a physical law. The suffix _m is our reminder of metres, not a unit system built into Python.

x0_m = -2.0
v_m_per_s = 0.75
t0_s = 12.0
t_s = 20.0
elapsed_s = t_s - t0_s
x_m = x0_m + v_m_per_s * elapsed_s
x_m

Run these lines together in one code cell. Python works through them from top to bottom. The first four lines supply the initial position, rate and two clock readings. The next two calculate elapsed time and position. The final line asks the notebook to display the result. Assignment lines do not themselves display a result. In a plain script, use print(x_m) instead of the final line; print writes the value to the output.

Keep the names exactly as written when reproducing this example. x0_m and x_m have different jobs; replacing one with the other can lose the initial condition. If a name has not been assigned before it is used, Python reports a NameError. Check the spelling and run from the beginning rather than inventing an unexplained value to silence the error.

1.12 Checking the program and the model

A successful run tells us that Python could execute the instructions. A match with one hand calculation gives stronger evidence that we translated that example correctly. We should still test cases chosen for a reason. The starting event and zero rate are especially useful because we know the answer without another long computation.

Example 1.10 · A check with a known answer

Keep x0_m = -2.0, v_m_per_s = 0.75 and t0_s = 12.0, but set t_s = 12.0. Before running the whole cell again, predict the result.

Solution

The elapsed time is zero, so the change is zero. The output must be -2.0. This checks that the program returns the specified initial position at the specified initial time. It does not test the real trolley’s rate.

Activity 1.14 · Three checks and an interpretation

Use a fresh copy of the named-value cell for each case. Write the predicted result first, then run the cell and record the output with its unit and a one-sentence interpretation.

  1. The starting-event case from Example 1.10.
  2. x0 = −2 m, v = 0, t0 = 12 s, t = 20 s.
  3. The negative-rate case from Activity 1.10: +5 m, −0.50 m/s, 4 s and 10 s.
Discussion of Activity 1.14

The outputs should be (a) -2.0, the initial position with no elapsed time; (b) -2.0, unchanged position when no motion is assumed; (c) 2.0, still right of the origin after a change towards negative x. All positions are in metres. If a case fails, compare its four inputs and each calculation line with the physical statement. Do not alter an expected answer just to agree with the program.

Changing a line on screen is not the same as executing it. Likewise, assigning a new input after calculating x_m does not automatically recompute that earlier result. Run the complete cell again after each change. Lesson 3 will examine stored values and execution order in more depth; today, keep the inputs and their calculation together.

Activity 1.15 · Make a calculation someone else can inspect

For a new stipulated motion, a mark is at +1.5 m at 30 s and has constant signed rate −0.25 m/s. Predict its position at 38 s by hand. Write your own named-value cell, then check the starting-event and zero-rate cases. In a text cell, state the origin/direction convention you chose, the unit of every input, and one physical limitation of the prediction. Restart the kernel, run the notebook from the top, and save it.

Discussion of Activity 1.15

The duration is 8 s and the change is −2 m, giving −0.5 m. One appropriate convention is a fixed laboratory marker as origin, positive to the right. The program uses x0_m = 1.5, v_m_per_s = -0.25, t0_s = 30.0 and t_s = 38.0. The same two calculation lines produce -0.5. Setting the final time to 30 s gives +1.5 m; restoring 38 s and setting the rate to zero also gives +1.5 m. A suitable limitation is that the rate must remain constant throughout the interval, with no intervening end stop. A complete response contains the reasoning and interpretation as well as runnable code. Different sensible reference descriptions are acceptable if they agree with the signs used.

Our first model now exists in three connected forms: a physical statement, an equation and an executable calculation. The equation exposes the assumptions; the code makes a particular evaluation repeatable; the interpretation tells us what the output means. None can stand in for the other two. In Lesson 3 we will make the calculation easier to read, change and recover, paying particular attention to what a stored value does—and does not—remember.
