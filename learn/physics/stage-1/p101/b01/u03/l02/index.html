P101 · Block 1 · Unit 3 · Lesson 2

From a table to a labelled graph

A table keeps the observations separate. A graph lets us see them together. Moving from one to the other should change what is easy to notice, without quietly changing what we know.

In Lesson 1 we followed a marked point on an inspection carriage along a straight rail. We calculated changes between recorded positions, and found that the record did not determine everything that happened between readings. Here we will give that record a visual form, then build the same graph in Python. The hand construction comes first: it gives us something against which to check the computer’s result.

Allow four hours: about 60 minutes for reading and examples, 90 for the written activities, 60 for the notebook and 30 for feedback and corrections. Activity 3.12 is the notebook hour, not additional work. You will need the position and average-rate ideas from Lesson 1 and the basic Python and data-record habits developed in Units 1 and 2. NumPy arrays and Matplotlib are introduced here; no previous plotting-library experience is assumed.

Download the complete working folder (notebook, Python companion, CSV and metadata), or download the notebook, Python companion, data file and metadata separately. Keep the files together after extracting the ZIP.

3.7 What a point on the graph means

We will reuse the four pairs below. They are invented teaching observations, not readings from an experiment we have performed. The position is measured from the same fixed rail mark throughout, with rightwards positive; all times use one clock. The letters identify events, while the two numerical columns tell us where the tracked point was and when.

Record P101-U03-L01-A, carried forward unchanged. Each row is one paired observation.
EventClock reading / sPosition / m
A10−2
B144
C164
D191

A position–time graph puts time along the horizontal axis and position along the vertical axis. To plot A, find 10 s on the time scale, then move vertically to the level −2 m and place a point. The point combines both entries in its row. It does not say that the carriage has moved 10 units sideways and two units downwards: the two axes describe different quantities.

This last distinction matters because our physical rail is horizontal, whereas position increases upwards on the graph. A graph is a representation of a relationship, not necessarily a picture of the apparatus. In Figure 3.5 the rail snapshots use left and right to show physical position. The graph uses left and right to show earlier and later times. The event labels help you follow the same observation through the two representations.

Example 3.5 · Reading in both directions

Question. Locate B and D on the graph. What displacement occurred between them, and where does that information appear visually?

Working. B is at horizontal coordinate 14 s and vertical coordinate 4 m. D is at 19 s and 1 m. Read each quantity from its own axis before subtracting. The final position is lower on the graph, so we expect a negative change:

Δx=1m−4m=−3m

The vertical difference represents the displacement −3 m. The horizontal difference represents an elapsed time of 5 s. Neither difference is the slanting length of a line on the page. That page length would combine two different axis scales and would change if we stretched the drawing.

Interpretation. The marked point finishes 3 m to the left of its position at B. This is a net change. We have not yet specified whether it paused or reversed during the interval.

The axis label Position / m means that its tick numbers give position expressed in metres. Thus the tick marked 4 corresponds to 4 m. Similarly, Clock reading / s gives times expressed in seconds. Including the quantity and unit lets the graph travel beyond the paragraph in which it was introduced. An isolated label such as “x” or “time” would leave more for the reader to guess.

Key idea · Each plotted point preserves a pair

On a position–time graph, horizontal coordinate = time and vertical coordinate = position. Keep the two entries from each observation together. The graph’s shape is not the shape of the physical track.

Activity 3.7 · Translate without adding information

Allow 15 minutes. Draw a position–time graph of A–D on squared paper. Use one consistent scale on each axis, label quantities and units, and leave the points unjoined. Under it, sketch the rail and mark the positions of A–D. Explain why B and C coincide on the rail but occupy different points on the graph. Finally, correct the statement “D is above the time axis, so the carriage must be moving rightwards at D.”

Hint for Activity 3.7

Ask what the vertical coordinate tells you at one instant, and what you would need to know to describe a change. Label B and C separately even where their physical positions coincide.

Discussion of Activity 3.7

Your four graph points should have coordinates (10 s, −2 m), (14 s, 4 m), (16 s, 4 m) and (19 s, 1 m). The B–C horizontal spacing represents 2 s, compared with 4 s for A–B and 3 s for C–D. On the rail B and C both mark 4 m; the graph also distinguishes their times. D’s positive position says it lies to the right of the chosen origin. It does not establish its direction of motion at that instant. Even the negative average velocity from C to D describes an interval, not an instantaneous reading.

3.8 What belongs between the points?

It is tempting to join the points as soon as they appear. A line makes a pattern easier to follow, but it also occupies times at which the table contains no observation. We must decide what that line is intended to mean. It might be a visual guide, explicitly described as such, or it might represent an assumed history of the motion. A plotting command cannot make this scientific decision for us.

For example, a horizontal segment from B to C represents position 4 m throughout those two seconds. That is a model of rest. The two endpoint observations are also compatible with an excursion away from 4 m followed by a return. Figure 3.6 shows both possibilities. The extra point on the dotted alternative is part of that imagined history; it is not an additional observation.

Joining A to B by a straight segment likewise supplies information: equal time increments along that segment correspond to equal position increments. It represents constant velocity over that interval. The samples establish the interval’s average velocity, but constant velocity requires the additional assumption. The direct, steady travelling stages and middle rest used in a stipulated journey in Lesson 1 are one possible model for the whole record; they are not recovered from the data merely by drawing straight lines.

Example 3.6 · A slope has units

Question. Find the average velocity from C to D. Would making the graph twice as tall change the answer?

Working. C is (16 s, 4 m) and D is (19 s, 1 m). The position change is −3 m, and the elapsed time is 3 s. The average velocity is

vav=−3m3s=−1m/s

The ratio is the vertical-coordinate change divided by the horizontal-coordinate change: the gradient of the straight segment joining these two points. Here its unit is metres per second. We divide changes in the labelled quantities, not centimetres measured with a ruler on the display.

Interpretation. Stretching the graph makes the drawn segment look steeper, but the endpoint values, their differences and the average velocity remain unchanged. The negative sign means a net change towards decreasing position. It does not mean that the rail slopes downhill, and the magnitude 1 m/s is not evidence of a constant speed throughout the interval.

A useful graph does not have to begin at zero on every axis. Our time record begins at 10 s, so showing the neighbourhood of 10–19 s gives the points room. The tick labels must make that choice clear. The position scale must include the negative reading; cropping it away would hide an observation. For the ordinary linear axes used here, equal numerical increments occupy equal distances along each individual axis. The spacing of events therefore follows their clock readings, not their order in the table.

Activity 3.8 · Diagnose the claim

Allow 20 minutes. A report places A, B, C and D at equally spaced horizontal locations labelled “reading 1” to “reading 4”, joins them and calls the result a position–time graph. Explain the problem. Calculate the A–B and B–C average velocities from the record. Then sketch two different position histories between B and C, both consistent with the recorded endpoints, and say what you can conclude about travelled distance over that interval.

Hint for Activity 3.8

The actual time gaps are unequal. For the second sketch you may choose a new, explicitly imagined position at 15 s. A zero displacement need not mean zero distance.

Discussion of Activity 3.8

A reading-number graph can display order, but its horizontal increments are not seconds. Equal spacing conceals the 4 s, 2 s and 3 s gaps, so its visual gradients cannot be read as velocities in metres per second. A–B changes by +6 m in 4 s, giving +1.5 m/s. B–C changes by 0 m in 2 s, giving 0 m/s. Rest at 4 m is one possible history. Another goes from 4 m to 5 m at 15 s and back to 4 m at 16 s, travelling 2 m if both stages are direct. The exact endpoints establish zero displacement, and zero is the minimum possible distance. They do not establish the actual distance or a finite upper bound without further physical constraints. Our simple sketches need not describe realistic behaviour at their sharp corners; they demonstrate missing information, not a detailed mechanical model.

A wider view · A luminous trace is not a time axis

Optional, about two minutes within the reading allowance. Don Pettit assembled this view from multiple 30-second exposures taken with a camera mounted on the station. The luminous arcs make change during the exposures visible, but they are not position–time curves of the kind we have just constructed.

To obtain a position–time record from images, we would need to identify the tracked feature, define a coordinate and scale, and associate readings with times. The photograph is a reminder to ask how a visual representation was made before interpreting its curves. No astronomy calculation is needed here.

3.9 Give the file a meaning

We can now ask a computer to reproduce our graph. The first step is less glamorous than drawing: preserve the paired values in a form that another calculation can read. A CSV file is a plain-text table whose fields are separated by commas. In this lesson each line after the header represents one observation, and each line contains exactly two numeric fields.

time_s,position_m
10,-2
14,4
16,4
19,1

The first line is a header. It names the two columns and records their units using the suffixes _s and _m. These names are a convention we have chosen; a CSV reader does not automatically know that “s” means seconds. The minus sign in -2 belongs to the number in the position field. There is no unit text inside that field because we want to read it as a number.

A file of numbers still leaves questions unanswered. Where is the position origin? Which way is positive? Are these observations measured, simulated or invented? Does the clock restart between rows? The accompanying metadata supplies this meaning. It identifies the source record P101-U03-L01-A, states that these are invented teaching values, specifies a fixed rail origin and right-positive convention, and says that the same clock runs throughout. It also states that there are no missing entries in this small file and that no measurement uncertainties have been supplied.

The absence of uncertainties is not a claim of a perfect instrument. There is no physical instrument behind this invented example. The numerical values are stipulated so that we can check the representation exactly. An actual investigation would need the measurement procedure and limitations discussed in Unit 2. A plot of realistic data would not become precise simply because its markers were small.

Example 3.7 · Keep the record and its meaning together

Question. A colleague sends the line 14,4 and writes “this is the second observation”. What is missing, and what would make it usable here?

Working. The line alone does not identify which quantity comes first or either unit. A header restores that order: time_s,position_m. It then describes position 4 m at clock reading 14 s. To compare it with the other rows, we also need the common coordinate and clock conventions and the source of the values. Calling it the second observation establishes order, but does not supply elapsed time.

Interpretation. A useful record combines the numerical fields, their names and the metadata. None of these parts can repair an incorrectly transcribed value by itself. Checking the downloaded file against the displayed table gives us a separate check of the transfer.

Keep the supplied CSV unchanged. A derived column, such as elapsed time since A, belongs in a calculation or a separately named processed file. This preserves a route back to the original record. Do not replace an awkward value just to make a graph look smooth. If a different file contains a blank or invalid field, investigate and document it; the simple loading recipe below is deliberately designed for this complete numeric table.

The rows should be 10,−2; 14,4; 16,4; 19,1, with time first. 3.10 Read the table into an array

Python’s basic lists were enough for our earlier short records. NumPy provides an array: a container particularly suited to organised numerical data and calculations on whole collections of values. We will use a two-dimensional array for the table. Its first direction runs through rows and its second through columns. “Two-dimensional” here describes the arrangement of numbers in memory, not a claim that the carriage moves in two spatial dimensions.

Use a Python environment with NumPy and Matplotlib installed; many scientific notebook environments already include them. Extract the working folder and open the notebook there, or run the companion from that folder. The bare filename below is resolved relative to the Python session’s working directory, the folder from which it looks for files. In a hosted notebook, upload the CSV alongside the notebook. If Python reports FileNotFoundError, check that folder and the exact filename; changing the numbers will not fix a file-location problem. If an import reports ModuleNotFoundError, the named package needs installing in that environment; the official installation links at the end explain the options.

import numpy as np

data = np.loadtxt(
    "p101-u03-l02.csv",
    delimiter=",",
    skiprows=1,
    ndmin=2,
)
print("Array shape (rows, columns):", data.shape)
print(data)

The first line imports NumPy and gives it the short name np. The as clause is an alias, so subsequent calls can use np rather than the full package name. The dot in np.loadtxt selects NumPy’s text-loading function. Its first argument names our data file. The remaining named arguments explain how to read it: delimiter="," separates fields at commas; skiprows=1 skips the header line; and ndmin=2 keeps the result arranged in at least two dimensions, including when a suitable file contains only one data row.

For this file the result printed by data.shape is (4, 2): four rows and two columns. The printed array contains 10., -2. and so on. These are floating-point representations of our input numbers. The added decimal point reflects the numerical storage type; it does not add measurement precision. Reading the file successfully only establishes that the loader accepted its contents. It does not establish that the columns have the intended meaning or that the values match our source.

To inspect one entry, use square brackets with a row index followed by a column index. Python starts counting at zero, so the first row is row 0 and the second column is column 1. The expression data[0, 1] therefore selects −2, the first recorded position. Counting from one would pick a different entry without necessarily producing an error.

Example 3.8 · Check a pair before plotting a column

Question. Which entries select C’s clock reading and position? How can we check that we have not confused rows and columns?

Working. C is the third data row, so its row index is 2. Time is the first column, index 0; position is the second, index 1. Thus data[2, 0] gives 16 and data[2, 1] gives 4. Compare both values with the original row, including units. The header is not row 0 of this array: it was skipped during loading.

Interpretation. Selecting one familiar pair checks our understanding of the layout. It is stronger than merely observing that the array contains a 4 somewhere, because B also has position 4 m.

time_s = data[:, 0]
position_m = data[:, 1]
print("First position / m:", data[0, 1])
print("C clock reading / s:", data[2, 0])
elapsed_s = time_s - time_s[0]

In data[:, 0], the colon means “all rows”; the 0 selects the time column. The result is a one-dimensional array of four times, assigned the descriptive name time_s. The next line extracts all positions in the same row order. The first time remains paired with the first position, and so on. Sorting either array on its own would destroy those pairs.

The final line introduces a useful array calculation. time_s[0] is the first clock reading, 10 s. Subtracting that one number from the array subtracts it from every entry, producing elapsed times [0., 4., 6., 9.]. The new name elapsed_s keeps the distinction visible. This subtraction creates a derived array; the original time_s is unchanged. That distinction would be lost if we deliberately overwrote the original variable.

Activity 3.10 · Predict the selection

Allow 15 minutes of written work before running the notebook. Give the meaning, value and unit of data[1, 0] and data[3, 1]. Write the four values selected by data[:, 1]. Predict the C–D elapsed-time difference using both time_s and elapsed_s. Explain why the answers should agree, and why sorting the positions from smallest to largest would spoil this record if times were left unchanged.

Hint for Activity 3.10

Annotate the table with row indices 0–3 and column indices 0–1. The same constant is subtracted from both endpoints when we change the time origin.

Discussion of Activity 3.10

data[1, 0] is B’s clock reading, 14 s. data[3, 1] is D’s position, 1 m. The position column is −2, 4, 4, 1 m in that order. C–D is 19 − 16 = 3 s using clock readings and 9 − 6 = 3 s using elapsed times. A shared origin shift cancels in a difference. Independently sorting positions would produce −2, 1, 4, 4; it would wrongly pair 1 m with 14 s and 4 m with 19 s. Plausible-looking arrays can therefore describe the wrong experiment.

3.11 Make a graph that explains itself

Matplotlib is a plotting library. We will give it the two arrays and choose how to show them. Run the loading and column-selection blocks first, in the same session, then the block below. This uses the recorded clock readings; the elapsed-time version is a later check.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4), layout="constrained")
ax.plot(time_s, position_m, marker="o", linestyle="none")
ax.set_xlabel("Clock reading / s")
ax.set_ylabel("Position / m")
ax.set_title("Invented carriage record P101-U03-L01-A")
ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
fig.savefig("p101-u03-l02-clock.png", dpi=160)
plt.show()

The import supplies the plotting interface under the conventional short name plt. The call to plt.subplots creates two related objects. fig represents the whole figure; ax represents the plotting area, which Matplotlib calls an Axes even when we make just one. The two names on the left receive these two returned objects in order. The figsize values set a width of 6 inches and height of 4 inches for the figure, not the ranges of time and position. layout="constrained" asks Matplotlib to reserve space for labels.

In ax.plot(time_s, position_m, ...), the first array supplies horizontal coordinates and the second supplies vertical coordinates. This is worth reading aloud: time horizontally, position vertically. marker="o" requests a circular marker at each pair, while linestyle="none" suppresses joining lines. Without that deliberate choice, a usual line plot could introduce a continuous-looking history we have not established.

The label commands name quantities and units. The title identifies the invented record; the grid provides a light reading aid, with alpha=0.3 making its lines partly transparent. Finally, the figure is saved to a PNG image before plt.show() displays it. The value dpi=160 sets image resolution in dots per inch; it changes the stored image’s pixel density, not the number or accuracy of the observations. The PNG is written to the working directory and is replaced if you run the same save command again. Give a revised plot a different filename if you want to preserve both versions.

Compare this result with your paper graph. B and C should share a height; the two-second gap between them should be narrower than the four-second A–B gap. A must be below zero position, D above it. Automatic tick placement need not put a tick exactly beneath every observation. A point at 19 s is still meaningful if the nearest labelled ticks are 18 s and 20 s. Different software versions may choose slightly different ticks or spacing without changing the plotted pairs.

The title and axis labels are necessary, but they do not carry the whole explanation. A caption should identify the record, say what the symbols or lines represent, and explain a relevant limitation. Figure 3.9’s caption does this. In a report, place your caption next to the exported figure rather than expecting a reader to infer its provenance from a filename.

Reporting rule · Check the graph against its source

Check at least one pair in each direction: locate a source row on the graph, then read a plotted point back to the record. Check all rows when the file is this small. Name quantities and units, preserve meaningful time spacing, and explain whether lines represent observations, guides or a model.

3.12 Reproduce, inspect and explain

We now have a chain from a documented record to arrays and then to a figure. Each link has a different check. Matching rows tests transcription and selection; matching plotted coordinates tests the representation; a caption tests whether we can state what the result means. Running without an error is useful, but it answers none of those questions on its own.

The notebook brings the three worked code blocks together, followed by spaces for your own checks. Keep predictions beside outputs. When a result surprises you, return to the smallest checkable part: one row, one selected entry or one axis. This makes a correction explainable and avoids changing several things at once until the picture happens to look familiar.

Discussion of Activity 3.12

The expected shape is (4, 2); A’s position is −2 m and C’s clock reading is 16 s. The elapsed-time plot places A–D at 0, 4, 6 and 9 s, with positions unchanged. A satisfactory report lets another reader recover the data, understand the coordinate and clock conventions, and reproduce your figure. It distinguishes the invented observations from assumptions, includes labels and a source-specific caption, and supports at least one statement with a hand calculation. For example: “Using P101-U03-L01-A, I plotted position from a fixed rail origin, right positive, against elapsed time since A. C and D are separated by 3 s and −3 m, giving average velocity −1 m/s. The marker-only plot does not establish steady motion between them.” Your own report should also describe the file and rerun checks you actually performed; do not claim a check simply because this discussion recommends it.

Bring the representations together

Use the remaining 10 minutes of written practice to compare your rail sketch, table, CSV and final graph. Write one sentence about what each makes easy to see and one about information it leaves to the accompanying explanation. Use the 30-minute feedback allowance to revisit activities where your first answer changed; record why it changed rather than copying the discussion.

The rail sketch locates positions in space; the table and file preserve paired values; the graph reveals their time pattern. The metadata and caption carry the conventions and limitations that numbers alone cannot supply. In Lesson 3 we will add a model’s predictions to this chain and compare them with observations. The distinction between a plotted observation and an assumed history will remain essential.
