P101 · Physics, space & scientific thinking · Block 1

Unit 3
Motion graphs and
reproducible data work

An inspection carriage sets off along a rail, pauses beside a joint, and returns to its starting point. At the end of the trip, its position is unchanged. Yet its wheels have turned, time has passed and stretches of rail have been crossed twice. What should we record if we want to describe that journey?

There is more than one useful answer. The final position tells us where to find the carriage. The length of its route tells us how far it travelled. A sequence of positions and times reveals more of what happened between departure and return. These descriptions belong to the same event, but they preserve different information. Learning to choose between them is the first task of this unit.

In Unit 1, a constant-rate model connected an initial position, an elapsed time and a predicted position. In Unit 2, we examined the measurement procedures that might supply numerical inputs. We now bring those strands together. A motion record will be both a set of quantities we can calculate with and a piece of evidence whose meaning depends on how it was obtained.

The distinction matters even when the arithmetic is simple. Two recorded positions determine a change over an interval. They do not tell us that the object moved steadily throughout that interval. If we fill the gap with a straight line or a formula, we have added a description of what might have happened. A central question for this unit is how to keep that model separate from the observations used to examine it.

Making a pattern visible

A small table is a good place to begin: each row can identify a time, a position and any relevant note. As the record grows, a graph helps us see changes and compare them. We will learn how to construct a position–time graph and read it as a relationship between two quantities. Its shape is not a drawing of the track. A line rising on the page may describe an object moving horizontally along a bench.

Next we will put predictions beside observations. Differences between them may reveal a weakness in a model, a measurement problem, or a question that needs further investigation. A close-looking plot alone cannot settle which explanation is right. We will calculate and interpret the differences, and ask where a prediction is supported by evidence and where it reaches beyond the record.

Python will help us organise this work. The opening lesson reuses scalar calculations and functions. Later lessons introduce small arrays, documented data files and labelled plots, explaining the new tools as they are needed. The aim is an analysis that another person can run and understand: its data source, units, choices and limitations should travel with its result.

By the end of Unit 3

You should be able to distinguish position, displacement, distance travelled and average rates; construct and interpret a labelled motion graph; compare a constant-rate model with a record; and explain a reproducible calculation without overstating what the evidence establishes.

Your route through the unit

  1. Position, displacement and average rates. Decide what a motion calculation means before drawing a graph.
  2. From a table to a labelled graph. Connect a documented record to arrays and a readable position–time plot.
  3. Comparing a model with observations. Examine predictions, differences and the limits of extrapolation.
  4. An analysis another person can rerun. Preserve sources and make processing, files and execution order explicit.
  5. Motion evidence and block synthesis. Complete an investigation, the cumulative unit exercises, the formative checkpoint and the unit reference.

The planned workload is 20 hours, including 6 hours of Python. The first lesson takes 4 hours, including this introduction and 1 hour of Python. Attempts, feedback and corrections belong within these hours. The unit’s closing work prepares for TMA 01, whose existing six-hour allocation is separate.

You need the signed-number arithmetic, unit conventions, lists and functions developed in Unit 1, together with Unit 2’s habits of keeping source records and qualifying a conclusion. No calculus, vector component methods or plotting package is assumed at the start. All opening calculations use explicitly invented records or idealised journeys. They can develop your reasoning without requiring apparatus or being mistaken for measurements you made.

We begin by choosing a reference and tracking a single point. That small decision will make the difference between a meaningful position and an unexplained number.
