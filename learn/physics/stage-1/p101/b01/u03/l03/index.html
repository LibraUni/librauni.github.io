Comparing a model with observations

A model earns its usefulness by making a definite prediction. A comparison becomes informative when we can say exactly where that prediction agrees with the record, where it disagrees, and what the difference allows us to conclude.

Lesson 2 turned four time–position pairs into a graph. We deliberately left the observed points unjoined because they did not specify a continuous history. Now we will draw a line with a different role: it will represent an explicitly stated constant-velocity model. We can then compare its predictions with the observations at the same times. The gaps between them will become a second graph, one designed to make disagreement easier to inspect.

Allow four hours: 60 minutes for explanation and examples, 90 for written activities, 60 for Python and 30 for feedback and corrections. Activity 3.18 is the Python hour. You will reuse Lesson 2’s arrays and plotting ideas and Lesson 1’s signed rates; no regression method or calculus is needed. Our numerical records remain invented teaching cases. They let us check reasoning and code, but they cannot establish how a real carriage behaves.

Download the working folder, containing the notebook, Python companion, CSV and metadata. Extract it and keep these files together. The CSV repeats the unchanged four pairs from Lessons 1–2.

3.13 Turn a rate into a prediction

Recall the invented record P101-U03-L01-A. At clock readings 10, 14, 16 and 19 s, the marked point has positions −2, 4, 4 and 1 m respectively. The rail origin is fixed, and rightwards is positive. Between the first two readings, A and B, position changes by +6 m in 4 s. The average velocity over that interval is therefore +1.5 m/s.

Suppose we ask what would happen if the carriage maintained that velocity. This is a new assumption, stronger than the average-rate calculation. A constant velocity of +1.5 m/s predicts an additional 1.5 m of displacement for each second that passes. Starting at −2 m when the clock reads 10 s, we add that predicted displacement to the starting position. The time to multiply by the velocity is the elapsed time since 10 s, not the clock reading itself.

We can express the reasoning for any chosen reference event. Let tref denote its clock reading, xref its position, and v the assumed constant velocity. The predicted position at clock reading t is

xpred(t)=xref+v(t−tref)

The subscript “pred” identifies a predicted value. The notation xpred(t) means “the model’s predicted position at time t”; the parentheses indicate an input to the prediction, not multiplication. The term t−tref is elapsed time from the reference event. Multiplying it by velocity gives a displacement, with units of metres, which can be added to the reference position.

Model M1 · Continue the A–B rate

Use reference time 10 s, reference position −2 m and constant velocity +1.5 m/s. The rate was calculated from A and B. Apply the same rule at C and D as a test of continuing it across the rest of the supplied record. The rule assumes one straight coordinate axis, an unchanged clock and velocity that does not change during the interval being modelled.

Example 3.9 · Predict before comparing

Question. What position does M1 predict at the time of C, 16 s?

Working. The elapsed time since A is 16 s − 10 s = 6 s. At the assumed velocity, the predicted displacement is +9 m. Add it to the reference position:

xpred(16s)=−2m+9m=7m

Interpretation. M1 predicts position 7 m. This is a prediction at the time of C; it is not a replacement for C’s recorded position of 4 m. Keeping both numbers is precisely what makes a comparison possible.

At A the model returns −2 m because the elapsed time is zero. At B it returns 4 m because we chose its velocity from A and B. Those agreements are useful checks of substitution and programming, but they are built into the construction. They are not independent evidence that the velocity remained constant, even between A and B. Another motion history could pass through both endpoints.

Discussion of Activity 3.13

At 12 s, 2 s have elapsed: predicted displacement is +3 m and position is 1 m. At 19 s, 9 s have elapsed: displacement is +13.5 m and position is 11.5 m. Multiplying by the full clock reading wrongly counts motion during the ten seconds before the reference event. It would even predict a nonzero displacement from A at A itself. A supplies the anchor; A and B supply the velocity. C and D are additional checks of that chosen rule, although the whole record is invented rather than experimental evidence.

3.14 Measure the disagreement

At C, the observation is 4 m and M1 predicts 7 m. The observation lies 3 m below the prediction on the position–time graph. To record both the size and direction of the disagreement, we subtract predicted position from observed position. This signed difference is called a residual. We will use the letter r:

r=xobs−xpred

The two positions must refer to the same time, origin, direction and units. The residual has the same unit as position: metres in this lesson. A positive residual means the observed position is more positive than the predicted position; a negative residual means it is less positive. “More positive” is safer than “farther from the origin”: for example, −1 m is more positive than −3 m, but closer to the origin.

Example 3.10 · Keep the sign of the gap

Question. Calculate the residual at C and compare it with a separate invented case whose observed position is −1 m and predicted position is −3 m.

Working. At C, subtract the 7 m prediction from the 4 m observation. In the separate case, subtract the whole negative prediction:

rC=4m−7m=−3m
r=−1m−(−3m)=+2m

Interpretation. C lies below M1’s line, giving a negative residual. The separate observation is 2 m more positive than its prediction, giving a positive residual even though both positions are negative. Taking an absolute value immediately would hide this distinction.

A residual is a disagreement with a chosen model, not automatically a measurement error. Measurement error concerns the difference between a measured value and the quantity’s true value, which is usually not exactly known. A model prediction is not guaranteed to be that true value. A nonzero residual could involve limitations of the model, the measuring procedure, the recorded data or the calculation. Its sign alone does not tell us which.

Nor is a residual a velocity. The residual −10.5 m at D says that D’s position is 10.5 m less positive than M1 predicts at 19 s. It does not say the carriage is moving at −10.5 m/s. We have compared two positions at one time; we have not divided a change of position by a time interval.

Rearrange the definition in words: observed = predicted + residual. A disagreement is a reason to inspect an observation and its assumptions, not evidence by itself that the observation is invalid.

Discussion of Activity 3.14

The separate observed position is −2 m + 0.6 m = −1.4 m: 0.6 m more positive than predicted. For D, 1 m − 11.5 m = −10.5 m. Calling this a true error assumes without evidence that the model gives the true position. Deleting D would also discard a supplied observation solely because it challenges the model. Preserve the raw record, check the transcription and calculation, and report the disagreement. In this invented case the given positions are stipulated exactly; there is no instrument whose error has been measured.

3.15 Read the pattern without inventing a cause

The upper panel of Figure 3.10 places M1 and the observed points on the same axes. The vertical gaps at the sample times are the residuals. The lower panel plots those gaps against time, with zero as a reference. It removes the model’s rising trend from the display, allowing the remaining disagreement to be inspected directly.

Read the panels together. At A and B the residuals are zero, as the construction requires. At C and D the residuals are negative, with a larger negative value at D. M1 continues increasing at the A–B rate while the recorded position first stays at the same sampled value and then decreases. The rule therefore fails to reproduce the complete stipulated record. We do not need a statistical test to see that exact disagreement in an invented example.

A real experiment requires an additional judgement: how large are the discrepancies compared with the measurement limitations and the accuracy needed for the task? A small nonzero residual may be unsurprising when readings have limited resolution or repeatability. A residual plot alone cannot supply those limits. Here no instrument uncertainty has been provided, so we will not attach a probability, call a difference “statistically significant”, or decide a numerical acceptance threshold.

Some patterns suggest useful questions. Residuals clustered around a positive value might make us check the position origin or the model’s reference position. Residuals that steadily become more positive might make us check the assumed rate, or whether a clock or scale is behaving as expected. Curvature suggests that one fixed rate may be missing a change across the interval. These are leads for investigation, not diagnoses established by a shape.

The three panels in Figure 3.11 are deliberately constructed residual sequences, not three experiments. They isolate shapes so that you can think about them without random scatter. In actual data a pattern may be weak, mixed with other effects, or produced by too few readings to judge confidently. Even residuals that look irregular and small do not prove the model: missing observations, inappropriate scales or limited coverage may conceal a failure.

Example 3.11 · What a changed reference can repair

Question. Suppose every predicted position is increased by 0.2 m, with observations unchanged. What happens to every residual? Could that change remove M1’s whole residual pattern?

Working. Each residual is observed minus predicted. Increasing the number subtracted by 0.2 m decreases the residual by 0.2 m. Thus residuals all equal to +0.2 m would become zero. M1’s residuals would instead become −0.2, −0.2, −3.2 and −10.7 m.

Interpretation. A fixed position shift moves the residual pattern vertically. It cannot remove the differences between its values. A reference-position adjustment alone cannot make M1 agree with all four exact observations. This is a limited, checkable conclusion; it does not identify the physical cause of the disagreement.

Hint for Activity 3.15

Separate a description of the plotted differences from an explanation of their cause. A numerical or recording mistake should be checked before a new physical mechanism is asserted.

Discussion of Activity 3.15

Only the constant +0.2 m sequence can be removed by adding 0.2 m to every prediction. A shared shift leaves the changes between residuals intact, so it cannot flatten the rising or curved sequences. For the carriage, “C and D lie below M1, with residuals −3 and −10.5 m” is directly supported. “The velocity may not have remained constant after B” is a possible explanation in a physical investigation; so are some failures of the measurement or recording process. A first check is to recompute the predictions and confirm the time/position pairs and units. If those checks passed in a real experiment, additional intermediate readings and a review of the measurement method would help investigate the departure. No forces have been measured here. The residuals cannot identify friction, and a constructed teaching record cannot provide experimental evidence for it.

3.16 Between the readings and beyond them

M1 gives a value at 12 s even though the record has no observation then. Using a model to estimate a value inside a stated interval covered by observations is called interpolation. Using it outside that interval is extrapolation. Always identify the interval: a word such as “inside” is incomplete until we know inside what.

For M1, there are two relevant ranges. Its parameters were chosen using A and B, spanning 10–14 s. The complete supplied record spans 10–19 s. Relative to A–B, the prediction at 12 s is interpolation and the prediction at 16 s is extrapolation. Relative to the complete record’s range, 16 s is inside the range—and it is an actual observation time at which we can check M1. These descriptions answer different questions; neither makes the prediction reliable by itself.

At 12 s the predicted position is 1 m. This may be a useful estimate if constant velocity between A and B is a justified assumption. But both a changing-velocity journey and the steady one could reach the same endpoints. Interpolation is not a guarantee: a change can occur in a gap between readings. Lesson 2’s imagined excursion between equal-position samples was an example of just such missing information.

At 22 s, outside the complete 10–19 s record, M1 predicts 16 m. The arithmetic is straightforward. The physical justification is weak: M1 already disagrees with C and D, and the record supplies no later observations. A smooth extension on the page cannot repair that loss of support. Extrapolation can also encounter new conditions—an end stop, a change of driving force or a different measurement range—that were absent earlier.

Example 3.12 · Matching the endpoints is not enough

Question. Choose a second constant-velocity model, M2, using A and D instead of A and B. Does matching the first and last observations make it agree with the whole record?

Working. Keep the anchor at A. The displacement A–D is +3 m over 9 s, giving

vM2=3m9s=13m/s

At C, 6 s after A, this rate predicts a displacement of 2 m and therefore position 0 m. The observed position is 4 m, so the residual is +4 m. At B, the prediction is −23m, giving residual +143m.

Interpretation. M2’s residuals are zero at A and D by construction, but positive at both interior observations. A correct whole-interval average velocity does not imply that the velocity was constant. Neither M1 nor M2 reproduces all four exact positions. We have compared two explicitly chosen rules; we have not searched for a best-fitting line.

Hint for Activity 3.16

Write the two intervals explicitly before using “interpolation” or “extrapolation”. B or C already provides a check that endpoint agreement alone overlooks.

Discussion of Activity 3.16

Relative to 10–14 s, 13 s is interpolation while 18 and 22 s are extrapolation. Relative to 10–19 s, 13 and 18 s are interpolation while 22 s is extrapolation. At 22 s, 12 s have elapsed since A, giving −2 m + (1.5 m/s)(12 s) = 16 m. M2’s endpoints do not determine the intervening history; its disagreement with B and C already shows that it does not reproduce the full record. At C, M1 predicts 7 m and M2 predicts 0 m, whereas the observed position is 4 m. Thus C is an additional observation relative to either model’s construction and distinguishes their predictions, while challenging both as exact descriptions. A new measurement at 15 s could give further information in a real investigation, but it must not be fabricated.

3.17 Compare arrays and draw both panels

We will now make the numerical comparison in Python. The data-loading lines repeat Lesson 2: skip the header, read two numeric columns and preserve their pairing. The new work is to evaluate a model at every observed time, then subtract arrays in the stated order. Predictions and residuals receive their own names; the observed positions are not overwritten.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

data = np.loadtxt(
    "p101-u03-l03.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1, ndmin=2
)
time_s = data[:, 0]
position_m = data[:, 1]
reference_time_s = time_s[0]
reference_position_m = position_m[0]
velocity_m_per_s = (
    (position_m[1] - reference_position_m)
    / (time_s[1] - reference_time_s)
)

The first two entries are A and B. Index 0 selects A, and index 1 selects B. Subtracting the reference values in the velocity calculation therefore gives the A–B differences. The resulting number is 1.5, in metres per second. These variable names document the numerical unit convention; Python does not check the units for us.

The function below expresses the model once. Its four arguments supply the requested times, the reference time, the reference position and the velocity. With a single time it returns one prediction. With a NumPy array of times, the subtraction and multiplication operate on every entry. This is the same array arithmetic introduced for elapsed time in Lesson 2, now with an added multiplication and position offset.

def predict_position(times_s, t_ref_s, x_ref_m, velocity_m_per_s):
    return x_ref_m + velocity_m_per_s * (times_s - t_ref_s)

predicted_m = predict_position(
    time_s, reference_time_s, reference_position_m, velocity_m_per_s
)
residual_m = position_m - predicted_m
print("M1 predicted position / m:", predicted_m)
print("M1 residual (observed - predicted) / m:", residual_m)

The prediction array should be −2, 4, 7 and 11.5 m, and the residual array 0, 0, −3 and −10.5 m. Subtraction works entry by entry: first observation minus first prediction, second minus second, and so forth. Arrays of the same length can still be wrongly paired if one was reordered or calculated for different times. Checking C by hand is therefore more informative than checking only that the arrays have four entries.

For the picture, we need two sets of prediction values with different jobs. predicted_m contains predictions at the four observation times and is used for the residual calculation. line_position_m below contains values at a closely spaced set of times used only to draw the model line. These extra times are drawing coordinates, not new observations. For this straight line, two endpoint predictions would suffice; using a regular grid keeps the drawing values explicitly separate from the observation times. It does not strengthen the evidence.

line_time_s = np.linspace(time_s[0], time_s[-1], 100)
line_position_m = predict_position(
    line_time_s, reference_time_s, reference_position_m, velocity_m_per_s
)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 1, sharex=True, figsize=(7, 7), layout="constrained")
position_ax = axes[0]
residual_ax = axes[1]
position_ax.plot(time_s, position_m, "o", label="Invented observations")
position_ax.plot(line_time_s, line_position_m, "--", label="M1: A-B rate")
position_ax.set_ylabel("Position / m")
position_ax.set_title("Invented record P101-U03-L01-A")
position_ax.legend()
position_ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
residual_ax.plot(time_s, residual_m, "o")
residual_ax.axhline(0, color="grey", linewidth=1)
residual_ax.set_ylabel("Residual: observed - predicted / m")
residual_ax.set_xlabel("Clock reading / s")
residual_ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
fig.savefig("p101-u03-l03-m1.png", dpi=160)
plt.show()

np.linspace creates 100 evenly spaced times from the first to the last observation, including both endpoints. Index -1 means the last array entry. We pass these times through the same prediction function, so the line and the four numerical predictions describe the same model.

The arguments 2, 1 to plt.subplots request two rows and one column of plotting areas. This time the second returned object, axes, is an array containing two Axes. We give its entries the names position_ax and residual_ax. The option sharex=True makes their horizontal scales agree, so a given time lines up vertically between panels. Each panel still needs its own vertical label: position and residual have the same unit but are different quantities.

The observed positions use circles without joining lines. The dashed model line has a label that identifies its origin; legend() displays those labels. The residual panel likewise shows only sampled differences. axhline(0, ...) draws a horizontal reference at zero residual, with grey colour and a line width of 1. It is a reference, not an uncertainty band or acceptance boundary. Saving and showing the figure work as in Lesson 2.

(xobs+2m)−(xpred+2m)=xobs−xpred

3.18 Make a defensible comparison

A successful calculation should leave a reader able to distinguish three things: the supplied values, the chosen model and the conclusion drawn from comparing them. The notebook keeps those parts separate. Its worked cells reproduce M1; your task is to check them, then compare M2 without rewriting the raw data or quietly replacing the first result.

Changing a model after inspecting disagreement is a legitimate part of modelling, provided the change is recorded. But the revised agreement has a different evidential status from a successful prediction made before a new observation. Here both rules use selected points from an invented record. The exercise establishes whether we implemented and interpreted those rules consistently. Experimental support would require suitable real observations, their measurement limitations, and a clear account of how the model was chosen.

Discussion of Activity 3.18

M1 predicts −2, 4, 7 and 11.5 m, with residuals 0, 0, −3 and −10.5 m. The following code computes M2 without overwriting those arrays:

velocity_m2 = (
    (position_m[-1] - reference_position_m)
    / (time_s[-1] - reference_time_s)
)
predicted_m2 = predict_position(
    time_s, reference_time_s, reference_position_m, velocity_m2
)
residual_m2 = position_m - predicted_m2
line_position_m2 = predict_position(
    line_time_s, reference_time_s, reference_position_m, velocity_m2
)
print("M2 predicted position / m:", predicted_m2)
print("M2 residual / m:", residual_m2)

M2’s predictions are −2, approximately −0.6667, 0 and 1 m; its residuals are 0, approximately 4.6667, 4 and 0 m. The repeating decimal comes from one-third; retain the calculation’s precision and round only when reporting. A very small numerical remainder near a theoretical zero can arise from floating-point arithmetic and is not new physical evidence.

For the second figure, reuse the plotting block with line_position_m2 in the model-line call and residual_m2 in the residual call. The reference-time and zero-velocity checks both return the reference position, −2 m; their purpose is to test the formula’s behaviour.

A defensible conclusion says that neither chosen constant-velocity rule reproduces all four exact supplied positions. It identifies which points set each rule and which further points challenge it. A suitable report might say: “For invented record P101-U03-L01-A, M1 uses the A–B average velocity, +1.5 m/s, anchored at A. At C it predicts 7 m against the stipulated 4 m, giving residual −3 m. M2 uses the A–D average and also disagrees with the interior readings. These checks expose the limits of the chosen constant-velocity descriptions; they neither identify a force nor experimentally validate a motion law.” Add your own figure caption, conventions and checks actually performed.

From disagreement to a better question

Use the remaining 10 minutes of written practice to write a short response to “The graph looks convincing, so the model is correct.” Refer to an endpoint used to choose a rule, an additional observation, the residual’s sign and the observation interval. During the 30-minute feedback allowance, revisit changed answers and record the reasoning behind each correction.

The useful outcome is a more precise claim. A model predicts under stated assumptions; residuals expose what it leaves unexplained in a particular record; the observation range limits where the comparison has been made. None of these steps is helped by hiding awkward points or mistaking a successful calculation for a successful experiment. Lesson 4 will make the whole analysis easier for another person to rerun, including the files, environment and decisions on which it depends.
