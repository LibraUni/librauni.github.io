P101 · Physics, space & scientific thinking

Finding out how the universe works

A shadow moves across a wall. A bicycle slows when you stop pedalling. A point of light changes its position among the stars. Each observation can become a question: what is changing, what might account for the change, and how could we find out whether our explanation is any good?

Physics gives us ways of pursuing questions like these. It brings together close observation, mathematical descriptions and experiments designed to distinguish between possibilities. Sometimes an explanation makes a strikingly accurate prediction. Sometimes a discrepancy is the most interesting part of the result. In either case, we need to know what was measured, what was assumed and how the calculation connects the two.

This module is an introduction to that way of working. You will study motion, forces and energy, then waves and light, before exploring fields, circuits and evidence about atoms. These are substantial subjects, and this first encounter will leave much to develop later. The aim is to build a connected foundation: enough physical understanding to ask useful questions, choose a simple model, work out what it predicts and discuss its limitations.

A description we can put to the test

Imagine watching a small trolley travel along a straight track. You could describe it as moving steadily, but the word “steadily” leaves room for disagreement. Does it cover similar distances in equal times? Does it slow a little near one end? To make the question more precise, choose a point on the trolley, mark positions along the track and record when that point passes them.

You now have observations that another person can interpret. A possible model is that the trolley advances by the same distance in every second. That assumption allows you to predict positions between your observations, and perhaps beyond them. The prediction is useful precisely because it can turn out to be wrong. A comparison with further observations might support the model over part of the track, reveal a systematic slowing, or expose a problem with the timing method.

A model is a selective representation of a situation. Treating a trolley as a point leaves out its size and rotating wheels; treating its rate of advance as constant leaves out changes in that rate. Whether those simplifications are sensible depends on the question. If you want the approximate time to cross a short stretch of track, a simple description may be enough. If you want to understand how a wheel slips, it is missing something essential.

Throughout P101 we will distinguish a calculation performed correctly from a model that describes the world adequately. A program may reproduce a formula perfectly while the formula rests on an unsuitable assumption. Conversely, disagreement does not automatically show that a physical principle is wrong: a mistaken unit, a poorly chosen reference point or an instrument problem may be responsible. Learning to investigate those possibilities is part of learning physics.

The route through P101

Block 1, Measure, model and compute, establishes our working methods. We begin with quantities, units and physical scale, then build a small model that can be evaluated by hand and in Python. Measurement brings a different set of questions: how was a reading obtained, why do repeated readings vary, and how many digits are justified? Motion tables and graphs bring these strands together in an analysis that another person can rerun and understand.

Block 2, Motion, forces and conservation, asks why motion changes and how interactions can be described. Diagrams help us identify the system and the forces acting on it. Momentum and energy offer further ways to organise a problem, often making a complicated sequence easier to reason about. We will also distinguish temperature, heating and internal energy, taking care over what a simple model does and does not describe.

Block 3, Oscillations, waves and light, moves from individual motions to repeating patterns and travelling disturbances. You will connect a graph with an oscillation, investigate how waves combine, and study how light changes direction and carries information. A computer-generated signal will also let us examine a practical trap: the samples we record may give a misleading picture of what is changing between them.

Block 4, Fields, circuits and microscopic evidence, extends the argument into situations where the explanation is less directly visible. Fields describe how interactions depend on position; circuits connect charge flow with potential differences and energy transfer. Atomic spectra provide a first encounter with evidence that familiar continuous models cannot explain. The emphasis remains on carefully bounded models, their predictions and the evidence used to assess them.

Working with mathematics and computation

M101 develops the mathematical methods alongside this module. Its vectors will help you describe directions and resolve forces; differentiation and integration will give more precise ways of describing change and accumulation. Here we will develop the physical questions and explain why a method applies. Both modules have their own narrative, so there is no need to keep their lessons in step. When a calculation needs a method you already know, use it; when you need a reminder, the cross-references provide a route back.

Python becomes a working tool during Unit 1. It lets us repeat calculations, inspect data and explore the consequences of changing an assumption. The early teaching introduces programming rather than assuming it. If these operations are familiar, concentrate on the physical choices: what the variables mean, which units they use, what a check establishes and where the model stops being credible. Familiar syntax should give you more attention for the physics.

Keep a notebook in both senses: paper for sketches and reasoning, and a saved computational notebook for calculations. Before running code, predict a simple result. After running it, explain what the output means and compare it with a separate hand calculation. Saving a result is useful; saving enough explanation and input information to reconstruct it is much more useful.

Reading, trying and returning

Worked examples show the choices behind a calculation as well as its steps. Pause before opening an activity’s discussion, and retain your first attempt long enough to locate the point where your reasoning differs. An incorrect prediction can be informative when you can explain why you made it and what changes your mind.

Green boxes collect important results that the surrounding text has developed. Sepia boxes contain worked examples. Blue boxes offer optional context; they do not hide required prerequisites. Numbered activities belong within the teaching, while each completed unit ends with a substantial exercise set, a short conclusion and reference resources. You can spread a lesson across several sittings without losing its place in the argument.

The module’s 300-hour planning budget includes practical work, practice, three tutor-marked assignments, revision and an end-of-module assessment. A short calculation may deserve more thought than its length suggests. The aim is not to collect pages read, but to become increasingly able to give a clear account of a physical situation and defend the choices in your explanation.

We begin with a small but powerful question: when someone gives you a number about the physical world, what else do you need to know before you can use it?
