P101 · Unit 11 · Lesson 2

Work accumulated along a path

A field tells us how force changes from place to place. To calculate the work during a journey, we must let that changing force contribute over each part of the displacement.

Allow four hours: 95 minutes reading and worked examples, 100 minutes of activities and diagrams, and 45 minutes for feedback and correction. Retrieve the definite-integral methods of M101 Units 8–9 as needed.

11.5 From a small displacement to a total

For constant force, Unit 6 gave W=F·s. Along an x-axis this becomes FxΔx. Suppose instead that a trolley is pulled by a force whose horizontal component grows with position. Its force at the starting point describes only the beginning of the journey. Multiplying that value by the entire displacement misses the larger contributions later on.

Divide a journey from x=a to x=b into short intervals. On one interval, use a representative force component Fx(xi) and multiply by its signed width Δxi. This gives an approximate contribution ΔWi≈Fx(xi)Δxi. Add all the contributions:

W≈∑iFx(xi)Δxi.

The product has units Nm, hence joules. If the force is continuous on the interval, refining the partition leads to the definite integral

Wa→b=∫abFx(x)dx.

The component is measured along the chosen positive axis; the bounds specify the actual starting and finishing coordinates. For increasing x, a positive component makes positive work and a negative component makes negative work. Reversing the journey reverses the integration bounds and the work of the same position-dependent force. A resistance force that reverses with the velocity would require a new signed force model for the return journey.

The shape of a force–position graph helps us interpret the sum. Each rectangle has signed height Fx and width Δx. For increasing bounds, regions above the axis contribute positive work and regions below contribute negative work. The integral gives their algebraic total. The force need not be zero when that total vanishes.

Example 11.5 · A graph gives an independent benchmark

An applied horizontal force has component Fx(x)=αx on 0≤x≤2.0m, where α=3.0Nm−1. Find its work for motion from 0 to 2.0 m.

The graph is a straight line from 0 N to 6.0 N. Its triangular area is 12(2.0m)(6.0N)=6.0J. Integration gives the same result:

W=∫02.0mαxdx=[αx22]02.0m=6.0J.

The average force over this displacement is 3.0 N. Using the initial force alone gives zero work; using the final force alone gives 12 J. The graph explains why both are poor whole-interval approximations, and why the mean of the endpoint forces works for this linear variation.

Activity 11.5 · Build the sum by hand (20 minutes)

For Example 11.5, divide the interval into four equal pieces. Use the force at each left endpoint to calculate four work contributions and their total. Repeat using right endpoints. Place the exact work between your estimates, and explain the ordering from the graph. What factor would be missing if you merely added the force readings?

Discussion of Activity 11.5

The width is 0.50 m. Left-endpoint forces are 0, 1.5, 3.0 and 4.5 N, giving contributions 0, 0.75, 1.50 and 2.25 J, total 4.50 J. Right-endpoint forces are 1.5, 3.0, 4.5 and 6.0 N, total work 7.50 J. Increasing force makes left rectangles underestimate and right rectangles overestimate the area. The missing factor in a sum of forces is the width; without it the result still has units of force.

11.6 Signed work and changing kinetic energy

Once the physical contribution and bounds are established, the mathematics follows the methods of M101. Find an antiderivative, evaluate it at the finishing coordinate and subtract its complete value at the starting coordinate. Keep the dimensions of coefficients visible: a term proportional to x needs a coefficient in N/m if the result is a force.

Example 11.6 · Positive and negative contributions

A particle experiences a specified force component Fx=F0−βx, with F0=4.0N and β=2.0Nm−1. Calculate this force’s work from x=0 to x=3.0m.

The force changes sign at x=F0/β=2.0m. It assists the increasing-x motion before that point and opposes it afterwards. Integrate the signed component:

W=[F0x−βx22]03.0m=12.0J−9.0J=3.0J.

Geometrically, the positive triangle contributes 4.0 J and the negative triangle −1.0 J. Adding their unsigned areas would give 5.0 J and lose the energy-transfer meaning. The positive total says that this force contributes a net gain of kinetic energy; the speed need not increase throughout the journey.

To infer a kinetic-energy change, add the work of all forces on the particle. The work–kinetic-energy theorem remains ΔK=Wnet. For constant mass moving along one axis, its calculus connection is useful: Fnet,x=mdv/dt, while dx=vdt. Thus Fnet,xdx=mvdv=d(12mv2). Accumulating gives the endpoint kinetic-energy change. This uses signed velocity in the intermediate relation and gives speed squared in the final energy.

If the force in Example 11.6 is the resultant, a 1.0 kg particle starting at speed 2.0 m/s has initial kinetic energy 2.0 J and final kinetic energy 5.0 J. Its final speed is 2(5.0)/1.0=10ms−1≈3.16ms−1. If another force also acts, its work must be included before making that prediction.

Activity 11.6 · A zero total with nonzero force (30 minutes)

Extend Example 11.6’s force model to x=4.0m. Find its work from 0 to 4.0 m and from 4.0 m back to 0. For a particle moving from 0 in the positive direction under this resultant force, initially with speed 2.0 m/s and mass 1.0 kg, find its speed at 2.0 m and 4.0 m. Describe the change of speed along the way. Finally, explain why replacing the signed force by its magnitude gives the wrong endpoint prediction.

Discussion of Activity 11.6

The antiderivative gives 16−16=0 J from 0 to 4.0 m; reversing bounds also gives zero. At 2.0 m the accumulated work is 4.0 J, so kinetic energy is 6.0 J and speed 12≈3.46ms−1. At 4.0 m the speed returns to 2.0 m/s. It increases to 2.0 m, then decreases. The signed work is positive then negative. Using |Fx| would count the opposing part as another energy gain and incorrectly predict 8.0 J of net work.

11.7 Radial work and the domain of a model

For motion directly towards or away from a fixed, spherically symmetric source, use the radial coordinate r, increasing outwards. Gravity’s radial force on a probe of mass m is Fr=−GMm/r2. An outward displacement has dr>0, so gravity does negative work. An inward displacement has the opposite sign. This directional check is available before any integration.

Let the journey start at a and finish at b, with both radii outside the source. The power rule gives an antiderivative of r−2 as −r−1. Consequently

Wg=∫ab−GMmr2dr=[GMmr]ab=GMm(1b−1a).

For b>a, the reciprocal decreases, making the result negative as predicted. For b<a, it is positive. The coefficient GMm has units Nm2, and multiplication by a reciprocal length leaves Nm.

Example 11.7 · Lift through a changing gravitational field

Use an ideal spherical source with GM=12m3s−2 and radius smaller than 2.0 m. A 0.50 kg probe is moved radially from 2.0 m to 6.0 m. Find gravity’s work and the work of an external agent that moves it with unchanged kinetic energy, with no other work contributions.

Wg=(12)(0.50)(16−12)J=−2.0J.

The external agent must do +2.0 J so that the net work is zero. Multiplying the initial inward force, −1.5 N, by the 4.0 m displacement gives −6.0 J; that exaggerates the opposing work because the force weakens along the outward journey. The parameters define a transparent mathematical benchmark rather than measured data for a particular planet.

The model’s domain is part of the integral setup. The expression 1/r2 is singular at r=0. Subtracting two finite antiderivative values does not justify integrating through that point. Moreover, a real source with radius R has already taken us outside the exterior model once the path enters r<R. A numerical program must respect that physical boundary as well as avoid division by zero. We will enforce both in Lesson 4.

Activity 11.7 · Signs, bounds and a near-surface check (30 minutes)

Reverse the journey in Example 11.7 and calculate gravity’s work. Then derive its work for a small outward step from a to a+h, keeping h>0. Simplify the reciprocal difference and compare with the constant-field estimate −GMmh/a2. Under what condition do they agree closely? Explain why moving the origin of a plotted axis does not move the source’s singular point physically.

Hint

Combine 1/(a+h)−1/a over a common denominator before making an approximation.

Discussion of Activity 11.7

The return work is +2.0 J. The small-step result is −GMmh/[a(a+h)]. If h/a is small, replace a+h by a in the denominator to obtain −GMmh/a2=−mgh, with g=GM/a2. The approximation comes from little fractional change of radius. Changing coordinate labels does not change the physical separation from the source: the force law must still use that separation.

11.8 A force accumulated over time gives impulse

Work accumulates a force component over displacement. Unit 6 introduced a different product, force times duration, to describe impulse. With a time-varying resultant force, split the elapsed time into small pieces and add Fx(ti)Δti. The limiting quantity is

Jx=∫tatbFnet,x(t)dt=Δpx.

Here Jx is an impulse component, not the unit symbol for joule. Its units are newton seconds, equivalent to kilogram metres per second. The equality follows by integrating Newton’s momentum form, Fnet,x=dpx/dt. A force–time graph and a force–position graph may have the same shape, but their horizontal axes make their areas different physical quantities.

Example 11.8 · A force pulse

A resultant force acts along x with Fx(t)=ct for 0≤t≤2.0s, where c=3.0Ns−1. The impulse is

Jx=[ct22]02.0s=6.0Ns.

For a constant-mass 2.0 kg particle initially at rest, Δvx=Jx/m=3.0ms−1. The final kinetic energy is 9.0 J, so the net work is 9.0 J. The force–time area was 6.0 N s; it was never 6.0 J. Obtaining work directly from this time description would also require the velocity, through W=∫Fx(t)vx(t)dt.

Activity 11.8 · Choose what is accumulated (20 minutes)

A resultant force is +4.0 N for the first 0.50 s and −2.0 N for the next 0.50 s. Find its impulse and the change of velocity of a 0.50 kg particle. The initial velocity is −1.0 m/s. Find the final velocity and net work from the endpoint kinetic energies. Explain why the impulse can be nonzero while the work is zero.

Discussion of Activity 11.8

The impulse is 4.0(0.50)−2.0(0.50)=+1.0Ns. Thus Δvx=+2.0ms−1 and the final velocity is +1.0 m/s. The initial and final speeds are equal, so both kinetic energies are 0.25 J and net work is zero. Momentum retains velocity’s direction; kinetic energy depends on its square. The particle reverses direction during the force history, which a signed impulse records.

We can now distinguish two accumulations of force: over displacement for work, and over time for impulse. The field models in this unit will mainly use the first. For gravity and electrostatic forces, work also allows an energy difference to be assigned to two positions. That is the route to potential.
