P101 · Unit 11 · Lesson 3

Potential and energy differences

The work calculation follows a force along a journey. For gravitational and electrostatic interactions, the same work can be recovered from an energy difference between the endpoints. Potential expresses that energy description per unit mass or charge.

Allow four hours: 95 minutes reading, 100 minutes of activities, and 45 minutes for feedback, correction and retrieval.

11.9 Potential energy and its reference

Recall the lifted object in Unit 6. Gravity did negative work while its gravitational potential energy increased. The relationship was Wg=−ΔUg. We can extend this idea beyond a uniform near-surface field. A force is called conservative when its work between two positions depends only on those positions, rather than on the route. Newtonian gravity and the electric forces of fixed, stationary charges have this property. Their work around a closed route is zero.

For a conservative interaction, define the potential-energy difference by

UB−UA=−WA→B.

The minus sign ensures that positive work by the interaction corresponds to a decrease in its potential energy. The energy belongs to the interacting system. With a very massive or supported source treated as fixed, we often speak briefly of “the probe’s potential energy”; the source–probe interaction is still what the energy describes.

This definition supplies differences. To assign an individual value UA, choose a reference arrangement where the energy is zero. Adding the same constant to every value changes no difference, no work and no predicted motion. A negative potential energy is therefore permissible. Kinetic energy, by contrast, is nonnegative for the classical particles considered here.

Example 11.9 · Three energy quantities

A conservative field does −0.60 J of work during a move from A to B. At the same time an external agent does +0.90 J, and no other force does work. Find the potential-energy change and kinetic-energy change.

The field work gives ΔU=+0.60J. The net work is −0.60+0.90=+0.30J, so ΔK=+0.30J. Equivalently, the agent’s work becomes ΔK+ΔU=0.90J. The agent’s work would equal ΔU only if the kinetic energy were unchanged.

If UA=−1.0J, then UB=−0.40J. If we instead set UA=0, then UB=+0.60J. Both conventions describe the same transfer.

Activity 11.9 · Keep one energy account (20 minutes)

A particle moves from A to B under a conservative force alone. Its potential energy changes from +0.20 J to −0.10 J and its initial kinetic energy is 0.05 J. Find the field work and final kinetic energy. Repeat after adding 2.0 J to both potential energies. Explain the error in adding the field work to an equation that already conserves K+U.

Discussion of Activity 11.9

ΔU=−0.30J, so the field does +0.30 J and the final kinetic energy is 0.35 J. The shifted energies are 2.20 and 1.90 J, with the same difference. Use either ΔK=Wfield or Δ(K+U)=0. Adding field work to the latter would count its transfer twice.

11.10 Gravitational potential

Every term in the gravitational work found in Lesson 2 is proportional to the probe mass. Dividing potential energy by that mass gives a property of the source and position: the gravitational potential, denoted ϕ. Its unit is joules per kilogram. In a fixed source field, Ug=mϕ, and the finite-radius work result gives

ϕ(b)−ϕ(a)=GM(1a−1b).

A function with exactly these differences is ϕ(r)=−GM/r+C. For an isolated source, it is convenient to choose the potential to approach zero as the separation becomes arbitrarily large. Since 1/r then approaches zero, this convention sets C=0:

ϕ(r)=−GMr,Ug(r)=−GMmr.

The potential is negative at every finite exterior radius under this reference choice. Moving outwards makes it less negative, so it increases. The field is inward. These statements are consistent: gravity favours movement towards lower potential energy, which for a positive probe mass means lower gravitational potential.

Notice the different distance dependence. Field magnitude falls as 1/r2, while the magnitude of potential falls as 1/r. Potential has no arrow direction; it is a scalar energy-per-mass value. Its local change with position is what relates to a force. The radial relation is gr=−dϕ/dr, as you can verify by differentiating −GM/r.

Example 11.10 · Recover work from a potential difference

Return to the benchmark GM=12m3s−2, with source radius below 2.0 m. The potentials at 2.0 m and 6.0 m are −6.0 J/kg and −2.0 J/kg. A 0.50 kg probe therefore has

ΔUg=(0.50kg)(+4.0Jkg−1)=+2.0J.

Gravity’s work is −2.0 J, agreeing with the direct integral. If we choose zero potential at 2.0 m instead, add 6.0 J/kg to the entire potential function. The two values become 0 and +4.0 J/kg, while the field and work remain unchanged.

Activity 11.10 · A potential value is not a field value (20 minutes)

For the same source, calculate the potential and inward field magnitude at 3.0 m and 6.0 m. State their different scaling factors. A probe falls radially from rest at 6.0 m to 3.0 m, with no other work and remaining outside the source. Find its final speed without choosing a probe mass. Explain why the result has no mass dependence.

Discussion of Activity 11.10

The potentials are −4.0 and −2.0 J/kg; their magnitudes halve when radius doubles. Field magnitudes are 12/9=4/3 and 12/36=1/3 N/kg, a factor of four apart. Falling gives Δϕ=−2.0Jkg−1, so 12v2=−Δϕ=2.0Jkg−1 and v=2.0ms−1. Both kinetic and gravitational potential energy scale with the probe mass, which cancels.

11.11 Electric potential and signed charge

For stationary source charges, electric force is proportional to probe charge: F=qE. Its work and potential-energy differences are also proportional to q. Define electric potential V as potential energy per unit probe charge. The most useful relation is

ΔUe=qΔV,We=−qΔV.

The unit of potential is joule per coulomb, called the volt, symbol V. The italic variable V denotes potential; the upright unit symbol denotes volts. “Voltage between A and B” is a potential difference and needs an order: here ΔV=VB−VA. Once a zero reference is chosen, we may also write Ue=qV for the probe–source interaction.

The signed charge must remain in this equation. A positive probe gains potential energy when it moves to higher V. A negative probe loses potential energy during that same move. If the electric field alone does work, the lost potential energy becomes kinetic energy in either case. The statement “charges move downhill in potential” therefore needs the sign of the charge; decreasing potential energy is the common energy statement.

For a fixed point source Q, use the radial force keQq/r2. Integrating along a radial path gives

We=keQq(1a−1b),
V(b)−V(a)=keQ(1b−1a).

With zero potential at infinite separation, the corresponding function is V(r)=keQ/r. It has the source charge’s sign. For several fixed point charges, add their potentials as signed scalar values. Add their fields as vectors. The distinction matters even at a point where symmetry makes one of those sums zero.

Example 11.11 · Field cancellation does not imply zero potential

Revisit the two +2.0nC sources at x=±0.20m. At the midpoint, their fields cancel. With the potential zero at infinity, each contributes keQ/r=89.9V. Their potentials add to 179.8 V.

Now change the right-hand source to −2.0nC. The two midpoint potentials cancel, while the fields add to 899 N/C to the right, as in Lesson 1. A zero potential value describes the chosen energy reference at that point. A zero field describes cancellation of force per unit charge. Neither statement entails the other.

Example 11.12 · Follow a negative probe’s energy

A probe with charge q=−2.0μC moves from potential 30 V to 90 V. The sources are fixed. Find the electric work and potential-energy change. If no other force does work, what happens to kinetic energy?

The change is final minus initial: ΔV=+60V. Hence

ΔUe=(−2.0×10−6C)(60JC−1)=−1.2×10−4J.

The electric work is +1.2×10−4J, which is also the kinetic-energy gain under the stated condition. The particle gains kinetic energy while moving to higher potential, because its charge is negative. A positive charge following those same endpoints would instead lose kinetic energy if no other work occurred.

Activity 11.11 · One source, two probe signs (30 minutes)

A fixed point source is Q=+3.0nC. Find the potentials at radii 0.15 m and 0.45 m, using zero at infinity. For a move from the smaller radius to the larger, calculate ΔV, ΔU and electric work for a +1.0nC probe. Repeat the last two quantities for a −1.0nC probe. Explain the work signs using attraction or repulsion before comparing with your arithmetic.

Discussion of Activity 11.11

The potentials are 179.8 and about 59.93 V, giving ΔV≈−119.87V. For the positive probe, ΔU≈−1.1987×10−7J and work is positive by that amount: repulsion assists outward motion. For the negative probe these signs reverse; attraction opposes the outward displacement. The source potential and its difference are the same for both probes.

11.12 From a potential map to an energy prediction

Consider a region with uniform electric field component Ex. Moving a probe from xA to xB gives electric work qEx(xB−xA). Compare this with −q(VB−VA) and cancel the nonzero probe charge:

VB−VA=−Ex(xB−xA).

Potential decreases in the electric-field direction. A large magnitude of potential change over a short distance corresponds to a strong field component. A large potential value measured from an arbitrary reference, by itself, does not. In one dimension the local relationship is Ex=−dV/dx. Its units, volts per metre, equal newtons per coulomb because 1V=1NmC−1.

A set of positions with the same potential is an equipotential. A move along it has zero potential difference and therefore zero electrostatic work. For an isolated point source, spheres centred on the source are equipotentials. Their radial field is perpendicular to those surfaces. On a drawing through the source, the spheres appear as concentric circles. Where the field is nonzero it is perpendicular to an equipotential surface: a tangential field component would do work along that surface and contradict the unchanged potential.

To calculate a speed, first write the energy balance and its physical assumptions. For a fixed electrostatic source arrangement, a classical particle and no other work,

KB−KA=−q(VB−VA).

A particle initially at rest can reach an endpoint by this energy account only if the predicted KB is nonnegative. This endpoint condition is necessary but does not specify the trajectory or guarantee an unobstructed route. Those details require the forces and the intervening potential landscape. We use simple accessible paths in the calculations here.

Activity 11.12 · Read the slope and the energy (30 minutes)

In a one-dimensional region 0≤x≤0.050m, the potential is V(x)=120V−(600Vm−1)x. Find the field component. A particle of charge +2.0nC and mass 4.0×10−6kg is released at rest at x=0 and moves along the axis with no other work. Find its electric force, potential-energy change and speed at the other end. Then add 1000 V to the entire potential function and identify every answer that changes.

Hint

Differentiate the potential for the field. For speed, use the potential difference and the kinetic-energy formula; do not multiply by the absolute potential.

Discussion of Activity 11.12

The field is +600NC−1. The force is +1.2×10−6N. The final potential is 90 V, so ΔV=−30V and ΔU=−6.0×10−8J. The kinetic-energy gain is 6.0×10−8J, giving v=2(6.0×10−8)/(4.0×10−6)=0.030≈0.173ms−1. Adding 1000 V changes the endpoint potential values, and shifts both assigned potential energies by q(1000V). The field, force, differences and speed are unchanged.

Key idea 11.2 · Four quantities, four roles

A field predicts force per unit probe property; potential predicts potential energy per unit probe property. Force is a vector, energy a scalar. For fixed electrostatic sources, use F=qE and ΔU=qΔV, keeping the signed charge in both.

Potential gives voltage its energy meaning before we meet a circuit. We have also established a useful cross-check: a force integral and a potential difference should predict the same work. Next we will introduce magnetic interactions qualitatively and then use a numerical field calculation to test that agreement.
