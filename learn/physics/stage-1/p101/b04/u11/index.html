P101 · Block 4 · Unit 11

Fields, potentials and accumulated work

Two objects placed at the same location can experience different forces. Yet something about that location can be described before either object is put there. Separating those two statements is the starting point for a field.

The generator in Figure 11.1 belongs to a family of instruments used to build up electric charge and demonstrate its effects. A charged object can attract or repel another object without touching it. Gravity is another familiar interaction across a separation. We already know how to calculate the gravitational attraction between simple bodies; now we will reorganise that description so that the source and the object responding to it have distinct roles.

Imagine preparing a map before placing a small probe near a source. At each location, the map would tell us the force per unit mass, or the force per unit charge. Multiplying by the probe’s mass or charge then gives its force. Such a map is a field. Its arrows carry direction as well as magnitude, so reading it calls on the vector reasoning you have already used in force diagrams.

A second map answers an energy question. How much does the interaction’s potential energy change when a probe moves between two locations? Dividing that change by mass or charge produces a potential difference. Force and energy are connected through work, but they are different quantities. A large value of potential need not mean a large local field, and a negative charge responds differently from a positive one in the same electric field. We will develop these distinctions through calculations, diagrams and explicit sign choices.

The bridge between the two maps is accumulation. A gravitational or electric force can change substantially along a journey. Multiplying its initial value by the whole distance then gives only an approximation. We will build work from small displacement contributions, evaluate a definite integral and compare it with a transparent numerical sum. The comparison will help you distinguish an inaccurate approximation, an incorrectly implemented equation and a physical model used outside its domain.

Allow 18 hours, including three hours of Python. The five lessons move from fields, through accumulated work and potential, to magnetic interactions and a numerical investigation. The final lesson brings the unit together with cumulative exercises, separate hints and worked solutions, a conclusion and matching web/PDF references. Attempts and correction belong within those hours. The Python investigation uses functions, loops and labelled plots already introduced in P101; its calculations use integration from M101 Units 8–9.

Before beginning

You should be able to add signed force components, use inverse-square scaling, distinguish the work of one force from net work, and explain Wnet=ΔK. For the calculus, evaluate ∫abxndx when the power rule applies, check an antiderivative by differentiation and explain why an interval containing a singularity needs separate attention. Revisit P101 Unit 6 or M101 Units 8–9 if those steps are uncertain.

By the end, you should be able to choose between a force description and an energy description, keep the source separate from its probe, calculate work with justified signs and bounds, and support a short computational conclusion with an independent benchmark. These ideas will give voltage a precise meaning when Unit 12 turns to circuits.
