P101 · Unit 8 · Lesson 5

A defensible signal investigation

A useful investigation makes the connection between a question, a controlled comparison and a conclusion visible. You now have enough physical and computational tools to investigate a sampling choice and explain why the result deserves a particular level of confidence.

Allow four hours: 15 minutes opening reading, 60 minutes Python, 100 minutes cumulative exercises, 45 minutes solutions and correction, and 20 minutes reference revision. The exercises cover the whole unit. Full worked solutions remain closed until you choose to open them.

8.16 Investigate one choice at a time

In Lesson 4, a known 2 Hz model exposed an alias. The new task changes the signal and asks you to defend a comparison of your own. Before writing code, state which quantity you intend to estimate, which information is supplied independently and which choice you are changing. A numerical answer has more meaning when those roles are explicit.

Keep the generating model separate from the estimator. The model supplies a true period against which an error can be calculated. The estimator sees only sampled times and values. If you replace its result with the model’s period when it looks wrong, you remove the very disagreement the investigation is meant to explain.

Use two forms of evidence together: the sample markers against the known curve, and the individual upward-crossing intervals. A plot can reveal missed structure; the interval list can reveal an irregular estimate hidden by its mean. Neither establishes a real instrument’s performance because this investigation generates its own data. It tests the consequences of specified sampling choices under a controlled model.

Activity 8.15 · A controlled sampling comparison (60 minutes, Python)

Continue the Unit 8 notebook or script. Use a new model with amplitude 0.008 m, frequency 1.25 Hz and initial phase zero. Compare rates of 20 and 2 samples/s over eight seconds. Start from predictions on paper.

  1. Predict the true period, samples per cycle and the number of timestamps for both records. Check at least three values independently.
  2. Implement the comparison with the existing estimator. Show labelled graphs, crossing times and intervals, period estimates and differences from the true period.
  3. For the sparse record, test a second candidate at 0.75 Hz. Use the sample times to check whether it gives the same values. Explain the phase relation that makes this possible.
  4. Choose one follow-up: change initial phase to π/4 while keeping everything else fixed, or compare four- and eight-second records at the same rate. Predict what the change might reveal before running it.
  5. Write a short conclusion answering which sampling choice better supports this estimator, what the benchmark establishes and one limitation that would remain for a real observation. Save the raw times/values or retain their exact generating code and parameters. Restart and rerun.
Hint for Activity 8.15

The true period is the reciprocal of 1.25 Hz. At 2 samples/s, the phase of the 1.25 Hz model can be written as a whole number of turns minus the phase of the 0.75 Hz model. For a nonzero phase, the matching alias generally needs the opposite initial phase.

Discussion and criteria for Activity 8.15

The true period is 0.8 s. The two rates supply 16 and 1.6 samples per cycle; eight seconds inclusive gives 161 and 17 timestamps. With phase zero, the 20 samples/s record yields a period close to 0.8 s. The 2 samples/s record matches the 0.75 Hz cosine at every timestamp, because 1.25=2−0.75. Its apparent period is near 1/0.75≈1.333s, with finite-record/interpolation variation visible in the interval list. For the specified eight-second, phase-zero record, the estimator gives approximately 1.34142 s; its five intervals take values of approximately 1.29289 and 1.41421 s.

At times 0, 0.5 and 1 s, the sparse displacements are 0.008 m, approximately −0.005657 m and zero. A nonzero initial phase changes which parts of the cycle are sampled; compare the alias with initial phase of opposite sign. Extending duration may stabilise the mean of estimated intervals but cannot distinguish the exactly aliased candidates at the same sampling times.

A satisfactory report states model, units, rates and actual time range; preserves sample markers; includes a hand check and estimator output; changes only the chosen follow-up; and explains aliasing rather than blaming a correct program. The denser record better resolves crossings for this model. Real timing, noise, sensor response and unknown high-frequency content would need separate investigation. Different follow-up choices can support equally sound reports when the evidence and limitation are clear.

Before the cumulative exercises, return to one earlier explanation that the investigation made more useful: the definition of a cycle, the role of initial phase, or the distinction between a model and observations. A short written connection is more valuable than collecting additional plots without a question.

8.17 Cumulative exercises

Attempt these questions before opening the solutions. The suggested times total 100 minutes; reserve the separate 45 minutes for checking, diagnosing errors and correcting your reasoning. Use a calculator when needed. No additional program is required: the notebook investigation above supplies the coding practice. Hints support a second attempt; solutions explain the reasoning.

Exercise 8.1 · Read the centre and the cycle (5 minutes)

A sinusoidal position record ranges from −7 mm to +5 mm. Positive maxima occur at 1.1 s and 1.7 s, with no maximum between them. Find equilibrium position, amplitude, peak-to-peak displacement, period and frequency.

Hint for Exercise 8.1

Use the midpoint and half-range for position; subtract the maximum times.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.1

The centre is (−7+5)/2=−1mm. Amplitude is (5−(−7))/2=6mm, and peak-to-peak displacement is 12 mm. The adjacent maxima are 0.6 s apart, so T=0.6s and f=1/0.6≈1.67Hz. The negative centre is an origin convention, not a negative amplitude.

Return to Exercise 8.1

Exercise 8.2 · Count events carefully (6 minutes)

Nine successive leftward equilibrium crossings run from 3.2 s to 15.6 s. Estimate period and frequency. Each endpoint timestamp has a possible timing contribution bounded by ±0.08 s. Bound the resulting contribution to the period and identify one limitation not removed by counting more cycles.

Hint for Exercise 8.2

Nine matching events enclose eight intervals. Endpoint contributions can oppose one another.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.2

The interval is 12.4 s across eight cycles, giving T=12.4/8=1.55s and f≈0.645Hz. The duration contribution is bounded by 0.16 s, hence the period contribution by 0.16/8=0.020s. More cycles do not remove a systematic clock-rate error, a repeated miscount or a period changing during the record. This bound concerns the specified endpoint timing only.

Return to Exercise 8.2

Exercise 8.3 · Choose a starting phase (6 minutes)

A displacement has amplitude 0.025 m and period 1.2 s. At time zero it crosses equilibrium in the positive direction. Write a cosine model with units and find displacement at 0.3 s and 0.6 s.

Hint for Exercise 8.3

Which quarter-turn phase gives zero displacement followed by positive values?

Worked solution to Exercise 8.3

Angular frequency is ω=2π/(1.2s)=(5π/3)s−1. Choose ϕ=−π/2:

x(t)=(0.025m)cos[(5π3s−1)t−π2].
At 0.3 s the phase is 0, giving +0.025 m. At 0.6 s it is π/2, giving zero while moving negatively. Both zero crossings belong to different stages of the cycle.

Return to Exercise 8.3

Exercise 8.4 · A delayed record (5 minutes)

Two oscillations share a 3 s period. B reaches each corresponding maximum 0.75 s after A. If A uses initial phase zero, give a suitable phase for B and write its model for amplitude 2 cm. Explain whether frequency changed.

Hint for Exercise 8.4

Write a model whose bracket vanishes at the delayed maximum.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.4

Use xB=(2cm)cos[ω(t−0.75s)], where ω=2π/(3s). Its initial phase is −ω(0.75s)=−π/2, up to whole turns. Both retain frequency 1/3 Hz; the change is a quarter-cycle delay, not a new period.

Return to Exercise 8.4

Exercise 8.5 · Three quantities at a turning point (6 minutes)

A motion is x=(0.020m)cos[(6s−1)t]. Find its maximum speed and acceleration magnitude. At the negative extreme, give displacement, velocity, acceleration and the direction of resultant force.

Hint for Exercise 8.5

The acceleration is −ω2x; velocity vanishes at an extreme.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.5

Maximum speed is Aω=0.12m/s, and maximum acceleration magnitude is Aω2=0.72m/s2. At the negative extreme, x=−0.020m, v=0, and a=+0.72m/s2. The force points positively, towards equilibrium. Zero velocity at the turn does not imply a balanced force.

Return to Exercise 8.5

Exercise 8.6 · Design a slower oscillator (6 minutes)

An ideal spring oscillator has period 0.80 s. You want 1.20 s using the same spring. By what factor must mass change? If amplitude remains fixed, by what factor does maximum speed change? State two physical conditions for this prediction.

Hint for Exercise 8.6

Compare squared periods; maximum speed is proportional to angular frequency at fixed amplitude.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.6

The period ratio is 1.5, so the mass ratio is 1.52=2.25. Angular frequency and maximum speed change by 1/1.5=2/3. Conditions include a linear spring over the used range, negligible damping, fixed support and spring mass negligible relative to the moving mass. The prediction does not follow merely from a periodic-looking record.

Return to Exercise 8.6

Exercise 8.7 · Follow the energy (7 minutes)

A horizontal oscillator has m=0.20kg, k=32N/m and A=0.050m. Find total mechanical energy, potential and kinetic energy at x=−0.030m, and the two possible velocities there.

Hint for Exercise 8.7

Find total energy at an extreme; subtract potential energy before finding speed.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.7

E=12(32)(0.050)2=0.040J.
At the stated position, U=12(32)(0.030)2=0.0144J, leaving K=0.0256J. Thus |v|=2(0.0256)/0.20≈0.506m/s. Velocity is −0.506 m/s on the journey towards the negative extreme, and +0.506 m/s on the return towards equilibrium. Energy alone does not select the sign.

Return to Exercise 8.7

Exercise 8.8 · A shrinking free response (6 minutes)

A linear spring’s successive positive turning displacements decrease from 10 mm to 6 mm over a stated interval. What fraction of the initial mechanical energy remains at the later turning point? What fraction has left the oscillation? Explain why this does not imply failure of energy conservation.

Hint for Exercise 8.8

At each turning point kinetic energy is zero and elastic energy depends on displacement squared.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.8

The retained fraction is (6/10)2=0.36; the fraction removed from oscillatory mechanical energy is 0.64. Energy can become internal energy or leave the chosen system. A larger system including the relevant surroundings retains a complete account. The amplitude ratio 0.6 is not the energy ratio.

Return to Exercise 8.8

Exercise 8.9 · Interpret a response peak (5 minutes)

At fixed applied force amplitude, steady displacement amplitudes are 2, 5 and 3 mm at driving frequencies 1.8, 2.0 and 2.2 Hz. A report claims “the exact natural frequency is 2.0 Hz and the driver supplies no energy because amplitude is constant”. Evaluate both claims and propose one additional observation.

Hint for Exercise 8.9

A sampled maximum and a steady energy balance answer different questions.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.9

2.0 Hz gives the largest measured response among the three settings; the exact peak can lie between settings, and a damped displacement peak need not equal the undamped natural frequency exactly. Constant amplitude means no net change of stored mechanical energy per cycle, so continuing input can balance damping loss. More closely spaced frequencies with fixed force amplitude and adequate settling would help locate the response peak. A free-decay record could separately inform the natural-response comparison.

Return to Exercise 8.9

Exercise 8.10 · A sample grid (4 minutes)

An inclusive record runs from 0 to 3 s at 10 samples/s. Give its spacing, number of intervals and timestamps. For a 2.5 Hz signal, how many samples per cycle does it provide? Explain why duration is a separate choice.

Hint for Exercise 8.10

Count intervals first, then include the starting timestamp.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.10

Spacing is 0.1 s. There are 30 intervals and 31 timestamps. The ratio is 10/2.5=4 samples per cycle. Extending the record at the same rate adds cycles and samples without reducing the spacing; increasing the rate at fixed duration provides more samples within each cycle.

Return to Exercise 8.10

Exercise 8.11 · A period from crossings (7 minutes)

An upward crossing is bracketed by −3 mm at 0.2 s and +1 mm at 0.4 s. Estimate its time. Two later upward crossings are estimated at 1.15 s and 1.97 s. Find individual intervals, mean period and frequency. Explain one approximation in the method.

Hint for Exercise 8.11

Find the fraction of the rise needed to reach zero, then apply it to the time gap.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.11

The required rise is 3 mm out of 4 mm, so c0=0.2+(3/4)(0.2)=0.35s. The intervals are 0.80 and 0.82 s. Mean period is (1.97−0.35)/2=0.81s; frequency is approximately 1.23 Hz. Linear interpolation replaces the actual curve between samples by a straight segment. Timing, noise or a missed crossing can introduce further limitations in real records.

Return to Exercise 8.11

Exercise 8.12 · Construct an alias (7 minutes)

Two zero-phase cosines have frequencies 3 Hz and 2 Hz and equal amplitude. Show that their samples agree at 5 samples/s. Would a longer record at that same rate distinguish them? Suggest a rate that would resolve both known candidates more usefully for a graphical comparison.

Hint for Exercise 8.12

At sample n, write 3n/5 as n−2n/5 and use cosine symmetry.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.12

At tn=n/5s, the first phase is 6πn/5=2πn−4πn/5. Whole-turn periodicity and even symmetry give the same cosine as the 2 Hz candidate. This equality holds for every integer n, so a longer record at unchanged sampling times cannot distinguish them. A rate such as 30 samples/s gives at least ten samples per cycle for these candidates, comfortably above twice 3 Hz; check the resulting record rather than inferring a universal guarantee.

Return to Exercise 8.12

Exercise 8.13 · Exactly two samples per cycle (6 minutes)

A sinusoid of frequency 5 Hz is sampled at 10 samples/s. Compare the records for x=Acos(2πft) and x=Acos(2πft+π/2). Explain why “two is always enough” fails, and why constant sample values need not establish rest.

Hint for Exercise 8.13

Substitute tn=n/10 before evaluating cosine.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.13

The first record is Acos(πn)=A(−1)n, alternating ±A. The second is Acos(πn+π/2)=0 at every sample. Motion continues between the zero samples. Exactly two samples per cycle can therefore hide the signal for a particular phase. Constant samples support a stationary interpretation only with additional bounds or observations that exclude such missed motion.

Return to Exercise 8.13

Exercise 8.14 · Choose evidence to separate models (8 minutes)

A sparse record is consistent with both a 1 Hz and a 4 Hz zero-phase cosine of equal amplitude. It was sampled at 5 samples/s. Design a follow-up observation that distinguishes these candidates. State what to keep fixed, what to change, a hand-checkable time at which the predictions differ, and a limitation that remains for an unknown real signal.

Hint for Exercise 8.14

A quarter of a second is easy to evaluate for both candidates.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.14

Keep the same physical setup, position convention and documented phase origin; increase the sampling rate, for example to 20 samples/s. This includes 0.25 s, where the 1 Hz model predicts Acos(π/2)=0 and the 4 Hz model predicts Acos(2π)=A. A measurement precise enough to distinguish these values would separate the stipulated candidates. An unknown real signal could contain higher frequencies, noise or a changing phase; the candidate comparison alone does not establish an adequate upper frequency bound for every possible input.

Return to Exercise 8.14

Exercise 8.15 · A physical model meets a sampling choice (7 minutes)

An ideal oscillator has m=0.25kg and k=π2N/m. Find natural frequency and period. A recorder samples it once per second from its positive extreme. What samples does the ideal model predict? Would changing amplitude alone resolve the timing ambiguity?

Hint for Exercise 8.15

First find ω=k/m, then convert radians per second to cycles per second.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.15

ω=π20.25=2πs−1.

Therefore f=1Hz and T=1s.

At integer seconds the model returns to its positive extreme, so every sample equals +A. Changing amplitude changes that constant sampled value but leaves the ideal period unchanged. The rate or timing of observation must change to reveal intervening stages. This combines a force-model prediction with the sampling rule.

Return to Exercise 8.15

Exercise 8.16 · Review a scientific conclusion (9 minutes)

A notebook says: “My generated damped oscillator gives a smooth graph. At one sample per cycle all values look nearly constant. The program reruns, so the device has stopped and damping has been measured.” Identify at least four distinct problems and rewrite the conclusion as a defensible short statement. Describe one additional check.

Hint for Exercise 8.16

Separate model provenance, sampling information, physical mechanism and reproducibility.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.16

A generated record is not a device measurement; a smooth joined line supplies no missing observations; once-per-cycle samples miss intervening motion; successful rerunning checks reproducibility rather than truth of a physical model. Damping was imposed by the generator, not empirically measured. With appreciable imposed decay, same-phase amplitudes should change, so “nearly constant” also needs a numerical check. A defensible conclusion is: “This implementation reproduces the chosen damped model. The current sample times reveal only one stage per cycle and cannot establish rest between samples. I will compare denser samples with the exact model and inspect successive peak sizes before interpreting decay.” Actual damping measurements would require independent observations and documented instrumental limits.

Return to Exercise 8.16

Consult · Python sampling pattern

Seconds, metres and radians. The full checked estimator is supplied in Lesson 4 and the notebook; its absolute zero tolerance is 10−12m for these simulations.

import numpy as np
fs, f, A, phi = 20.0, 2.0, 0.01, 0.0
t = np.arange(81) / fs       # 0 to 4 s inclusive
x = A*np.cos(2*np.pi*f*t + phi)
# Use the lesson's checked crossing_period function:
period, crossings = crossing_period(t, x)
intervals = np.diff(crossings)
error = period - 1/f        # synthetic benchmark only

What the unit has connected

The camera question at the start of the unit had two possible answers because a repeated set of observed positions need not reveal the motion between them. We can now describe both sides of that ambiguity. Amplitude, period and phase specify a sinusoidal prediction; the sampling times determine which stages of that prediction appear in the record.

Force and energy reasoning made the model physical. A restoring acceleration changes the direction of motion, while inertia carries the body through equilibrium. Energy exchanges between kinetic and potential forms; damping and driving account for changes in the oscillation’s size. These relationships explain the model’s behaviour and identify conditions under which it should be reconsidered.

Sampling added a different limitation. Even an exactly evaluated model and a reproducible estimator can yield a misleading apparent period when distinct motions produce the same record. A defensible conclusion therefore keeps the generating assumptions, sampling choice, hand benchmark and remaining uncertainty visible.

Unit 9 will extend a periodic description through space. You will distinguish a time record at one place from a snapshot of a travelling pattern, then connect period to wavelength and propagation speed. Keep the corrected phase reasoning and sampling notebook ready for that transition. TMA 03 follows Unit 10 and remains a separate assessment.
