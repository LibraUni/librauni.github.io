P101 · Unit 8 · Lesson 2

A sinusoidal model of oscillation

Amplitude and period describe the size and timing of a cycle, but do not locate the object at a particular instant. We now add a starting phase and construct a model that predicts displacement, velocity and acceleration together.

Allow four hours: 85 minutes for the explanation and worked examples, 105 minutes for activities and 50 minutes for checking and correction. Use radian mode for trigonometric calculations.

8.5 From a cycle to phase

Think of a hand moving uniformly around a clock face. Its angle tells you how far through a revolution it has travelled. We can use an angle in the same way to label the stage of an oscillation, even when the oscillating object moves on a straight line. This angle-like variable is the phase.

In radian measure, an angle is the arc length it subtends divided by the circle’s radius. A complete circumference has length 2πr, so one complete turn corresponds to 2π radians. A half-turn is π radians and a quarter-turn is π/2. These correspond to 360°, 180° and 90°. Radians express the angle as a ratio of lengths; the label rad makes the convention visible.

For a cycle of period T, the phase advances by 2π in time T. Its steady rate of advance is called the angular frequency,

ω=2πT=2πf.

We use ω, the Greek letter omega, with units rad/s. Frequency f counts cycles per second; angular frequency counts radians per second. The factor 2π connects them. After time t, a motion that starts at phase zero has phase ωt.

To turn phase into a displacement, imagine the horizontal projection of a point moving around a circle of radius A. The horizontal coordinate is Acosθ. As the angle θ advances through 0, π/2, π, 3π/2 and 2π, that coordinate takes the values A,0,−A,0,A. The projection repeats the sequence established in Lesson 1.

Choosing θ=ωt gives the supplied sinusoidal displacement model

x(t)=Acos(ωt).

Cosine is bounded between −1 and +1, so multiplying by A sets the amplitude. Replacing t by t+T adds 2π to the cosine’s input and returns the same value. This checks the period directly. The circle supplies a representation of the phase; the physical trolley still moves along its track.

Example 8.4 · A quarter-cycle check

A model has amplitude 0.030 m and period 1.6 s, starting at its positive extreme. Find angular frequency and displacement at 0.4 s.

First convert the period into phase rate:

ω=2π1.6s=1.25πrad/s.

At 0.4 s the elapsed time is one quarter of a period, so the phase is π/2 and x=0.030cos(π/2)=0m. A calculator should agree when set to radians. Entering π/2 while in degree mode would describe an angle near 1.57°, rather than a quarter-turn.

Activity 8.5 · Make the model visible (25 minutes)

For Example 8.4, tabulate time, phase and displacement at 0, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2 and 1.6 s. Sketch a smooth graph through the points. Find f. Then halve the period while keeping amplitude fixed: what changes in the phase rate and in the graph?

Discussion of Activity 8.5

Phases are 0, π/2, π, 3π/2, 2π; displacements are 0.030, 0, −0.030, 0, 0.030 m. Frequency is 0.625 Hz. Halving the period doubles f to 1.25 Hz and ω to 2.5πrad/s. The horizontal pattern is compressed by a factor of two, while the vertical range stays the same.

8.6 Choosing the starting state

A recording need not begin at an extreme position. We account for its starting stage by adding a constant initial phase ϕ, pronounced “phi”, to the steadily advancing phase:

x(t)=Acos(ωt+ϕ).

At t=0, displacement is Acosϕ. Changing ϕ changes where the cycle begins without changing its amplitude or period. Adding a whole turn to ϕ gives exactly the same motion, so phase values that differ by 2π represent the same stage.

For ϕ=0, the object starts at +A. For ϕ=π, it starts at −A. The two zero-displacement starts require more care. With ϕ=π/2, the cosine passes through zero towards negative values: the object starts moving in the negative direction. With ϕ=−π/2, it passes through zero towards positive values. An initial displacement alone does not distinguish these two states; the direction of motion does.

When the instrument’s origin is elsewhere, write the measured position as y(t)=y0+x(t), where y0 is the equilibrium position. Thus

y(t)=y0+Acos(ωt+ϕ).

The offset y0 moves the curve vertically, and phase moves its timing horizontally. To understand the sign of a timing shift, let a positive maximum occur at time tp. One convenient form is x=Acos[ω(t−tp)]: the bracket vanishes at the maximum. Comparing with the phase form gives ϕ=−ωtp, up to whole turns.

Example 8.5 · Build a model from features

A sinusoidal position record has equilibrium 12 mm, amplitude 5 mm and period 2 s. It passes downwards through equilibrium at time zero. Write a model and check its position at 0.5 s.

The angular frequency is πrad/s. A downward crossing uses ϕ=π/2, giving

y(t)=12mm+(5mm)cos[(πs−1)t+π2].

At 0.5 s the phase is π, so y=12−5=7mm, the lower extreme. This is consistent with starting at the centre while moving downward. Choosing −π/2 would instead predict the upper extreme at that time.

Activity 8.6 · Compare starting states (25 minutes)

Two oscillations have amplitude 2 cm and period 4 s. A starts at its positive extreme. B first reaches its positive extreme at 1 s and was moving positively at time zero. Write a model for each, and tabulate their displacements at 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 s. Which reaches the same stage earlier, and by how much?

Discussion of Activity 8.6

Use xA=(2cm)cos[(π/2s−1)t] and xB=(2cm)cos[(π/2s−1)(t−1s)]. Their numerical displacement sequences in centimetres are A: 2, 0, −2, 0, 2; B: 0, 2, 0, −2, 0. B is delayed by 1 s, or a quarter-cycle, relative to A. Its initial phase is −π/2. Substituting the desired maximum time is a reliable check of the sign.

8.7 Velocity, acceleration and the restoring model

A displacement formula must agree with the motion it describes. For the sinusoidal model, the corresponding instantaneous velocity and acceleration are supplied here:

v(t)=−Aωsin(ωt+ϕ),
a(t)=−Aω2cos(ωt+ϕ)=−ω2x(t).

These are the first and second time derivatives of displacement. You can use them directly without deriving the trigonometric differentiation rules. Each extra time rate supplies one extra factor of ω, so the units are m/s and m/s² when amplitude is in metres. The offset in measured position is constant and therefore makes no contribution to either rate.

The final relation, a=−ω2x, is the physical signature of simple harmonic motion (SHM): acceleration is proportional to displacement from equilibrium and directed towards equilibrium. At x>0, acceleration is negative; at x<0, acceleration is positive. It vanishes at equilibrium. Its magnitude is greatest at the extremes, where |x|=A.

Velocity follows a different pattern. At a positive extreme, phase is zero modulo a whole turn, sine is zero and velocity vanishes. A quarter-period later, phase is π/2, displacement vanishes and velocity is −Aω. The trolley passes equilibrium at its greatest speed. At half a period after the positive extreme it is at the negative extreme with positive acceleration and zero velocity. This is why acceleration and velocity cannot be inferred from the sign of displacement alone.

Key result 8.2 · An ideal harmonic motion

For constant A, ω>0 and ϕ, displacement from equilibrium is Acos(ωt+ϕ). Maximum speed is Aω; maximum acceleration magnitude is Aω2. The motion obeys a=−ω2x. A repeating motion with another shape need not obey this relation.

Example 8.6 · At rest with a large acceleration

Take A=0.040m, ω=5.0rad/s, ϕ=0. Find the maximum speed and acceleration magnitude, then describe the state at t=π/5s.

The maxima are Aω=0.20m/s and Aω2=1.0m/s2. At the specified time the phase is π, so x=−0.040m, v=0 and a=+1.0m/s2. This is the left turning point: the positive acceleration changes the velocity from negative to positive.

For a trolley of mass m attached to an ideal spring, let the horizontal spring force be F=−kx. The positive constant k, in N/m, describes stiffness: more force is needed for the same displacement when k is larger. With other horizontal forces negligible, Newton’s law gives ma=−kx. Comparing with the supplied SHM acceleration requires

ω2=km,ω=km,T=2πmk.

This comparison checks that the supplied motion satisfies the force law. Solving for all possible motions from a differential equation is a later task. Here the starting amplitude and phase select the particular motion, while the force law fixes its angular frequency.

Activity 8.7 · Audit a motion report (30 minutes)

A report gives x=(0.060m)cos[(4s−1)t] for a 0.50 kg trolley. Find its period, maximum speed, maximum acceleration magnitude and spring stiffness. At the positive extreme, a student writes “velocity is zero, so the resultant force is zero”. Correct the statement using a numerical force. Sketch the signs of displacement, velocity and acceleration over one cycle.

Discussion of Activity 8.7

Here ω=4rad/s, so T=2π/4=π/2s, maximum speed is 0.24 m/s and maximum acceleration magnitude is 0.96 m/s². From k=mω2, stiffness is 8 N/m. At x=+0.060m, force is −kx=−0.48N, and acceleration is −0.96 m/s². At quarter-cycle stages, x has signs +,0,−,0,+; v has 0,−,0,+,0; a has −,0,+,0,−. The turning point has zero velocity and maximum force magnitude.

8.8 Using and checking the spring model

The period formula contains a square root, which gives a useful scaling prediction. Multiplying mass by four while keeping the spring fixed doubles the period. Multiplying stiffness by four while keeping mass fixed halves the period. A heavier trolley accelerates less under a given restoring force; a stiffer spring supplies more force at the same displacement. These physical checks agree with the algebra.

Amplitude does not appear in the ideal period. A larger displacement gives a longer path but also a stronger restoring force and greater speeds. Within the linear force model these changes produce the same period. This independence is a prediction to test, rather than a guarantee for every real spring. At large deformations the force may cease to be proportional to displacement; contact, slack or friction can alter the motion.

Example 8.7 · Check dimensions before numbers

A 0.32 kg trolley is attached to a spring with k=20N/m. Predict its ideal period, then the period after replacing the trolley with one of mass 0.72 kg.

Since 1N/m=1kg/s2, the ratio m/k has units s². Its square root is a time, as required. Substitution gives

T1=2π0.32kg20N/m≈0.795s.

Use the ratio to avoid repeating the full calculation:

T2T1=0.720.32=1.5,T2≈1.19s.

The mass increased by a factor 2.25, while the period increased by 1.5. Retaining extra digits until the final step avoids unnecessary rounding drift.

A real spring has its own moving mass. Our model treats that mass as negligible compared with the trolley’s and assumes one horizontal degree of freedom, a fixed support and a linear spring over the displacement range used. If a heavy spring or a flexible support participates appreciably, the model needs refinement. A measured period can then be compared with the prediction while keeping those assumptions visible.

Activity 8.8 · Choose a controlled test (25 minutes)

For a fixed ideal spring, predict the effect of (a) doubling amplitude, (b) doubling mass, and (c) doubling both mass and stiffness. In each case state the factors by which period and maximum speed change, keeping amplitude fixed except in (a). Then propose a graph or table that could test amplitude independence experimentally, including one control and one measurement limitation.

Discussion of Activity 8.8

(a) Period is unchanged and maximum speed doubles. (b) Period increases by 2 and angular frequency, hence maximum speed, decreases by 1/2. (c) The ratio k/m is unchanged, so period and maximum speed are unchanged. A possible test records multi-cycle period estimates against starting amplitude for the same mass and spring, with repeated trials and a specified release method. Keep the spring within its linear range. Timing resolution or a changing amplitude during the timed interval limits interpretation. A nearly flat table supports amplitude independence over the tested range; it does not establish it at all deformations.

The sinusoidal model now ties together a starting state, a time graph and a restoring force. Next we will follow energy through the motion and examine why a free oscillation can decay while a driven one can persist.
