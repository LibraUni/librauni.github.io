P101 · Unit 10 · Lesson 2

Forming an image

One ray tells us a direction. An image requires us to ask what happens to several rays from the same object point. A lens can bring them together, or make them leave as though they came from a different place.

Allow four hours: 96 minutes reading and examples, 90 minutes constructing and interpreting diagrams, and 54 minutes checking, correcting and revisiting the reasoning.

10.5 Rays from one object point

Consider a small illuminated arrow. Light from its tip travels in many directions. Some enters a lens; some misses it. To locate the image of the tip, follow several of the rays that enter. If the lens brings those rays to a common point beyond it, a screen placed there receives a concentrated contribution from the tip. Repeating this process for the other object points builds an extended image.

A converging lens makes rays initially parallel to its principal axis meet at a focus. The principal axis passes through the lens centre and the centres of curvature in our symmetric model. The focal length f is the distance from the lens to that focus. The same lens has a focus on either side: reverse the light’s travel and the construction reverses. We use positive f for a converging lens.

We will approximate a thin lens in air by a single vertical plane. This combines the refraction at its two surfaces into one change of direction. Our rays stay close to the axis and make small angles with it: this is the paraxial regime. Lens thickness is small compared with the relevant distances. These conditions make a single focal length and the simple construction useful; thinness alone does not eliminate every optical imperfection.

Three paths are particularly convenient. A ray arriving parallel to the axis leaves through the far focus. A ray through the centre continues straight in this thin-lens approximation. A ray arriving through the near focus leaves parallel to the axis. The first and third paths reverse one another. Two suitable rays locate an image point; a third provides a check.

Start all the construction rays at the same object point. Intersecting a ray from the top of an object with one from its bottom does not locate either point’s image. The selected rays are useful representatives of a whole bundle. They do not imply that light travels only along those two or three paths.

Activity 10.5 · Build the construction (25 minutes)

Use one square for 2 cm in both directions. Place a lens at horizontal coordinate zero, its foci at ±10 cm, and an upright 2 cm object at −30 cm. Trace a ray from its tip parallel to the axis and then through the far focus. Add a ray from the tip through the lens centre. Locate their meeting point and draw the image arrow. Keep this drawing for the next section.

Discussion of Activity 10.5

The rays meet at +15 cm, 1 cm below the axis. The image arrow therefore points downwards. Your drawing should show rays travelling from left to right through the lens, crossing below the axis. A third ray aimed through the near focus reaches the same point after leaving the lens parallel to the axis. Small drawing inaccuracies are expected; the algebra below gives an exact result within the model.

10.6 Locating a real image

Adopt a real-object convention: do>0 is the object’s distance in front of the lens. An image formed by rays meeting on the far side has di>0. Heights are signed relative to the axis: our upright object has positive ho, and an inverted image has negative hi. The distance signs specify sides in this convention; they are not ordinary unsigned ruler lengths.

The supplied thin-lens relation is

1f=1do+1di.

Use one length unit throughout. Each term has units of inverse length, so mixing centimetres and metres without conversion gives an invalid addition. Solve for the reciprocal image distance first, then invert. The transverse magnification describes the ratio of image height to object height:

m=hiho=−dido.

The central undeviated ray explains the distance ratio. The object and image form two right triangles with that ray and the axis. Their corresponding height-to-distance ratios have equal magnitudes. The image lies below the axis when the object lies above it, producing the minus sign. Magnification has no units; its sign records orientation and its magnitude records size.

Example 10.5 · Put a screen at the image

Check Activity 10.5 algebraically for f=10cm, do=30cm and ho=2cm.

1di=110cm−130cm=115cm.

Thus di=15cm. The magnification is −15/30=−0.50, giving hi=−1.0cm. Put a screen 15 cm beyond the lens to receive the inverted image. At a different screen position, the rays from the tip have not yet met or have already separated, producing a patch instead of a focused point.

This is a real image: the outgoing rays actually pass through the image point. A screen can intercept it. A camera sensor occupies such an image plane; focusing adjusts the geometry so that the desired object is imaged there. The distance to the focus is not automatically the image distance for every object. The focus receives rays parallel to the axis, which correspond to a very distant on-axis point.

Activity 10.6 · Move the object (25 minutes)

Keep the 10 cm focal length. Predict what changes when the object moves from 30 cm to 20 cm and then to 15 cm in front of the lens. Calculate image distance and magnification in each case, and add separate ray sketches. Explain where a screen must move and whether the image grows.

Discussion of Activity 10.6

At 20 cm, 1/di=1/10−1/20=1/20, so the image is 20 cm beyond the lens with m=−1. At 15 cm, 1/di=1/30, giving 30 cm and m=−2. As the object approaches the focus from outside it, the real image moves farther away and grows. All three images are inverted. The screen must follow the image plane; keeping it fixed would mix the effect of magnification with defocus.

10.7 A converging lens as a magnifier

Now move the object closer to the lens than its focal length. The lens still changes each ray’s direction towards the axis, but the incoming bundle spreads too strongly for the outgoing rays to meet on the far side. Instead, the outgoing rays diverge. Extend their straight paths backwards with dashed lines: those extensions meet on the object side. An eye receiving the rays sees them as coming from this apparent origin.

This is a virtual image. Light leaving the lens does not pass through the backwards intersection. A screen placed there cannot collect a focused image made by the outgoing bundle. The eye can nevertheless see the virtual image because its own optics collect the diverging rays and form a real image on the retina.

Example 10.6 · Interpret a negative image distance

Place an object 6.0 cm from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. Locate its image and find its magnification.

1di=110cm−16cm=−115cm.

Therefore di=−15cm. The minus sign places the virtual image 15 cm from the lens on the object side. Magnification is m=−(−15)/6=+2.5: the image is upright and 2.5 times the object’s height. The sign and size now match the backwards ray construction.

At the boundary between the real and virtual cases, do=f. The lens equation gives 1/di=0, which means there is no finite image distance. Rays from a given object point leave parallel to one another. We often describe this as an image at infinity. It does not mean that the image is at the lens, and dividing by zero is not a way to obtain a finite position.

Key idea 10.2 · Read the sign as geometry

For a real object and one converging thin lens in air: do>f gives a real, inverted image; 0<do<f gives a virtual, upright image. At do=f, there is no finite image plane. Use the ray construction to interpret the algebra.

Activity 10.7 · Test a magnifier claim (20 minutes)

A learner says that a converging lens always makes a bigger, upright image. Use this lesson’s examples to correct the claim. Then locate the image for a 10 cm lens with an object 5 cm away. Draw the outgoing rays and their backwards extensions using different line styles.

Discussion of Activity 10.7

The 30 cm object gave a smaller inverted real image, and the 15 cm object a larger inverted one. Upright enlarged images occur for objects inside the focal length in this model. At 5 cm, 1/di=1/10−1/5=−1/10, so di=−10cm and m=+2. Solid outgoing rays diverge; dashed extensions meet on the object side. Keep arrowheads on the solid light paths.

10.8 Image size and the limits of the model

A useful rearrangement of the thin-lens relation makes the limiting behaviour explicit. Multiplying by fdodi and collecting terms gives dodi=fdi+fdo, hence

di=fdodo−f,do≠f.

For an object much farther away than the focal length, the denominator is nearly do, so di approaches f. For an object close to the focus, the small denominator produces a large image distance. It also makes that distance sensitive to a small change in object position. This explains why focusing a close object can require a substantial adjustment.

Example 10.7 · Why a near-focus estimate is sensitive

A 10 cm lens views an object at 11 cm and then at 12 cm. Calculate the two real image distances.

The values are 10(11)/(11−10)=110cm and 10(12)/(12−10)=60cm. A 1 cm change in object position changes the required screen position by 50 cm. Near the focus, rounded measurements can therefore lead to a large uncertainty in the predicted image position. Reporting many calculator digits would not repair uncertain input distances.

The lens model brings rays from one point to a point. A real instrument produces a finite patch. Diffraction through the aperture, departures from the ideal lens shape, different refractive indices for different wavelengths, and imperfect focus can all broaden it. The term resolution concerns whether nearby features can be distinguished. Magnification concerns their displayed size. Enlarging an already blurred image enlarges its blur too.

Covering part of a lens also helps clarify how images are formed. Each object point sends a bundle through many parts of the lens. If a portion is blocked, the remaining aperture can still transmit rays from every visible object point. The image generally becomes dimmer rather than losing a matching half. Changing the aperture can also change diffraction and aberrations; the ideal ray model by itself predicts only the surviving paths.

Activity 10.8 · Explain the observation (20 minutes)

A camera makes a focused image of two nearby illuminated marks. One action enlarges the stored image on a screen; another refocuses the camera; a third covers half the lens. Explain which changes image display size, which changes where a point’s rays meet, and which reduces admitted light. Does any action by itself guarantee that the two marks become distinguishable? Give a reason.

Discussion of Activity 10.8

Digital enlargement changes display size. Refocusing brings the appropriate object plane into focus and can remove defocus blur. Blocking half the lens reduces the admitted bundle from each object point. None guarantees separation: enlargement cannot recover missing detail; refocusing cannot remove every resolution limit; blocking light can reduce signal and alters aperture effects. A useful answer distinguishes these causes instead of treating all improvements as “more magnification”.

We now have a geometrical account of image position, orientation and size. The next question is how much light reaches the image or detector. For that we return to energy conservation and the area over which power is spread.

Further reading and context
