P101 · Unit 10 · Lesson 3

Spreading and detecting light

A lens determines where an image forms. Its brightness also depends on the energy reaching the instrument. We will build an intensity model from a power budget, check it with Python, and connect it to the values a detector records.

Allow four hours: 72 minutes reading, 65 minutes written activities, 60 minutes Python and 43 minutes feedback and correction. Download the unit notebook or Python companion. Use Part A here and Part B in Lesson 4.

10.9 Power spread over area

Power is the rate of energy transfer. A source emitting 12 joules of light each second has radiant power 12 W. This describes light emitted, not necessarily the electrical power consumed by a lamp. Some input energy can leave by other routes or in wavelengths outside the range being considered.

Intensity I is power crossing unit area perpendicular to propagation. For uniform illumination across area A, I=Preceived/A, with units Wm−2. Thus the same received power spread over twice the area gives half the intensity. A small detector facing the incoming light receives approximately IA, provided intensity is nearly constant across its area. The distinction between the source’s total output and the detector’s received power will matter throughout this lesson.

Imagine a small, steady source emitting equally in every direction. This is an isotropic point-source model. Surround it with an imaginary sphere of radius r. If no light is absorbed or blocked on the way, the source’s entire radiant power P crosses that sphere. The sphere’s area is 4πr2, so equal sharing over that area gives

I(r)=P4πr2,r>0.

The sphere is a bookkeeping surface, not a physical shell that uses up light. The same power crosses a larger sphere farther out, but each square metre receives a smaller share. Doubling radius quadruples area and reduces intensity to one quarter. This is geometrical spreading.

Key idea 10.3 · Conditions belong to the inverse-square model

Use I=P/(4πr2) for a steady isotropic source, distances large compared with its size, and negligible absorption or obstruction. Distance is measured from the source. Intensity is in watts per square metre; received power is intensity times a small perpendicular collecting area.

The divergence at r=0 is a warning about the point-source idealisation. A real lamp has a finite emitting region, and “distance zero from a point source” is outside this physical approximation. A collimated beam also requires a different spatial model: power is concentrated into selected directions, so it is not uniformly distributed over the sphere.

Example 10.8 · From emitted power to collected energy

A 12 W isotropic source illuminates a small perpendicular detector 2.0 m away. Its collecting area is 2.0cm2. Find incident intensity and energy received in 0.50 s under the model.

I=124π(2.0)2Wm−2≈0.239Wm−2.

The area is 2.0×10−4m2: each centimetre contributes a factor of 10−2, so square centimetres contribute 10−4. Received power is IA≈4.77×10−5W. Multiplying by 0.50 s gives 2.39×10−5J. This is incident energy; an imperfect detector can absorb or record only a fraction of it.

Activity 10.9 · Keep the power budget straight (20 minutes)

Move the detector in Example 10.8 to 4.0 m without changing its area or the source. Find the new intensity and received energy for the same exposure. What happens to the total power crossing an entire sphere? Then double the detector area at 4.0 m: which quantities double?

Discussion of Activity 10.9

Intensity and received energy become one quarter: approximately 0.0597Wm−2 and 5.97×10−6J. Total power through the sphere remains 12 W. Doubling the small collecting area doubles received power and energy, but does not change the intensity at that distance. Distinguish a larger collector from a brighter incident field.

10.10 Distance comparisons and background

Often we know one intensity but not the source’s output. For the same unchanged source, divide the model at two distances. The factor P/(4π) cancels:

I2I1=(r1r2)2.

Write the distance ratio in the order that gives the expected result: a farther point must have lower intensity. If a distance is multiplied by three, intensity is divided by nine. To infer a distance factor from an intensity factor, undo the square using a positive square root, because distance is positive.

Example 10.9 · Use a ratio and state what it assumes

A source contribution is 0.80Wm−2 at 1.5 m. Predict it at 3.0 m and find the distance at which it would be 0.20Wm−2.

Doubling distance gives 0.80/4=0.20Wm−2. Conversely,

r2=r1I1I2=(1.5m)0.800.20=3.0m.

This inference assumes the same steady source, isotropic spreading and negligible losses. A dimmer source at the original distance could also produce a smaller intensity; intensity alone does not identify which changed.

A detector may also receive background light from the room or sky. For a simple model with a known additive background B, a recorded intensity-equivalent value is R=I+B. Ratios of R need not obey the source’s inverse-square ratio. First estimate the background using a comparable measurement without the target source contribution; then examine R−B. An uncertain or changing background limits that subtraction.

Activity 10.10 · A misleading ratio (25 minutes)

At distances 1 m and 2 m a synthetic detector gives 4.4 and 1.4 units. A separate background measurement gives 0.4 units at both positions with unchanged settings. Compare the raw ratio with the background-subtracted ratio. Does this establish an inverse-square law for every distance? State one additional test and one assumption.

Discussion of Activity 10.10

The raw far/near ratio is 1.4/4.4≈0.318. After subtraction it is (1.4−0.4)/(4.4−0.4)=1/4, matching the prediction for doubled distance. Two points are consistent with this model, not a universal test. Additional distances could test the shape; repeated background readings could test stability. The inference assumes a fixed source and comparable detector response. The stated synthetic units are not watts per square metre without a conversion model.

Spreading, absorption and a changing source can all reduce a recorded value. An inverse-square calculation is useful when its conditions are justified, and its failure can guide the next measurement. We now build a numerical prediction whose assumptions and checks are visible.

10.11 A checked intensity plot

Use distances from 1 to 5 m, well away from the excluded origin. In the code below, r_m stores distances in metres and power_w stores emitted radiant power in watts. NumPy’s elementwise operations evaluate the model at every distance. The guard rejects non-finite or non-positive distances rather than producing a plausible-looking plot from invalid inputs.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def intensity(power_w, r_m):
    r_m = np.asarray(r_m, dtype=float)
    if not np.isfinite(power_w) or power_w < 0:
        raise ValueError("Power must be finite and non-negative")
    if np.any(~np.isfinite(r_m)) or np.any(r_m <= 0):
        raise ValueError("Distances must be finite and positive")
    return power_w / (4 * np.pi * r_m**2)

r_m = np.linspace(1.0, 5.0, 81)
print("Benchmark at 2 m:", intensity(12.0, 2.0))
print("4 m / 2 m ratio:", intensity(12.0, 4.0) / intensity(12.0, 2.0))
plt.figure()
plt.plot(r_m, intensity(12.0, r_m), label="12 W source")
plt.xlabel("Distance from source / m")
plt.ylabel("Intensity / W m$^{-2}$")
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True)
plt.show()

np.asarray permits a single distance or an array; np.isfinite tests for ordinary finite numbers. The ~ reverses that Boolean test, and np.any asks whether any array element fails it. These guards check the numerical domain; you must still justify whether the distance is large enough for the physical point-source approximation.

The printed values should be about 0.238732 and 0.25. The first checks an absolute value independently calculated in Example 10.8. The second checks a ratio that must hold at any positive starting distance under this model. Together they can detect errors that a smooth curve might conceal, such as a missing factor or an incorrect exponent.

The grid contains 81 points and 80 intervals, giving a spacing of 0.05 m. Increasing the point count smooths the plotted curve but does not make the physical assumptions more accurate. Zero emitted power should produce zero intensity at every allowed distance. A very large distance should give a small positive intensity. These limiting checks examine whether the implementation behaves like the intended model.

Activity 10.11 · Make a reproducible comparison (60 minutes Python)

Run Part A of the notebook from a fresh start. Record the two benchmarks. Add a second curve for 24 W and predict its relationship to the first before running it. Then compare the 12 W model with an added constant background of 0.020Wm−2. Give the raw and background-subtracted 4 m/2 m ratios. Test zero power and an invalid zero distance. Finish with a labelled plot and two sentences distinguishing a code check from a test using measured light.

Discussion and checking criteria

Doubling power doubles the entire intensity curve. With background, the raw ratio is approximately 0.0796831/0.2587324=0.308; subtracting 0.020 from both restores 0.25. Zero power returns zero. Zero distance raises ValueError. A complete record states parameters, units and checks, retains the background value, and explains that agreement with hand calculations validates implementation of this model. A physical test needs measured readings, distances and controlled source/background conditions. The notebook contains a separate worked check after the attempt prompts.

10.12 From incident light to recorded values

A sensor translates incident light into a recorded quantity. That quantity might be an electrical signal or a digital count. Its numerical value depends on collecting area, exposure duration, efficiency and settings as well as the source. Without information about that response, a count cannot be identified directly with intensity in physical units.

For a steady incident intensity, a small perpendicular collector receives energy E=IAΔt over exposure time Δt. In a simple linear response range, doubling that energy doubles the source contribution to the record. A detector with a finite maximum can saturate: further incident energy then no longer produces a larger recorded value. A flat-topped signal may therefore be a recording limit rather than a flat-topped source.

An exposure also combines light arriving over a time interval. If brightness changes appreciably during that interval, the resulting value represents an average response rather than an instantaneous brightness. For equal short subintervals, a simple numerical exposure average is the arithmetic mean of their intensity values. This uses the averaging introduced earlier in P101. It is distinct from the cadence—the interval between successive recorded samples. A record can have frequent samples but exposures long enough to smear rapid variation.

Example 10.10 · Interpret an exposure

During four equal parts of one exposure, intensity is 2, 2, 6 and 6 units. Find the exposure-average intensity. Could the record distinguish that history from a steady intensity of 4 units?

The mean is (2+2+6+6)/4=4 units. A linear detector integrating over the full exposure gives the same result for the steady history. Shorter exposures within the interval could distinguish them, if their measurement precision were adequate. The full-exposure value alone has discarded the ordering and variation within that interval.

Activity 10.12 · Specify a fair comparison (20 minutes)

Two records of a lamp use the same detector. The second has twice the exposure duration and twice the source count above background. Explain why this does not establish that the lamp brightened. List the settings and background information needed for a useful comparison. Describe what saturation would do to the reasoning.

Discussion of Activity 10.12

A steady lamp already supplies twice the energy during twice the exposure, so a linear response predicts doubled source count. Compare counts per exposure time with the same collecting area, optical path and response settings, and with background treated using matched exposures. Saturated counts do not remain proportional to exposure or intensity; dividing by exposure time cannot recover the clipped signal. Keep raw values and settings so that a later reader can assess the comparison.

We have connected emitted power, incident intensity, collected energy and recorded values. Each step carries information and conditions. Lesson 4 asks how finite image width and finite sampling affect the distinctions we can make between possible sources.
