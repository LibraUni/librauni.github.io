P101 · Block 3 · Unit 9

Travelling waves and superposition

A short flick of a long rope sends a bend away from your hand. A small mark on the rope rises and falls as the bend passes, then settles close to where it began. How can a disturbance travel while the material carrying it stays nearby? And what happens when a second disturbance meets the first?

Unit 8 described an oscillation by following one position through time. We now extend that description to many positions. The extension introduces a new length, the wavelength, and a new distinction: the speed of a travelling pattern differs from the speed of a piece of the medium. A photograph and a time record can both look sinusoidal, yet their horizontal axes answer different questions. Learning to read them together will be our first task.

Once a single wave is described, we can ask how waves combine. In a suitable linear model, we add the disturbances at each position and time. This apparently modest rule explains both reinforcement and cancellation. When waves travel in opposite directions, it can produce a pattern with fixed quiet points and strongly moving regions. The boundary conditions then determine which patterns fit a stretched string.

The final part brings the ideal descriptions into contact with boundaries and obstacles. Reflection, transmission and diffraction show why a wave cannot always be treated as a narrow object following a single straight path. Comparing wavelength with an opening’s width will prepare you to judge when the ray descriptions of light in Unit 10 are useful.

A wider view · Patterns in the atmosphere

The atmosphere gives wave patterns a visible form when changing conditions produce or evaporate cloud droplets. The photograph offers a reason to keep asking which quantity an image records and which additional observations would reveal motion. Our rope model will establish the basic spatial and temporal descriptions before we encounter more complex media.

What you will learn to do

Read spatial and temporal wave graphs; connect wavelength, frequency and propagation speed; evaluate and add supplied travelling profiles; explain phase-dependent interference; locate nodes and antinodes with stated boundaries; and defend a controlled numerical investigation using hand checks and an explicit physical limitation.

Bring the amplitude, period, radian phase and sampling ideas from Unit 8, and the arrays, functions and labelled plotting methods from earlier P101 units. We will introduce the spatial phase factor and supply the trigonometric identity used to combine waves. No differential-equation method or Fourier transform is needed. Use paper alongside the notebook: a predicted sign or node is valuable evidence when a graph looks plausible.

Allow 18 hours, including three hours of Python. Lessons 1–3 each take four hours and contain one Python hour; Lessons 4–5 each take three hours. The final lesson includes the cumulative exercises, worked feedback, correction and reference use. Keep a short record of predictions, checks and revised explanations as you go. The unit closes with a compact web/PDF reference and an offline reading and notebook pack.
