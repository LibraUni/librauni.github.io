P101 · Unit 9 · Lesson 2

Adding waves

Two disturbances can reach the same piece of rope at the same time. That piece has one actual displacement. We now construct it by adding the displacements that each wave would produce separately, then examine how the relative timing controls the result.

Allow four hours: 70 minutes reading, 60 minutes written activities, 60 minutes Python and 50 minutes feedback and correction.

9.5 Adding at the same place and time

Suppose a right-moving pulse would raise a particular piece of rope by 3 mm at a chosen instant, while a left-moving pulse would lower it by 1 mm. With upward displacement positive, the contributions are +3 mm and −1 mm. Their sum is +2 mm. We must use the same position, time, direction convention and equilibrium reference for both contributions.

The principle of superposition states that, in a linear system, the total disturbance is the sum of the individual disturbances. For two transverse displacements it reads

y(x,t)=y1(x,t)+y2(x,t).

Linearity is a physical condition on the model. For the rope, small displacements and slopes allow an approximately linear description with nearly unchanged tension. Large disturbances may change the tension or other properties enough to invalidate simple addition. For a pressure wave, we add pressure changes relative to the same equilibrium pressure; we do not add the equilibrium pressure twice.

Example 9.3 · Add signed displacements

At three positions, one profile gives displacements +4, 0 and −4 mm. A second gives −1, +2 and +1 mm at the same instant. Find the resultant profile at those positions and explain why the two amplitudes alone are insufficient.

Pointwise addition gives +3, +2 and −3 mm. At the first and third positions, opposite signs reduce the magnitude; at the middle position the second wave supplies the entire displacement. Knowing only each wave’s maximum magnitude would not tell us its signed value at these positions. We must align the profiles before adding them.

If two pulses briefly cancel at one position, they have not necessarily disappeared. In the ideal linear model, each continues propagating, and later the pulses may separate again. Cancellation describes the total disturbance where they overlap. It is especially important not to infer that energy has vanished from a zero displacement: the medium can still be moving, just as an oscillator can pass through equilibrium with kinetic energy.

Activity 9.5 · What exactly is being added? (15 minutes)

At a point, two pressure changes are +6 Pa and −4 Pa relative to an equilibrium pressure of 100 000 Pa. Find the total pressure change and total pressure. Then explain the mistake in adding the two separate total pressures, 100 006 Pa and 99 996 Pa.

Discussion of Activity 9.5

The changes sum to +2 Pa, so the total pressure is 100 002 Pa. Adding the separate total pressures counts the same equilibrium background twice. Superposition here applies to the deviations from that background. The same care is needed when displacement records have different zero offsets.

9.6 Phase controls reinforcement

Consider two sinusoidal waves of equal amplitude travelling in the same direction with the same frequency and wavelength. Write the first phase as θ=kx−ωt, and let the second be ahead by a fixed phase difference δ. Then

y1=Acosθ,y2=Acos(θ+δ).

When δ=0, the crests and troughs coincide. Every value doubles, so the resulting amplitude is 2A: fully constructive interference. When δ=π, a crest of either profile aligns with a trough of the other. Since cos(θ+π)=−cosθ, the sum vanishes everywhere: fully destructive interference in this equal-amplitude idealisation.

Intermediate phases give partial reinforcement. The following supplied trigonometric identity lets us see both the size and phase of the resultant without solving a new physical equation:

cosu+cosv=2cos(u−v2)cos(u+v2).

Put u=θ and v=θ+δ. Cosine is even, so the sign of its half-difference argument does not matter. We obtain

y=2Acos(δ/2)cos(θ+δ/2).

The first factor is independent of position and time. The second varies between −1 and +1. The nonnegative resultant amplitude is therefore 2A|cos(δ/2)|. For the range 0≤δ≤π, the absolute-value bars can be omitted because the coefficient is nonnegative. A negative coefficient outside that range reverses the phase of the oscillation; it does not make its amplitude negative.

Example 9.4 · A quarter-cycle offset

Two waves each have amplitude 4 mm and phase difference π/2. Find the resultant amplitude. Compare it with the resultant displacement when the first wave is at its crest.

Ar=2(4mm)cos(π/4)=42mm≈5.66mm.

At the first wave’s crest, θ=0. The second displacement is zero, so the resultant displacement at that instant is 4 mm. The resultant reaches its own crest at a different phase, θ=−π/4. There both waves contribute 4/2 mm, giving the amplitude above. A single displacement value is not generally the maximum of the combined motion.

Complete cancellation also requires equal amplitudes. If one wave has amplitude 5 mm and the other 3 mm, a phase difference of π gives (5−3)cosθ mm. The remaining amplitude is 2 mm. At equal phase the amplitude is 8 mm. Relative phase alone does not determine the size without the individual amplitudes.

Activity 9.6 · Predict before changing phase (25 minutes)

For two equal 3 mm waves, predict the resultant amplitudes at phase differences 0, 2π/3 and π. Use the explorer to test these predictions, keeping time fixed first. Then keep phase fixed and change time. Explain which control changes the resultant amplitude and which moves the profile.

Explore phase and time. Both waves have amplitude 3 mm, wavelength 2 m and frequency 1 Hz; they travel right. Solid blue is the sum, dashed green wave 1, dotted brown wave 2.

Discussion of Activity 9.6

The amplitudes are 6, 3 and 0 mm. Relative phase changes the amount of reinforcement; time translates the fixed-phase resultant through space. At δ=2π/3, the coefficient is 6cos(π/3)=3 mm. At δ=π, numerical calculations may leave a minute rounding residual, but the exact sum is zero. The static Figure 9.3 supplies the three comparisons if the explorer is unavailable.

9.7 Two paths to an observation point

Phase differences can arise because sources start at different stages, or because their waves travel different distances. For a simple path comparison, assume two sources oscillate in step at the same frequency, the waves travel at the same speed, and the path lengths to a detector are r1 and r2. The wave that travels farther arrives later.

The travel-time difference is Δr/vw, where Δr=r2−r1. Multiplying by angular frequency gives the magnitude and signed scale of the phase difference associated with that delay:

kΔr=2πΔrλ.

At the detector, a delayed source signal written as cos[ω(t−r/vw)] has phase contribution −kr. Thus the second signal’s phase relative to the first is −kΔr. For deciding reinforcement versus cancellation, either sign gives the same cosine factor. An integer number of wavelengths means an integer number of complete cycles of delay and hence matching phase. A half-integer number means opposite phase.

Key idea 9.2 · Path difference under stated source conditions

For in-phase sources of the same frequency in a common medium, Δr=mλ gives matching phase, and Δr=(m+12)λ gives opposite phase, for integer m. Equal arriving amplitudes are needed for complete cancellation. A fixed relative source phase is needed for a stable pattern.

Example 9.5 · A detector between two phase conditions

Two in-phase sources send waves of wavelength 0.80 m to a detector. The paths are 2.0 m and 3.2 m. Determine their phase relation there. What changes if the longer path becomes 3.6 m, with all other assumptions retained?

Initially the path difference is 1.2 m, or 1.5 wavelengths. One complete cycle has no effect on relative stage; the remaining half-cycle gives opposite phase. Equal arriving amplitudes would cancel. With a 3.6 m path, the difference is 1.6 m, or two wavelengths, so the arrivals are in phase and reinforce. Their actual amplitudes may also depend on travel distance; a complete physical prediction must account for that separately.

Activity 9.7 · Diagnose an incomplete conclusion (20 minutes)

A report says, “The path difference is half a wavelength, so the detector must read zero.” Give two missing conditions and explain how each could change the result. Then predict the phase relation for in-phase sources when the path difference is 5λ/2.

Discussion of Activity 9.7

The sources must have the assumed stable phase relation; opposite-phase sources would acquire a further half-cycle delay and arrive in phase. The arriving amplitudes must be equal for zero resultant amplitude; unequal waves leave a residual. With in-phase sources, 5λ/2 is two full cycles plus a half-cycle, again giving opposite phase. The path comparison also assumes a common wavelength along those paths.

9.8 Compare two sampled profiles

Use the position array and travelling function from Lesson 1. Two arrays can be added element by element because their entries refer to the same positions and time. If one array were sampled at shifted positions, adding equal indices would combine different places. Matching array lengths alone is not enough.

A, wavelength, frequency = 0.003, 2.0, 1.0
x = np.linspace(0.0, 4.0, 401)
t, delta = 0.0, 2*np.pi/3
y1 = travelling(x, t, A, wavelength, frequency)
y2 = travelling(x, t, A, wavelength, frequency, phase=delta)
y = y1 + y2
expected = 2*A*np.cos(delta/2)*np.cos(
    2*np.pi*x/wavelength - 2*np.pi*frequency*t + delta/2)
print(np.max(np.abs(y - expected)))
for values, label in [(y1, "wave 1"), (y2, "wave 2"), (y, "sum")]:
    plt.plot(x, values, label=label)
plt.xlabel("Position x / m")
plt.ylabel("Displacement y / m")
plt.legend()
plt.grid()
plt.show()

The comparison uses two expressions for the same prediction: direct addition and the supplied identity. A near-zero difference checks implementation of that identity over the sampled positions. It does not test whether a real rope is linear; that requires physical observations. Keep numerical and physical checks distinct in your conclusion.

Activity 9.8 · Phase and amplitude investigation (60 minutes Python)

Run Part 2 of the notebook. Compare phase differences 0, 2π/3 and π, using the same axes and positions. Check the predicted amplitudes and one hand-calculated displacement in each case. Then, at phase π, change only the second amplitude from 3 mm to 2 mm. Predict the residual and test it. Repeat one comparison with twice as many spatial intervals, and explain the difference between the largest sampled magnitude and the exact amplitude.

Discussion of Activity 9.8

The equal-amplitude cases have amplitudes 6, 3 and 0 mm. At x=t=0, their displacements are 6, 1.5 and 0 mm. With the second amplitude reduced to 2 mm and opposite phase, the sum is a 1 mm cosine profile. Direct evaluation at a predicted maximum supplies an exact benchmark; the maximum of a finite grid can be slightly smaller if that point is absent. Increasing resolution should reduce that discrepancy without changing the analytic prediction. Retain the controlled parameter change, not just the final plot.

Same-direction waves produced another travelling profile. Next we reverse one direction while retaining equal frequency, wavelength and amplitude. The sum will then have positions that remain at zero throughout the motion.
