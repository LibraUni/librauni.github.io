P101 · Unit 9 · Lesson 5

Wave investigation and synthesis

A wave graph becomes evidence when its variables, assumptions and checks are clear. We will now turn the calculations from Lessons 1–3 into a short defensible conclusion, then use mixed exercises to connect propagation, interference and boundaries.

Allow three hours: 25 minutes for the investigation write-up, 100 minutes for the cumulative exercises, 40 minutes for solutions and correction, and 15 minutes for reference use and the conclusion. Use the notebook work already completed within the unit’s three Python hours.

9.16 A controlled wave investigation

A useful investigation begins with a question whose answer can change when one specified input changes. “What do waves do?” is too broad for a short notebook. “How does reducing the second amplitude affect cancellation at opposite phase?” is bounded: it identifies a parameter, an observable and a condition to retain. Your Lesson 2 calculation already contains the essential comparison.

Alternatively, you can investigate a fixed-end mode. Choose a string length and supplied speed, change the mode number, then compare predicted nodes with computed profiles. In that case frequency and wavelength must change together according to the boundary condition. A controlled comparison does not mean freezing quantities that the model requires to depend on the chosen parameter. It means distinguishing the independent choice from its consequences.

Activity 9.16 · Write an evidence-based conclusion (25 minutes)

Choose the phase/amplitude investigation from Activity 9.8 or the mode investigation from Activity 9.12. Write about 200–300 words using your existing plots and checks. State the question, model assumptions, independent change, quantities held fixed, prediction, numerical result and one physical limitation. Include one numerical hand check and identify a comparison that could test the physical model rather than just its implementation. Finish by stating what your evidence supports.

Sample approach and criteria for Activity 9.16

One approach uses two opposite-phase right-moving waves with amplitudes 3 and 3 mm, then 3 and 2 mm, keeping wavelength 2 m and frequency 1 Hz. The predicted residual amplitude changes from zero to 1 mm. At the origin and time zero, the second case gives 3−2=1 mm. The direct sampled sum agrees with that value and its cosine shape; doubling the spatial resolution leaves the prediction unchanged. This checks the program under the linear model. A real-string test would measure the separate and combined disturbances with common timing and sufficiently small amplitudes, then compare their signed values. Changing tension or nonlinear response could defeat the model.

A successful report makes the parameter change reproducible, gives a numerical check rather than only “the graph looks right”, separates sampling error from physical limitations, and draws a conclusion no broader than the evidence. For the mode route, the same criteria apply, with endpoint tests and a nonzero antinode check. A correctly identified failed boundary condition is a useful result.

When reviewing your report, look for places where an observation silently becomes a stronger claim. A flat snapshot is not evidence of permanent nodes everywhere. Agreement between two formulae checks algebra and implementation, but both can embody the same inaccurate physical assumption. Conversely, a well-chosen failure can be informative: a wavelength that violates an endpoint condition tells us exactly why that candidate is unsuitable.

9.17 Cumulative exercises

Attempt these questions before opening the solutions. The suggested times total 100 minutes; use the separate 40-minute correction allocation to compare reasoning, annotate errors and retry selected parts. All questions use methods taught in this unit. Keep units and the conditions of each model visible. Hints and full solutions are separate.

Exercise 9.1 · Two kinds of graph (4 minutes)

A snapshot has crests at 0.2, 0.8 and 1.4 m. A fixed-position record has maxima at 0.1, 0.4 and 0.7 s. Find wavelength, period, frequency and propagation speed. State what is still missing to determine propagation direction.

Hint for Exercise 9.1

Read spatial and temporal separations separately.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.1

The wavelength is 0.60 m and period 0.30 s. Thus f=1/0.30=10/3Hz and vw=0.60/0.30=2.0ms−1. A second snapshot or equivalent phase-tracking information is needed for direction; the listed separations give only the speed.

Return to Exercise 9.1

Exercise 9.2 · A model at two instants (6 minutes)

For y(x,t)=(0.006m)cos(kx−ωt), take k=4πradm−1 and ω=10πrads−1. Find wavelength, frequency and speed, then evaluate displacement at x=0.125m at times 0 and 0.05 s.

Hint for Exercise 9.2

Use the definitions of k and angular frequency before evaluating phase.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.2

λ=2π/(4π)=0.50m, f=10π/(2π)=5Hz, and speed is 2.5 m/s to the right. The initial phase is π/2, giving zero displacement. At 0.05 s the time contribution is also π/2, so the phase is zero and displacement is +0.006 m. The initial zero is a stage of local motion, not evidence of a node.

Return to Exercise 9.2

Exercise 9.3 · Track a crest (5 minutes)

The crest of Exercise 9.2 is initially at the origin. Find its location after 0.08 s. Repeat if the time term is positive. Explain why the initial profile cannot distinguish these directions.

Hint for Exercise 9.3

Hold one crest’s phase fixed.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.3

With the minus time term, kx−ωt=0 gives x=2.5t, hence +0.20 m. With the plus term, x=−2.5t, hence −0.20 m. At time zero both models reduce to the same cosine profile. Tracking a feature through time supplies the missing direction.

Return to Exercise 9.3

Exercise 9.4 · Sampling a spatial maximum (6 minutes)

A sinusoidal profile has an exact amplitude of 5 mm. At one time its nearest crest is at x=0.23 m, while the stored grid uses 0.1 m intervals. The largest sampled value is 4.96 mm. Explain the discrepancy, give two useful checks and state whether the result establishes damping.

Hint for Exercise 9.4

Distinguish evaluating a known function from sampling its maximum.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.4

The grid misses the crest, so its maximum can fall below the exact amplitude. Evaluate the formula at 0.23 m to check the predicted 5 mm, and refine the grid or move a sample onto the crest. Neither a missed maximum nor denser sampling establishes physical damping. Damping would need amplitude change with time or distance and evidence that it is not an observation effect.

Return to Exercise 9.4

Exercise 9.5 · Signed addition (4 minutes)

At the same three positions and time, two displacement profiles give (2, −3, 1) mm and (−5, 3, 4) mm. Find their sum. Is the zero result at one position enough to identify a standing-wave node?

Hint for Exercise 9.5

Add corresponding signed entries, then consider the definition of node.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.5

The sum is (−3, 0, 5) mm. The middle position is zero at this instant. A node must remain zero throughout the motion, so further times or a justified standing-wave expression are needed.

Return to Exercise 9.5

Exercise 9.6 · Amplitude and phase (7 minutes)

Two equal 2 mm waves share frequency, wavelength and propagation direction. Find resultant amplitudes for phase differences 0, π/2 and π. For the middle case, compare the amplitude with the displacement when the first component is at its positive crest.

Hint for Exercise 9.6

Use the half-phase coefficient, then evaluate the two components at the specified instant.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.6

The amplitudes are 4 mm, 22mm≈2.83mm, and zero. At the first component’s crest, the second is zero for the quarter-cycle offset, so the displacement is 2 mm. The resultant maximum occurs at another phase; a single instantaneous value is not the amplitude.

Return to Exercise 9.6

Exercise 9.7 · Imperfect cancellation (5 minutes)

Two same-direction waves with amplitudes 6 mm and 4 mm have equal wavelength and frequency. Write their sum when they are in phase and when they are opposite in phase, using the first phase θ. Give the amplitudes and explain why changing phase cannot make the second case exactly zero.

Hint for Exercise 9.7

At opposite phase, cosine changes sign.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.7

In phase the sum is 10cosθ mm, with amplitude 10 mm. Opposite phase gives 2cosθ mm, with amplitude 2 mm. Cancellation can remove only equal and opposite contributions; the amplitude mismatch leaves this minimum residual. Changing the phase away from opposition cannot compensate for unequal magnitudes throughout a cycle.

Return to Exercise 9.7

Exercise 9.8 · Two paths and one source condition (6 minutes)

In-phase sources produce a wavelength of 0.40 m. At detector P the path difference is 0.60 m; at detector Q it is 0.80 m. Compare phase relations. Then suppose one source is reversed in phase by π. What happens at P and Q? State a further condition for a zero reading.

Hint for Exercise 9.8

Count complete and half cycles of path delay; include the source phase.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.8

At P the difference is 1.5 wavelengths, so arrivals are opposite in phase; at Q it is two wavelengths, so they are in phase. Reversing one source swaps those relations: P becomes in phase and Q opposite. Equal arriving amplitudes are also required for complete cancellation. A stable relative source phase and the assumed common wavelength underpin this comparison.

Return to Exercise 9.8

Exercise 9.9 · Read a standing pattern (6 minutes)

Let y(x,t)=(0.008m)sin(5πx)cos(20πt), with x in metres and t in seconds. Find component wavelength, oscillation frequency, nodes and antinodes in 0≤x≤0.80m, and each travelling component’s amplitude.

Hint for Exercise 9.9

The product coefficient is twice the component amplitude.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.9

The wavenumber is 5π, giving wavelength 0.40 m; frequency is 10 Hz. Nodes are 0, 0.20, 0.40, 0.60 and 0.80 m. Antinodes are 0.10, 0.30, 0.50 and 0.70 m. The maximum local standing amplitude is 8 mm, so each equal travelling component has amplitude 4 mm.

Return to Exercise 9.9

Exercise 9.10 · A straight string is not necessarily at rest (5 minutes)

For Exercise 9.9, find the earliest positive time at which the entire string has zero displacement. Explain why that photograph does not show every point to be a node or establish zero mechanical energy.

Hint for Exercise 9.10

Set the time factor to zero and recall an oscillator crossing equilibrium.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.10

The first zero of cosine is at 20πt=π/2, hence t=0.025s. The spatial factor has permanent zeros only at the node positions. Other points are crossing equilibrium and can have kinetic energy. At time zero, the antinodes have nonzero displacement, which directly distinguishes them from nodes.

Return to Exercise 9.10

Exercise 9.11 · Infer the mode (7 minutes)

A fixed-end string is 0.90 m long. A standing pattern has two interior nodes and oscillates at 150 Hz. Determine its mode number, wavelength, propagation speed, fundamental frequency and antinode locations.

Hint for Exercise 9.11

Include both endpoints when counting nodes.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.11

Two interior nodes mean three lobes: n=3. The wavelength is 2L/3=0.60m; speed is 150×0.60=90ms−1; the fundamental is 50 Hz. Nodes are 0, 0.30, 0.60 and 0.90 m; antinodes lie halfway between them at 0.15, 0.45 and 0.75 m.

Return to Exercise 9.11

Exercise 9.12 · Reject a boundary model (7 minutes)

A proposed profile is y(x,t)=Bsin(3πx2L)cos(ωt), with nonzero B. Can it represent a string fixed at both x=0 and x=L? Give a decisive time and endpoint check, then propose two valid spatial factors.

Hint for Exercise 9.12

At time zero the cosine multiplier is one.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.12

The origin is always zero, but at x=L and t=0 the displacement is Bsin(3π/2)=−B, violating the far fixed end. Valid examples are sin(πx/L) and sin(2πx/L), whose arguments at L are integer multiples of π. Testing only a time when the cosine multiplier vanishes would conceal the defect.

Return to Exercise 9.12

Exercise 9.13 · Reflection and return (5 minutes)

An upward transverse pulse travels 2.4 m to an ideal fixed end at 4 m/s. Describe the reflected pulse and find the return time to the launch point. How does the description change for an ideal free end?

Hint for Exercise 9.13

The return journey has two legs; boundary sign and travel speed have different roles.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.13

The fixed-end reflection is a downward pulse travelling back. The round trip takes 2(2.4)/4=1.2s. At the ideal free end the returned pulse is upward; the return time is unchanged under the same speed assumption. The endpoint constraints determine inversion, while distance and propagation speed determine timing.

Return to Exercise 9.13

Exercise 9.14 · A transmitted wavelength (6 minutes)

A 10 Hz wave of wavelength 0.60 m reaches a stationary boundary. Its speed beyond the boundary is 4.0 m/s. Find the incident speed and transmitted wavelength. A report claims the frequency must fall because the wave slows. Explain the error.

Hint for Exercise 9.14

The steady source continues imposing the same cycles per second at the boundary.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.14

The incident speed is 10×0.60=6.0ms−1. The transmitted frequency remains 10 Hz, giving λ2=4/10=0.40m. The wave advances less distance between successive cycles; that changes wavelength rather than the boundary’s driving frequency. This conclusion assumes the stationary linear boundary and steady sinusoid used in the lesson.

Return to Exercise 9.14

Exercise 9.15 · An opening in wavelength units (8 minutes)

Waves of speed 0.24 m/s pass through a 0.06 m gap in a uniform medium. Compare frequencies 2 Hz and 12 Hz: find wavelength and gap-to-wavelength ratio, predict relative spreading, and explain why broader spreading need not mean greater transmitted power. Propose a controlled comparison.

Hint for Exercise 9.15

Calculate wavelength before comparing the gap widths in wavelength units.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.15

At 2 Hz, wavelength is 0.12 m and a/λ=0.5. At 12 Hz, wavelength is 0.02 m and a/λ=3. The lower-frequency wave should spread more broadly. Angular distribution differs from total power; coupling through the opening and source output can affect power. Keep medium, gap and source geometry fixed, document source amplitude, and compare angular patterns using the same distance and detection criterion. If the supplied speed depends on frequency in a real medium, measure or model that dependence.

Return to Exercise 9.15

Exercise 9.16 · Audit a numerical claim (13 minutes)

A notebook evaluates a standing wave only at t=T/4, gets zeros at every grid position, and concludes: “All points are nodes, the string has no energy, and the program proves the model of my instrument.” Write a corrected interpretation and a short validation plan. Include a nonzero benchmark, a boundary check, a sampling check and a physical observation that would test the model.

Hint for Exercise 9.16

Ask which conclusions follow from a zero time multiplier and which need independent evidence.

Worked solution to Exercise 9.16

At T/4 the cosine time factor is zero everywhere, so this is an equilibrium-crossing snapshot. Nodes are the zeros of the spatial factor that persist at all times. Other points may be moving and carrying kinetic energy. Test a predicted antinode at t=0, where the displacement should be ±2A, and test both endpoints at several nonzero time factors. Compare direct addition with the separated product, then refine the spatial grid and sample additional phases. Finally compare measured separate/combined displacements or measured node locations with the model under documented boundary and amplitude conditions. Reproducibility and agreement between implementations support a calculation; independent observations test its suitability for the instrument. A good answer separates all four forms of evidence and states one remaining limitation.

Return to Exercise 9.16

Consult · Python

Metres, seconds and radians; tolerance below is for these synthetic metre-scale values.

import numpy as np
x = np.linspace(0, 2, 401)       # metres
A, lam, f, t = 0.003, 2.0, 1.0, 0.0
k, omega = 2*np.pi/lam, 2*np.pi*f
phase = k*x - omega*t
y1 = A*np.cos(phase)
y2 = A*np.cos(phase + np.pi)
y = y1 + y2
assert np.allclose(y, 0, rtol=0, atol=1e-12)
# Standing product: equal sine components
standing = 2*A*np.sin(k*x)*np.cos(omega*t)

What the unit has connected

The rope question at the start asked how a disturbance could travel while its material remained nearby. Our two-input description supplied the answer: position labels which part of the medium is observed, while displacement describes its changing state. Wavelength and period measure different repetitions, joined by the speed at which a phase of the pattern advances.

Adding disturbances introduced a second level of description. Reinforcement and cancellation depend on signed values at the same place and time. Relative phase explains why a sum can grow, shrink or vanish, and opposite propagation directions explain how moving contributions can create a stationary pattern. Fixed endpoints then turn the possible spatial patterns into a discrete family of modes.

Boundaries and openings made the assumptions consequential. A fixed end requires cancellation of transverse displacement; a stationary transmitting boundary preserves frequency while allowing wavelength to change; an opening must be judged relative to the wavelength to anticipate spreading. These are choices of physical description, not merely formula substitutions.

Your notebook and corrected exercises now provide several ways to challenge a prediction: follow a crest, check a signed value, test an endpoint, change one independent parameter, or refine the sampling. In Unit 10, light will give these ideas another setting. We will ask when a ray description captures the useful geometry and when wave behaviour and observational limits must also be considered. TMA 03 follows Unit 10 as a separate assessment.
