P101 · Unit 9 · Lesson 1

From oscillation to propagation

The rope’s bend travels, but a mark on the rope moves mainly across its direction of travel. To describe both motions clearly, we need a position coordinate for the medium and a displacement for its disturbance. Keeping these roles separate will make the wave formula much easier to interpret.

Allow four hours: 75 minutes reading, including the unit introduction; 55 minutes written activities; 60 minutes Python; and 50 minutes checking and correction.

9.1 Local motion and travelling change

Imagine a long rope stretched horizontally. Let x label distance along its undisturbed length, measured from the left-hand end. Let y be a small vertical displacement from that straight equilibrium line. We take upward displacement as positive. A mark whose equilibrium position is x=2m may have different values of y at successive times. Its label x remains the same in this small-displacement model.

A flick raises the nearby rope. Tension in the sloping rope exerts transverse forces on neighbouring pieces, setting them moving; those pieces then influence their neighbours. The disturbance spreads through the interaction between adjacent parts and their inertia. It takes time to travel. A wave is this propagation of a disturbance through space. One isolated disturbance is a pulse; a repeated drive can generate a succession of similar crests and troughs.

For a transverse wave, the local displacement is perpendicular to propagation. The rope is our working example. In a longitudinal wave, local displacement is parallel to propagation. In a small-amplitude sound wave in air, particles move back and forth while regions of compression and rarefaction travel. A graph of pressure against distance can have peaks and troughs even though the air does not form a wavy-shaped line. Always establish what the vertical coordinate represents.

Propagation can transfer energy from the source to a distant receiver. The receiving part begins moving because neighbouring parts do work on it. This does not require each piece of material to travel from source to receiver. Real water surfaces have more complicated local trajectories and can also have currents; the rope model gives us a particularly clean way to isolate the distinction between local motion and propagation.

Activity 9.1 · Follow a mark, then a crest (15 minutes)

Sketch three successive views of a small upward pulse moving right along a rope. Mark the same piece of rope in every view, with the pulse first to its left, then centred on it, then to its right. Describe the mark’s motion and the crest’s motion separately. Repeat the distinction in words for a longitudinal compression pulse.

Discussion of Activity 9.1

The rope mark starts near equilibrium, rises as the pulse reaches it and returns as the pulse leaves. The crest advances rightwards through successive pieces of rope. For the longitudinal case, a particle moves along the propagation direction and back, whereas the region of compression advances through different particles. Both descriptions need a spatial pattern and the local response through time.

9.2 A snapshot and a time record

A photograph taken at one instant gives y at many values of x. That is a spatial profile. A sensor fixed at one position records y at many values of time t. That is a time record, like the oscillation graphs of Unit 8. The two graphs below describe the same ideal right-moving wave. Read their horizontal axes before comparing their shapes.

In the upper graph, successive crests are 2 m apart. This distance is the wavelength, λ. More generally, one wavelength takes us to the nearest repetition of the whole spatial cycle: crest to crest, trough to trough, or a zero crossing to the next crossing with the same slope. Adjacent zero crossings are only half a wavelength apart. In the lower graph, successive maxima at the fixed position are 1 s apart. This is the period T, so the frequency is 1 Hz.

During one period, one crest takes the place occupied by the crest ahead of it. The pattern has advanced one wavelength. If the wave retains its shape and has constant propagation speed vw, distance divided by time therefore gives

vw=λT=fλ.

Key idea 9.1 · Two axes, two repetitions

Wavelength λ is a distance between matching stages in a snapshot; period T is a time between matching stages at one position. Their connection is vw=λ/T. Here vw is a positive speed; a separate direction specifies propagation.

Units provide a useful check: hertz times metres gives metres per second. They do not, however, distinguish propagation speed from a material particle’s speed, since both have the same unit. On the rope, the pattern travels horizontally while the mark moves vertically and reverses direction each cycle. The mark is momentarily at rest at a crest even while that crest continues to advance.

Example 9.1 · Combine two observations

A snapshot has crests at 0.30, 1.10 and 1.90 m. A fixed sensor has maxima at 0.20, 0.70 and 1.20 s. Find wavelength, frequency and propagation speed. What extra information determines direction?

The repeated spatial separation is 0.80m; the repeated time separation is 0.50s. Thus

f=10.50s=2.0Hz.
vw=fλ=1.6ms−1.

The observations determine the size of the speed. A second nearby snapshot that tracks a crest would distinguish leftward from rightward travel. Its timing should be short enough to identify the same crest rather than accidentally choose a neighbouring repetition.

Activity 9.2 · Audit the axes (20 minutes)

A spatial graph crosses zero at 0.1, 0.4 and 0.7 m, with slopes positive, negative and positive respectively. A time record at one position repeats every 0.20 s. Determine wavelength, frequency and wave speed. Explain why 0.3 m and 0.20 s cannot both be called periods. Sketch both graphs with labelled axes and one complete repetition marked.

Discussion of Activity 9.2

Matching slopes occur at 0.1 and 0.7 m, so λ=0.6m. The 0.3 m separation is half a spatial cycle. The period is 0.20 s, giving 5 Hz and vw=3ms−1. Period describes a time interval. The profile’s horizontal axis is position, so its repetition is described by wavelength. A clear sketch marks one rising crossing to the next on the spatial graph and one matching event to the next on the time graph.

9.3 A travelling sinusoidal model

Unit 8 used a phase angle to locate a point within an oscillation. For a wave, phase also changes with position. One full wavelength must advance the spatial phase by 2π radians. We therefore define the wavenumber k=2π/λ, in radians per metre. The angular frequency remains ω=2πf, in radians per second. Multiplying by position and time respectively produces phase angles.

A useful supplied model for a right-moving wave is

y(x,t)=Acos(kx−ωt+ϕ).

The notation y(x,t) says that displacement depends on two inputs. Set t to one value and vary x to obtain a snapshot. Set x to one value and vary t to obtain a local time record. The amplitude A sets the displacement scale; ϕ sets the chosen initial spatial phase. The equilibrium displacement has been set to zero.

Why does the minus sign produce rightward travel? Follow a particular crest. Its phase stays at the same multiple of 2π. Over a short interval, keeping that phase fixed requires

kΔx−ωΔt=0,ΔxΔt=ωk=fλ.

Since k and ω are positive, increasing time requires increasing x. Replacing the minus sign by a plus sign gives Δx/Δt=−ω/k, so the pattern travels left. This reasoning follows a feature of the pattern rather than a piece of rope.

Example 9.2 · Read and follow a model

Take A=0.010m, λ=2.0m, f=1.0Hz and ϕ=0. Find the displacement at x=0.50m, t=0.25s, and follow the crest initially at x=0.

The phase constants are k=πradm−1 and ω=2πrads−1. At the stated point, the spatial and temporal contributions cancel:

kx−ωt=π2−π2=0,y=0.010cos0=0.010m.

The zero-phase crest satisfies x=(ω/k)t=2t when metres and seconds are used. It moves from 0 to 0.50 m in 0.25 s. At the fixed position x=0, displacement instead changes from +0.010 m to zero during that interval. These are different paths through the same formula.

Activity 9.3 · Change one sign (20 minutes)

For Example 9.2, replace −ωt by +ωt. Evaluate the displacement at the same point and time. Find the location after 0.25 s of the crest initially at the origin. Does the snapshot at t=0 distinguish these two propagation directions? Explain.

Discussion of Activity 9.3

The phase at the chosen point is π, giving −0.010m. The zero-phase crest now follows x=−2t, reaching −0.50 m. Both models give Acos(kx) at time zero, so that snapshot alone is identical. Direction emerges from the way the profile changes with time.

We are supplying this model, not deriving it from the forces in a particular rope. It represents small sinusoidal disturbances with fixed amplitude, wavelength and speed in a uniform region. Damping, changing tension and reflections can require additional terms or different parameters. For the next calculation, the model provides a known prediction against which to check code.

9.4 Check a profile in Python

An array of positions now plays the role that an array of times played in Unit 8. Evaluate the formula at every position using one fixed time. The function below accepts a scalar or NumPy array for position, while the other inputs have explicit physical meanings. Use metres, seconds and radians.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def travelling(x, t, A, wavelength, frequency, phase=0.0, direction=1):
    if wavelength <= 0 or frequency <= 0 or A < 0:
        raise ValueError("Use positive wavelength/frequency and nonnegative A")
    if direction not in (-1, 1):
        raise ValueError("direction must be +1 or -1")
    k = 2*np.pi/wavelength
    omega = 2*np.pi*frequency
    return A*np.cos(k*np.asarray(x) - direction*omega*t + phase)

x = np.linspace(-1.0, 3.0, 401)
for t in (0.0, 0.25):
    plt.plot(x, travelling(x, t, 0.010, 2.0, 1.0), label=f"t = {t} s")
plt.xlabel("Position x / m")
plt.ylabel("Displacement y / m")
plt.legend()
plt.grid()
plt.show()

The sign convention is encoded by direction: +1 gives the minus time term and hence rightward travel; −1 gives leftward travel. The 401 points enclose 400 intervals over 4 m, so the spacing is 0.01 m. A 2 m wavelength spans 200 intervals. This is much finer than the few hand-check positions, making the drawn curve useful while retaining exact locations to test.

Activity 9.4 · A profile you can defend (60 minutes Python)

Use Part 1 of the unit notebook, or copy the code above. Before running it, predict the crest shift and the values at (x,t)=(0,0),(0,0.25),(0.5,0.25). Check those values directly with the function. Plot the two snapshots, then repeat with direction=-1. Finally halve the spatial spacing and explain whether the predicted crest shift changes. Keep the hand checks and labelled plots together.

Discussion of Activity 9.4

The rightward crest shift is 0.50 m. The three values are 0.010 m, zero and 0.010 m, up to floating-point rounding at zero. The leftward model shifts by −0.50 m and has −0.010 m at the third point. With 801 positions the spacing is 0.005 m; the model’s shift is unchanged. A denser grid improves the drawing, not the exact model’s speed. A suitable check is np.isclose(travelling(0.5, 0.25, 0.010, 2.0, 1.0), 0.010, rtol=0, atol=1e-12). The tolerance is in metres and is chosen for these small synthetic calculations.

A sampled maximum may miss the exact crest when the grid does not contain its position. Checking the formula at a predicted crest therefore answers a different question from asking where the largest sampled value occurs. Retain that distinction when two profiles are added next: a persuasive plot needs numerical checks at meaningful positions.
