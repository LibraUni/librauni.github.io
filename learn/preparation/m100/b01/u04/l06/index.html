SECTION 1 · ABOUT 42 MINUTES

Plan a mixed solution

An auditable solution lets another reader follow the route from inputs to conclusion. State the model and units, identify unknowns, solve with restrictions, check the original relations, and interpret the result. A calculation is not complete merely because software prints a number. Select a method that matches the problem: substitution for a single isolated unknown, elimination for paired linear constraints, or inequality reasoning for a permitted range.

Worked example

Two exact records give s = 8 m at t = 1 s and s = 14 m at t = 3 s under s = s₀ + vt. Subtraction gives v = 3 m/s and then s₀ = 5 m. Both records check. This establishes parameters for the supplied model, not proof that real motion is always uniform.

Work it through

Show intermediate steps and give units or restrictions where relevant. For code, record a prediction before running.

  1. List the two unknowns and their units in the example.
  2. Write the two original equations before subtracting.
  3. Check the recovered values in both.
  4. State one limitation of the conclusion.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. s₀ in metres and v in metres per second.
  2. s₀ + v × 1 s = 8 m; s₀ + v × 3 s = 14 m.
  3. 5 + 3 = 8 and 5 + 9 = 14, in metres.
  4. The conclusion depends on exact supplied measurements and a constant-velocity model.

If an answer differs, locate the first step where your reasoning changes. Correct that step, close this feedback, and solve it again independently.

SECTION 2 · ABOUT 48 MINUTES

An auditable algebraic argument

Mixed work tests whether you can choose a method without the question naming it. Begin by identifying the kind of statement. Preserve exact fractions where convenient, state excluded denominators, and verify candidates in originals. A contradiction or a family can be a correct answer. For inequalities, check whether an endpoint belongs.

Worked example

The relation 2x + y = 9 paired with x − y = 3 gives 3x = 12, hence x = 4, y = 1. If a separate constraint requires y > 1, that pair fails: satisfying the equations does not automatically satisfy an additional bound.

Work it through

Show intermediate steps and give units or restrictions where relevant. For code, record a prediction before running.

  1. Solve 2(x − 1) = x + 5.
  2. Simplify (3x² − 6x)/(3x), retaining restrictions.
  3. Solve x + y = 8 and 2x − y = 1.
  4. Solve 7 − 2x ≤ 3.
  5. Classify x + y = 2 and 3x + 3y = 7.
  6. Make x the subject of y = ax + b and state the zero-coefficient cases.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. x = 7.
  2. x − 2, x ≠ 0.
  3. x = 3, y = 5.
  4. x ≥ 2.
  5. No solution: subtraction yields 0 = 1.
  6. x = (y − b)/a for a ≠ 0; if a = 0, every x when y = b, otherwise none.

If an answer differs, locate the first step where your reasoning changes. Correct that step, close this feedback, and solve it again independently.

SECTION 3 · ABOUT 60 MINUTES

Assertions that check a claim

An assert statement checks a condition and stops with AssertionError if it is false. If true, it produces no output. Use it for a specific expected property, with an explanatory message after a comma. Here the values are small integers, so exact equality is appropriate. Do not treat exact float equality as a general numerical accuracy test. An assertion checks what you wrote; a mistaken expected value remains a mistaken check.

Worked example

The loop checks the model s = 5 + 3t at three explicit times. The assertion verifies the supplied observation at t = 1. Changing the expected result to 9 deliberately fails; restore the correct check before the final clean run.

Type, predict, run

initial_m = 5
speed_m_s = 3
times_s = [0, 1, 3]
for time_s in times_s:
    position_m = initial_m + speed_m_s * time_s
    print(time_s, position_m)
assert initial_m + speed_m_s * 1 == 8, "Check the first observation"

Work it through

Show intermediate steps and give units or restrictions where relevant. For code, record a prediction before running.

  1. Type and run the example, predicting the three positions.
  2. Deliberately change 8 to 9 in the assertion, read the error, then restore it.
  3. Add an exact check at t = 3.
  4. Explain why absence of an AssertionError does not validate every physical assumption.
  5. Restart, run in order and save.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. 5, 8 and 14 at t = 0, 1 and 3.
  2. AssertionError reports the failed condition with the supplied message.
  3. assert initial_m + speed_m_s * 3 == 14, "Check the second observation"
  4. The assertion covers a particular computed relation, not the model’s real-world validity.
  5. The final notebook should run cleanly with the intentional failure documented in text.

SECTION 4 · ABOUT 30 MINUTES

Bring relations and constraints together

Combine recovered parameters with a stated operating bound. With s = 5 + 3t and t ≥ 0, requiring s ≤ 20 gives 0 ≤ t ≤ 5 s. Check the boundary in the original relation and report the interval with units. A table of a few times can illustrate the result, but the inequality gives the continuous interval between them as well.

Worked example

At t = 5 s the coordinate is 20 m and is allowed; at t = 6 s it is 23 m and fails. The lower bound is supplied by the experiment’s non-negative elapsed time, not derived from s ≤ 20.

Work it through

Show intermediate steps and give units or restrictions where relevant. For code, record a prediction before running.

  1. Find times for s < 20 under the same model.
  2. Find times for 8 ≤ s ≤ 17 with t ≥ 0.
  3. Check both endpoints of the second interval.
  4. Describe which parts of the conclusion came from data, algebra and assumptions.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. 0 ≤ t < 5 s.
  2. 1 ≤ t ≤ 4 s.
  3. At 1 s, s = 8 m; at 4 s, s = 17 m.
  4. The observations determine parameters within the assumed model; algebra derives the bounds; constant velocity and t ≥ 0 are assumptions/domain conditions.

SECTION 5 · ABOUT 60 MINUTES

Block review and TMA preparation

This checkpoint closes Block 1 and prepares your TMA, which has its own separate six-hour allocation. Attempt the mixed questions before opening feedback. Review any weak area with a fresh problem, not by copying a solution. For the TMA, retain handwritten reasoning, a clean executable notebook and clear filenames. The formal questions are separate from these practice examples. Completion ticks record your reading; they do not award a grade or establish mastery automatically.

Worked example

A final audit asks: Are quantities defined? Are transformations valid? Are restrictions stated? Do checks use originals? Are conclusions proportionate to the evidence? Is the notebook reproducible from a restart?

Work it through

Show intermediate steps and give units or restrictions where relevant. For code, record a prediction before running.

  1. Evaluate 3/4 − 1/6 exactly.
  2. Convert 1800 cm² to m².
  3. Simplify 4(x − 2) − (x + 1).
  4. Solve x/2 + 1 = 5.
  5. Solve x + y = 5 and x − y = −1.
  6. Solve −2x ≤ 6 and state the interval.
  7. Explain what an assertion and a counterexample can each establish.
  8. Open TMA 01 and plan its six-hour allocation separately from this lesson.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. 7/12.
  2. 0.18 m².
  3. 3x − 9.
  4. x = 8.
  5. x = 2, y = 3.
  6. x ≥ −3, or [−3, ∞).
  7. An assertion checks a specified condition in an execution; a permitted counterexample disproves a universal claim. Neither automatically establishes physical validity.
  8. The TMA page specifies question groups, marks, time, permitted help and submission files. Do not treat this practice checkpoint as your formal submission.
