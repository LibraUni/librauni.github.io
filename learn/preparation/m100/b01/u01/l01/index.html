SECTION 1 · AVAILABLE · ABOUT 20 MINUTES

Before we begin: read, predict, explain

A useful calculation gives you more than an answer. It gives you a reason to trust that answer.

In physics, numbers describe something: a position, a time, an amount of energy. A small arithmetic slip can change that description. Our first task is to build a dependable habit: read the calculation carefully, predict something about its answer, and explain the steps you choose.

You do not need to be quick. You need to be able to follow your own reasoning. If these first activities feel familiar, use them to practise writing clearly. If something feels rusty, that tells us where to begin.

What you need

Paper and a pen, or somewhere you normally write your working. Try the three short activities without a calculator first. Python comes later in this lesson; you do not need to open it now. Your written attempts are for your own review here—this page does not submit answers or record completion.

Where this lesson is going

  1. Read, predict, explain — start with familiar arithmetic.
  2. Signed quantities — distinguish position, direction and distance.
  3. Signs and operations — make sense of negative numbers in calculations.
  4. Operation order — decide which calculation comes first.
  5. Python — type, run and check your own calculations.
  6. Review — explain what you can now do and what needs practice.

Sections 1–3 are published so far. The complete lesson has a four-hour study allocation, spread across its sections; this section uses about 20 minutes of it.

Three short starting points

Allow roughly ten minutes, including reading the feedback. For each activity, write an answer and a short explanation before opening the feedback. A sentence or a few labelled steps is enough. These are starting checks, not a test of speed or a graded assignment.

ACTIVITY 1 · PUT NUMBERS IN ORDER

Which comes first?

Arrange 3, 11 and 7 from smallest to largest. How did you decide?

Read the feedback after your attempt

The order is 3, 7, 11. Three is smaller than seven, and seven is smaller than eleven. In a counting sequence, these numbers occur in that order.

If you wrote 11 before 7 because its first digit is 1, compare the whole values: 11 contains a full ten and one more. We will extend number ordering to values below zero in the next section.

ACTIVITY 2 · SUBTRACT AND CHECK

What remains?

Calculate 9 − 4. Write a different calculation that checks your answer.

Read the feedback after your attempt

9 − 4 = 5. One check is 5 + 4 = 9: adding back the amount removed recovers the starting value.

This check uses the relationship between subtraction and addition. It gives you a different route to the same conclusion, rather than merely copying the original subtraction again. Counting back four steps from nine is another way to obtain the result.

ACTIVITY 3 · READ THE GROUPING

What do the brackets ask you to do?

Calculate 3 × (2 + 4). State which operation you do first.

Read the feedback after your attempt

The brackets group 2 + 4. Calculate that sum first: it is 6. Then multiply by 3, giving 18.

If you obtained 10 by doing 3 × 2 + 4, you evaluated a different expression. The brackets make the multiplication apply to the whole sum. We will develop operation order carefully later in this lesson; this activity simply shows whether that idea needs refreshing.

A model of clear reasoning

Now consider 4 × (7 − 2). Compare an answer on its own with a short, checkable explanation:

Read → predict → calculate → check

  1. Read: the brackets group 7 − 2, so I evaluate that difference before multiplying.
  2. Predict: 7 − 2 is positive and smaller than 7. Multiplying it by 4 should give a positive result smaller than 28.
  3. Calculate: 7 − 2 = 5, then 4 × 5 = 20.
  4. Check: four groups of five give 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20. The result also satisfies the prediction.

The prediction would not prove that 20 is correct: many positive numbers are smaller than 28. Its role is to catch some implausible answers. The calculation and the second check provide more specific evidence. Different checks do different jobs.

You will not need four long sentences for every calculation. A simple task may need only one clear line. What matters is that your working makes the choice of operations visible, especially when the result is surprising or a mistake is possible.

Your five-minute finishing activity

Choose one of your three earlier answers. Rewrite it so that someone else can identify what you calculated, why you chose those steps, and how you checked it. If you changed an answer after reading the feedback, keep the original attempt and add the correction with a reason.

Check the quality of your explanation
  • Have I answered the expression that was actually given?
  • Can another reader follow the order of my steps?
  • Does my check add information, rather than just repeat my answer?

For the ordering activity, an explanation of the comparisons is enough; do not invent an elaborate numerical check. Clear reasoning should suit the task.

Before you move on

You should now be able to distinguish an answer from an explanation and a check. A correct result after feedback is useful learning, but it is not yet evidence that you can solve every similar problem independently.

If ordering, subtraction or brackets caused difficulty, note which one. The next sections will build the relevant ideas step by step. You can bring your attempt to a tutorial whenever you want help explaining a step.

SECTION 2 · AVAILABLE · ABOUT 40 MINUTES

Position, direction and distance from zero

Two places can be equally far from a starting point and still be different places. Signed numbers let us keep that distinction.

In the previous section you ordered positive whole numbers and explained your calculations. Now we extend the number line below zero. By the end, you should be able to locate and compare signed numbers, identify opposite numbers, and explain the difference between a position and its distance from zero.

Allow about 15 minutes for the explanations and worked examples, and 25 minutes for the activities and feedback. Use paper for your sketches. No calculator or Python is needed here.

Choose a reference point

Imagine a straight track with equally spaced markers. We choose one marker as zero, called the origin. We also choose a direction in which numbers increase. In the diagram, that direction is to the right.

Starting at zero, the next markers to the right are +1, +2, +3 and so on. To the left they are −1, −2, −3. A plus sign is often omitted: +3 and 3 mean the same number. The minus sign in −3 is part of the number’s notation. Here it identifies a negative value; it is not asking you to perform a subtraction.

The same point on a track could receive a different coordinate if we moved the origin or reversed the chosen direction. Nothing physical would have moved: we would have changed the description. Once the reference is fixed, use it consistently.

What a negative coordinate does—and does not—tell you

If the scale is in metres, a point at −3 metres is three metres on the negative side of the origin. Its coordinate does not tell you whether an object there is moving, or which way it is moving. A position is not a direction of motion. The arrow on Figure 1 labels increasing values, not an object’s journey.

Order follows position

On this number line, a number to the right is greater than a number to the left. This rule applies on both sides of zero. For example, −3 is smaller than −1: −3 lies farther left.

We write “is smaller than” with < and “is greater than” with >. The pointed end faces the smaller value. Thus −3 < −1 and −1 > −3 say the same thing. Equality, =, says that two expressions name the same value.

Worked example 1 · Order three signed numbers

Arrange −2, +1 and −4 from smallest to largest.

  1. Locate the two negative numbers to the left of zero: −4 is farther left than −2.
  2. Place +1 to the right of zero, so it is greater than either negative number.
  3. Read from left to right: −4 < −2 < +1.

Do not order negative numbers by ignoring their signs. Four is greater than two, but −4 is smaller than −2.

Opposites and absolute value

Two numbers are opposites when they are equally far from zero on opposite sides of it: −3 and +3, for example. Zero is its own opposite. It is neither positive nor negative.

The absolute value of a number is its distance from zero on the number line. We write it using a vertical bar on each side:

  • |−3| = 3: the distance from −3 to zero is three units.
  • |+3| = 3: the distance from +3 to zero is also three units.
  • |0| = 0: zero is zero units from itself.

Read |−3| as “the absolute value of minus three”. The bars are not brackets asking you to change operation order; they name a particular operation: taking distance from zero. An absolute value is always non-negative, meaning zero or positive. Saying it is “always positive” would incorrectly exclude zero.

Worked example 2 · Which is farther from zero?

Compare the positions −4 and +2.

For numerical order, −4 < +2. For distance from zero, |−4| = 4 and |+2| = 2. So −4 is the smaller number, but it is farther from zero.

These statements are compatible because they answer different questions: “Which number is greater?” and “Which point is farther from the origin?”

Practise the distinctions

Allow roughly 20 minutes for these four activities and their feedback, then five minutes for the final error analysis. Draw small labelled number lines where useful; you do not need an elaborate illustration.

ACTIVITY 1 · LOCATE AND DESCRIBE

Make the reference visible

Sketch a number line from −4 to +4 with equal spacing. Mark zero, label the increasing direction, and mark P at −3 and Q at +2. Describe where each point lies relative to zero. Does either coordinate tell you which way an object is moving?

Compare your diagram and explanation

Your sketch should have the arrangement in Figure 1. P is three intervals to the left of zero; Q is two intervals to the right. Neither coordinate gives the direction of motion. Check that you counted intervals between ticks, not the ticks themselves.

ACTIVITY 2 · COMPARE

Keep the sign attached

Put −1, +3, −4 and 0 in increasing order. Then complete each comparison with <, > or =: −2 … −4; 0 … −1; +3 … 3.

Read the ordering feedback

−4 < −1 < 0 < +3. Next: −2 > −4, because −2 is farther right; 0 > −1; and +3 = 3. The optional plus sign does not change a positive value.

ACTIVITY 3 · DISTANCE FROM ZERO

What do the bars mean?

Evaluate |−4|, |+2| and |0|. Which of the original three numbers is greatest? Which is farthest from zero? Explain why the answers differ.

Read the absolute-value feedback

The absolute values are 4, 2 and 0. The greatest original number is +2; the point farthest from zero is −4. Numerical order follows left-to-right position, whereas distance ignores which side of zero the point occupies.

ACTIVITY 4 · REVERSE THE QUESTION

One distance, how many positions?

Which numbers have absolute value 3? Which numbers have absolute value 0? Could a number have absolute value −2? Justify each answer using the number line.

Read the reasoning

−3 and +3 both have absolute value 3. Only 0 has absolute value 0. No real number has absolute value −2: a distance cannot be negative. You do not need an equation-solving method to answer; locate points at the specified distance.

Find the mistake

A learner writes: “−4 is greater than +2 because it is four units from zero, and four is greater than two.” Explain which two ideas have been confused, and rewrite the statement correctly.

Check your correction

The learner has compared distances from zero and used that comparison to claim an ordering of the original numbers. Correctly: −4 is smaller than +2, but −4 is farther from zero. Both the position and the distance need to remain visible in the explanation.

Optional targeted practice · if negative-number ordering still feels uncertain

Use these in place of repeating comparable items from Activities 2 and 3: compare −1 with −3, then compare their distances from zero. Finally, identify the opposite of −1. Sketch before checking.

Check the targeted practice

−1 > −3, but |−1| = 1 < 3 = |−3|. The opposite of −1 is +1. If this distinction is still unclear, bring the two sketches to a tutorial; further practice may take additional time.

Carry these ideas forward

You should be able to explain a signed position, order numbers across zero and interpret absolute value as distance from zero. Keep any corrected attempt with a short reason. This page does not submit work or mark the unit complete.

SECTION 3 · AVAILABLE · ABOUT 45 MINUTES

A sign is not always a subtraction

In −3 − (−2), the three minus signs do different jobs. Reading those jobs before calculating makes the result much easier to explain.

Section 2 gave us positions, opposites and distances from zero. Here we use those ideas to add, subtract, multiply and divide signed numbers. Allow about 15 minutes for the explanations and three worked examples, and 30 minutes for practice and feedback. Work by hand; no calculator or Python is needed.

First read what each sign is doing

In −3, the minus sign identifies a negative number. In 5 − 3, it tells us to subtract one number from another. More generally, putting a minus sign in front of a number means take its opposite: −(−3) is +3.

A sign acting on one value is called unary; an operation combining two values is called binary. You do not need to memorise the terminology to calculate, but it helps describe the difference. In −3 − (−2), the first and last minus signs belong to the numbers −3 and −2. The middle minus sign is the subtraction operation. The parentheses make the second number easy to read.

Read aloud before applying a rule

Read −3 − (−2) as “negative three, subtract negative two”. A phrase such as “two minuses make a plus” is incomplete: it does not tell you which operation is being performed. For example, adding two negative numbers still gives a negative result.

Add a directed change

On a number line increasing to the right, adding a positive number moves right; adding a negative number moves left. The distance moved is the absolute value of the number added. Start at the first number, then apply the change.

Worked example 1 · Cross zero without losing the sign

Calculate −2 + (+5).

  1. Start at −2.
  2. Add +5: move five unit intervals to the right.
  3. Two intervals take you to zero; three more take you to +3.

Thus −2 + (+5) = +3. Check by undoing the change: starting at +3 and adding −5 returns you to −2.

Adding a negative number follows the same idea: −2 + (−3) = −5. You start left of zero and move three more intervals left. The minus sign in the result describes the final position, not the number of intervals travelled.

Subtract by adding the opposite

A number and its opposite add to zero: −3 + (+3) = 0. This is why the opposite is also called the additive inverse. Subtracting a number means undoing addition of that number. We can do that by adding its opposite.

  • Subtract +3: add −3 instead.
  • Subtract −3: add +3 instead.
  • Subtract 0: add 0, so nothing changes.

Worked example 2 · Same starting point, different operation

Compare −2 + (−3) with −2 − (−3).

The addition is −5, as above. For the subtraction, replace “subtract −3” with “add the opposite of −3”:

−2 − (−3) = −2 + (+3) = +1.

Check by adding the subtracted number back: +1 + (−3) = −2. This returns the starting value, as it should. Subtraction does not always make a number smaller.

A useful connection to physics

Suppose an object starts at position −2 metres and finishes at +1 metre, using the same origin and positive direction. Its displacement—final position minus initial position—is +1 − (−2) = +3 metres. The positive sign says that the net change is in the positive direction.

Those two positions do not tell us the total distance travelled: the object could have moved back and forth along the way. Here we are calculating a change in position, not reconstructing the journey.

Why multiplication has sign rules

The symbol × means multiplication. A positive whole-number multiplier can be read as repeated addition: 3 × (−2) means three copies of −2, giving −6. Reversing the order of two factors does not change their product, so (−2) × 3 is also −6.

A negative multiplier cannot literally mean “a negative number of copies”. To understand it, we preserve a familiar property of arithmetic: multiplying a sum gives the same result as multiplying each part and adding the products. For instance, 2 × (3 + 4) and (2 × 3) + (2 × 4) both give 14. This property is called distributivity. We use a numerical example here; its algebraic form comes in Unit 3.

Now consider (−2) × (3 + (−3)). The numbers in the inner parentheses add to zero, so the product is zero. Multiplying the two parts separately must give the same result:

0 = ((−2) × 3) + ((−2) × (−3)).

The first product is −6. The second must therefore be +6 to bring the sum to zero. Thus (−2) × (−3) = +6. The positive answer makes the two ways of calculating agree.

For non-zero factors, the rule is: matching signs give a positive product; different signs give a negative product. Multiply the absolute values to find its magnitude. Multiplying any number by zero gives zero, which is neither positive nor negative.

Division asks a multiplication question

The symbol ÷ means division. To calculate 12 ÷ 3, ask: “What number, multiplied by 3, gives 12?” The answer is 4. The same check works with signed numbers.

Worked example 3 · Choose the sign, then check

Calculate (−12) ÷ (−3).

The magnitude is 12 ÷ 3 = 4. The answer must be positive: (+4) × (−3) = −12. Therefore (−12) ÷ (−3) = +4.

Try the other sign as a check: (−4) × (−3) would give +12, not the required −12. Division by a non-zero number follows the same sign pattern as multiplication.

Division by zero is undefined. For 6 ÷ 0, no answer can pass the multiplication check: every number multiplied by zero gives zero, never six. For 0 ÷ 0, every number would pass, so there is no unique answer. Neither expression defines a quotient in ordinary arithmetic. This is different from 0 ÷ 3 = 0, which checks correctly because 0 × 3 = 0.

Practise, then explain

Spend about 20 minutes on these six calculations and their feedback. Write at least one line explaining the sign or checking the result. If these are already familiar, use the time to make your explanations precise rather than repeating calculations.

Six signed calculations

  1. −4 + (+7)
  2. +2 + (−6)
  3. −3 − (−5)
  4. (−4) × (−3)
  5. (−18) ÷ (+3)
  6. Compare 0 ÷ (−4) with (−4) ÷ 0. Which has a value?
Compare your reasoning
  1. +3. Start at −4 and move seven intervals right: four to zero and three beyond it.
  2. −4. Start at +2 and move six intervals left. Check: −4 + (+6) = +2.
  3. +2. Subtracting −5 adds its opposite: −3 + (+5) = +2. Check: +2 + (−5) = −3.
  4. +12. Both non-zero factors are negative, so the product is positive. Its magnitude is 4 × 3 = 12.
  5. −6. The signs differ. Check: (−6) × (+3) = −18.
  6. 0 ÷ (−4) = 0. Zero multiplied by −4 gives zero. (−4) ÷ 0 is undefined: no number multiplied by zero gives −4. An undefined quotient is not a negative number or a very large number.

Find the first mistaken idea

Allow about 10 minutes for these two explanations and their feedback.

“Two negatives make a positive”

A learner writes −3 + (−2) = +5, explaining that “two negatives make a positive”. Identify the operation, correct the result, and explain why the slogan fails here.

Read the correction

This is addition, not multiplication. Start at −3 and add a change of −2, giving −5. The two numbers have the same negative sign, so both contributions point left. A positive product from two negative factors does not imply a positive sum from two negative terms.

Does a smaller divisor always work?

A learner observes that 6 ÷ 3 = 2 and 6 ÷ 1 = 6, then concludes that 6 ÷ 0 must be an extremely large number. Explain the error using multiplication. Contrast it with (−6) ÷ 3.

Read the correction

The pattern cannot supply a quotient at zero. Whatever finite number is proposed, multiplying it by zero gives zero, not six. Thus 6 ÷ 0 is undefined. In contrast, (−6) ÷ 3 = −2, a perfectly valid negative result: (−2) × 3 = −6. A negative answer and an undefined operation are different things.

Optional targeted practice · choose only the difficulty you encountered

These replace comparable practice above; they are not another compulsory set.

  • Subtraction: rewrite 1 − (−2) as addition, then check your answer by adding −2 back.
  • Sign rules: compare (−3) + (−3) and (−3) × (−3). Explain why the signs of the results differ.
  • Zero: compare 0 ÷ 5 and 5 ÷ 0 using the multiplication check.
Check the targeted practice

1 − (−2) = 1 + 2 = 3; 3 + (−2) returns 1. The sum of −3 and −3 is −6, but their product is +9. Finally, 0 ÷ 5 = 0, whereas 5 ÷ 0 is undefined. If the reasoning remains unclear, bring the relevant line of working to a tutorial.

Carry the distinctions forward

Return to the opening expression: −3 − (−2) = −3 + (+2) = −1. You can now explain each minus sign and check the result: −1 + (−2) = −3.
