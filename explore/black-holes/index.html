A QUESTION WORTH FOLLOWING / 01

At the edge
of no return.

What happens at the edge of a black hole?

There is no solid surface to hit. The extraordinary change is in what your future can contain: beyond the horizon, no light signal you send can reach the outside universe.

Put light to the test ↓

01 / THE SHORT ANSWER

A boundary in possibility.
Not a wall in space.

Imagine falling freely towards a very large, quiet black hole, carrying a torch. Outside its event horizon, there are directions in which light can escape. Inside, even a beam aimed directly outwards moves towards smaller radii. You can still shine the torch locally; you cannot send its light back home.

Crossing the horizon need not produce a sudden local jolt. In classical general relativity, a freely falling observer crosses it in a finite time on their own watch. Whether the journey is survivable depends on tidal forces and the surroundings. A bright, hot accretion flow would be a very different environment from the empty model we explore here. What this model includes — and what the graphs mean

We use a spherical, non-rotating, uncharged Schwarzschild black hole of fixed mass. We omit surrounding matter, back-reaction and evaporation. Real black holes usually rotate. The illustration at the top is original artwork; the graphs here calculate specified relationships, not an astronaut’s visual experience.

Light: radius is expressed as x = r/rs, and time as T = ctPG/rs. The horizon radius is rs=2GMc2; G is the gravitational constant, M the mass and c the speed of light. The horizon-crossing time coordinate tPG is called Painlevé–Gullstrand time. In these coordinates the radial light paths satisfy:

dxdT=±1−1x

The plus sign is initially outward; the minus sign is inward. The slope is a coordinate rate, not a local measurement of light’s speed. Every local freely falling observer still measures light travelling at c. Traces stop before the central singularity, where this description cannot settle the physics.

Clocks: the ratio is 1−1x, for a clock held at fixed radius outside the horizon compared with a stationary clock infinitely far away. Holding position requires thrust; no stationary clock exists at or inside the horizon. This is not the ticking rate of a falling astronaut.

Tides: the leading radial relative acceleration across a small freely falling separation L is Δa≈2GMLr3. We use L = 2 m. The separation must be small compared with the curvature scale; this is not a model of a human body or a survival threshold. Here G is the gravitational constant, M the black-hole mass, r the radius and L the radial separation. The plotted difference is an acceleration in m/s². Geometry and clock reference ↗

03 / WHAT THE EXPERIMENTS ARE TELLING YOU

01

The horizon changes
the way out.

A spacetime diagram keeps track of both where something is and when it is there. Going upwards means going into the future. A path leaning right reaches larger radii; one leaning left reaches smaller radii. A vertical path stays at the same radius.

Outside the horizon, the outward light path leans right. On the horizon it stays there. Inside, even that path leans left. A material traveller cannot outrun light to find an extra escape route. This is why the horizon is more than a place with a particularly strong pull. Causal structure explained ↗

The horizon is also distinct from the central singularity: the place at zero radius where this classical model predicts unbounded curvature. General relativity does not establish what a complete quantum description would replace that singularity with.

Key idea

The event horizon separates events that can send signals to the distant exterior from those that cannot.

02

Your own watch
keeps ticking.

“Time stops at the horizon” mixes together different questions. A falling observer’s watch measures their own elapsed time. A distant observer instead receives light signals, whose arrival times and frequencies change during the fall. The falling source fades and redshifts; it does not remain as a bright, permanently frozen photograph.

The clock experiment asks a simpler question: how do two clocks held at fixed positions compare? Move the inner clock towards the horizon and its rate relative to the faraway one decreases. At the horizon the experiment becomes impossible: a rocket cannot hold that position. The display therefore says “No hovering clock”, rather than inventing a stopped watch.

Activity BH.1 · Read a clock comparison

At four horizon radii, the ratio is about 0.866. If the faraway clock records 60 seconds, how much time does the hovering clock record?

Show reasoning

Multiply 60 by 0.866: about 52.0 seconds. This compares elapsed times for the specified stationary clocks. It does not mean that the nearby observer feels their own seconds running slowly.

03

Bigger can mean
gentler at the edge.

Tides arise because neighbouring freely falling objects accelerate differently. A small radial separation tends to stretch; transverse separations tend to compress. This differential effect, rather than an observer feeling their own free fall as a weight, is the starting point for “spaghettification”.

Keep the position at one horizon radius and switch from 10 Suns to a million Suns. The tidal difference falls dramatically. More mass means a larger horizon: the horizon radius grows in direct proportion to mass. Substituting that into the tide formula makes the tidal difference at the horizon proportional to one divided by mass squared.

Worked insight · change the scale

Multiply the mass by 10. At the same number of horizon radii, the physical radius also multiplies by 10. In M/r³, the numerator gains a factor of 10 and the denominator a factor of 1,000. The tidal difference becomes 100 times smaller. At a fixed physical distance, that conclusion would not apply. NASA on tidal forces ↗

04 / FROM EQUATIONS TO EVIDENCE

We see what happens
around the darkness.

The glowing ring is emission from matter near the black hole. The central dark region is its shadow, shaped by light capture and bending. It is larger than the event horizon; the photograph does not show a solid rim that a traveller would strike. About this observation ↗

Our opening artwork suggests a luminous accretion disc. Such a disc is surrounding material, not part of the horizon. Gravitational lensing can bring the far side of a disc into view above and below the dark centre. Not every black hole has a bright disc. Explore the anatomy with NASA ↗

Making sense of an image like this needs more than an eye for a pattern. You need models of radiation and gravity, and methods for deciding which conclusions the data support. That is why the route through the degree includes both theory and investigation.

05 / READING THE GLOW

One disc.
More than one way to see it.

Look back at the large amber black-hole animation at the top of this page. Its bright horizontal band and sweeping upper and lower arches suggest a black hole surrounded by luminous gas. To read that picture, separate three things: where the gas is, how its light reaches us, and how we choose to draw the result.

Where does the light come from?

The glow comes from material outside the horizon. Gas can orbit in a flattened structure called an accretion disc; as it moves inwards, some of its gravitational energy becomes heat and radiation. We are seeing the surroundings of the black hole, not light escaping from its interior. A black hole without much nearby emitting material would not wear this luminous band. NASA: the accretion disc ↗

Why does the disc seem to bend upwards?

General relativity describes light following the straightest possible paths through curved spacetime. Seen across that geometry, those paths bend: gravitational lensing changes the direction from which the light arrives. A camera assigns light to a position in its image according to that arrival direction. The emitting gas can therefore appear somewhere other than where a straight-line picture would put it.

The arches are images of the disc.

Viewed nearly edge-on, the disc contributes a horizontal band. Bent light also reveals parts of its far side above and below the dark centre. Those arches need not be gas physically rising into hoops. Closer to the shadow, light taking longer routes around the black hole can produce further, increasingly narrow images, often discussed as photon rings. The broad arches and these fine rings are different features. NASA’s visual explanation ↗

What is moving?

Gas orbits, and bright structures within it change and shear because different parts of the flow move differently. Lensing transforms those changing patterns into the image we see. A travelling bright patch in a movie is therefore not necessarily a single photon circling along a visible hoop. In our animation, moving highlights simply suggest this activity; their paths and speeds are designed, not calculated from gas dynamics. Gas motion in a physical simulation ↗

Would it really be golden and symmetrical?

Not necessarily. The approaching side of a rotating disc can appear brighter than the receding side through relativistic Doppler boosting. Viewing angle also changes the appearance. Radiation need not peak in visible light, and visualisations may assign colours to make structure readable. NASA explains the brightness and colour choices ↗

How to read our illustration

The mirrored arcs, amber palette, dark central shape and flowing highlights echo the LibraUni logo. They are an artistic introduction to a lensed accretion disc, not a ray-traced prediction of a particular black hole. The dark centre suggests the shadow; its outline does not measure the event horizon. Matching the upper and lower arcs is a design choice, not a rule that real observations must obey.

Activity BH.2 · Does the upper arch mean gas is above the black hole?

Before opening the discussion, distinguish the position of the emitting material from its apparent position in an image.

Show discussion

No. Light from the far side of a flattened disc can be redirected so that it arrives from above the dark centre in the observer’s image. The arch alone does not establish a separate hoop of gas above the disc. To infer the material’s location, we need a model of the light paths as well as the image.

AND THE QUESTIONS BEYOND?

Quantum physics enters when the question changes.

You do not need quantum mechanics or string theory to explore a classical horizon. Questions about Hawking radiation, entropy and quantum gravity require further preparation: P201 quantum/thermal foundations and P302 · Quantum & atomic physics, together with appropriate gravitational theory. Those foundations do not amount to a course in quantum fields on curved spacetime.

A late conceptual introduction to string theory is a planning proposal, with P302 the preferred candidate. Technical string theory would need a separately designed optional pathway. Neither is required to make sense of this page, and neither is being presented as released teaching.
