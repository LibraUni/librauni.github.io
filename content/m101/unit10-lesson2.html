M101 · Unit 10 · Lesson 2

Displacement and distance

A velocity graph records direction as well as speed. Keeping its signs tells us how far the finishing position lies from the starting position; removing those signs before accumulation tells us how much ground was covered along the way.

Three hours: 75 minutes reading and reconstructing examples, 50 minutes activities, 35 minutes feedback and correction, and 20 minutes reserved for the closing exercises.

10.5 Recovering position from velocity

Consider motion along a straight track with a fixed positive direction. Write s(t) for the position coordinate and v(t) for the signed velocity component in that direction. The supplied kinematic relationship is s′(t)=v(t). A positive velocity increases the position coordinate; a negative velocity decreases it. If position is in metres and time in seconds, velocity is in metres per second.

Over a short time interval, the position changes by approximately velocity times elapsed time. Adding those small signed changes makes all intermediate positions cancel: only the last position minus the first remains. The integral expresses the limiting accumulation, and the fundamental theorem gives

s(b)−s(a)=∫abv(t)dt.

The left-hand side is the displacement from time a to time b. To obtain the final position, add the known starting position. In particular, the integral alone cannot locate the object relative to an origin that has not been specified.

Example 10.4 · From a rate to a position

A trolley has numerical velocity v(t)=2t−4 in m/s, where t is numerical time in seconds on [0,5]. At t=0, its position is 3 m. Find its position at any time in this interval and at the final time.

Use a separate integration variable u so that the upper endpoint t keeps its role as the time at which we want the position:

s(t)=3+∫0t(2u−4)du=3+t2−4t.

This expression gives position in metres. At the starting time it returns 3; its derivative returns 2t−4. At t=5, the position is 8 m, while the displacement is 8−3=5 m. These two checks test both the rate and the initial condition.

Initially v(0)=−4 m/s, so the trolley starts towards decreasing coordinates even though its position is positive. Position and velocity have separate signs with separate meanings.

Activity 10.5 · An initial position matters

A second trolley follows the same velocity model but starts at −6 m. Find its position function, its final position and its displacement. Describe how its position graph differs from the first trolley's graph.

Discussion of Activity 10.5

Its position is s(t)=−6+t2−4t, so it finishes at −1 m and has displacement 5 m. Its entire position graph lies 9 m below the first graph. The velocity and displacement are unchanged, because a constant shift of position has derivative zero.

10.6 Reversals and distance

Return to the first trolley. It moves towards decreasing coordinates until 2t−4=0, at t=2, then towards increasing coordinates. During the first part, its signed change is negative but its distance travelled is positive. During the second part, both are positive. To add the lengths of both parts, we integrate the speed |v(t)|.

Key idea 10.2 · Motion along a line

On a forward time interval a<b, for continuous velocity,

Δs=∫abv(t)dt,D=∫ab|v(t)|dt.

The displacement Δs is signed; the distance D is nonnegative. Always D≥|Δs|. To evaluate distance, split at velocity sign changes and add the magnitudes of the separate position changes.

Example 10.5 · Count both legs

Find the distance travelled by the trolley in Example 10.4 over five seconds.

At the reversal, s(2)=3+4−8=−1 m. The trolley first travels from 3 m to −1 m, a length of 4 m, then from −1 m to 8 m, a length of 9 m. Thus its distance is 13 m. The integral calculation records those same two legs:

D=−∫02(2t−4)dt+∫25(2t−4)dt=4+9=13 m.

The net displacement is still 5 m. The 4 m travelled towards decreasing coordinates cancels 4 m of the later travel in the signed total. Distance keeps both contributions. The check 13≥|5| passes.

The position graph gives another route to the same answer: add the absolute changes between successive turning points and endpoints. This works because the motion has one direction between those points. Reading only the first and last positions would lose the excursion to −1 m.

Activity 10.6 · A return to the start

For the same trolley, restrict attention to 0≤t≤4. Predict the displacement from the velocity graph, then calculate the displacement, distance and final position. Explain how a zero answer can coexist with motion.

Discussion of Activity 10.6

The equal triangular contributions cancel, so displacement is zero. The two legs each have length 4 m, giving distance 8 m. The trolley finishes at its starting position, 3 m. A zero displacement compares endpoints; it does not say the intermediate positions were unchanged.

10.7 A zero is not always a reversal

Finding where velocity vanishes locates possible direction changes. To decide whether there is a reversal, check its sign on both sides. This is the same distinction used in Lesson 1 between a root that crosses the axis and a root that merely touches it.

For example, suppose v(t)=(t−1)2 m/s on [0,2]. The trolley stops instantaneously at t=1, but velocity is positive before and after that time. Its position keeps increasing, with a horizontal tangent at 1. The distance equals the positive displacement, ∫02(t−1)2dt=2/3 m. There is no negative piece to reverse.

Example 10.6 · Two reversals

A particle's numerical velocity is v(t)=3(t−1)(t−3) m/s on [0,4], with position zero initially. Find the displacement, distance and positions at the reversals.

The zeros are at 1 s and 3 s. The factor signs show positive velocity before 1, negative between 1 and 3, then positive after 3. Expanding gives v(t)=3t2−12t+9, so the position is s(t)=t3−6t2+9t. It has the required derivative and initial value.

The four endpoint and turning-point positions are s(0)=0, s(1)=4, s(3)=0 and s(4)=4, in metres. Therefore the displacement is 4 m, while the distance is

D=|4−0|+|0−4|+|4−0|=12 m.

The position has a local maximum at 1 s and a local minimum at 3 s, consistent with the velocity sign changes. Checking those signs avoids treating every stationary point in a position graph as a reversal.

Activity 10.7 · Stop or turn?

A model gives v(t)=(t−1)2(t−3) m/s for 0≤t≤4. Identify the zero-velocity times and which are reversals. Write a correct distance integral without evaluating it. State where the position increases and decreases.

Discussion of Activity 10.7

The zeros are 1 and 3. The squared factor is nonnegative and positive except at 1; the factor t−3 is negative before 3 and positive after. Thus 1 is an instantaneous stop without reversal; 3 is a reversal. The distance is −∫03v(t)dt+∫34v(t)dt. Position decreases until 3, with a horizontal tangent at 1, then increases. Splitting additionally at 1 is harmless if both earlier integrals retain the same minus sign.

10.8 Read and check a journey

A trustworthy motion solution connects three representations: the velocity signs, the position changes, and the geometry of the graphs. Start by recording the time interval and positive direction. Solve for possible reversals within that interval, inspect signs, then calculate the required endpoint changes. This order makes the interpretation visible before a lengthy expression obscures it.

Changing the positive direction changes both s and v to their negatives if the origin stays fixed. Displacement then changes sign, but speed and distance do not. Moving only the origin adds a constant to position and leaves velocity unchanged. These coordinate choices provide useful consistency checks on any physical interpretation.

The time bounds for a distance travelled run from earlier to later. Reversing the bounds of an integral is an algebraic operation that changes its sign; it does not create a negative travelled distance. If a question asks about a return journey, use the velocity during that journey with its own increasing time interval.

Activity 10.8 · Diagnose three reports

For Example 10.6, a report says: (a) “The distance is 4 m because the particle finishes at 4 m”; (b) “The particle reverses whenever its position is zero”; (c) “Choosing the opposite positive direction makes its distance −12 m.” Correct each statement, giving the relevant mathematical reason.

Discussion of Activity 10.8

(a) The final position alone omits the middle return; distance is 12 m, obtained by adding all three leg lengths. (b) Reversal is determined by a sign change of velocity. Position zero merely means the chosen origin has been reached. Here the reversal at 1 s occurs at position 4 m. (c) The opposite direction changes velocity to −v, but |−v|=|v|, so distance stays 12 m. Displacement changes from 4 m to −4 m.

Activity 10.9 · Make a complete motion account

For v(t)=6−2t m/s on [0,5], with starting position −2 m, find the reversal time, position there, final position, displacement and distance. Check your account with a sketch of the velocity graph and the inequality relating distance to displacement.

Discussion of Activity 10.9

The reversal is at 3 s. The position function is s(t)=−2+6t−t2, so s(3)=7 m and s(5)=3 m. Displacement is 3−(−2)=5 m. The legs have lengths 9 m and 4 m, giving distance 13 m. The velocity triangles have those same magnitudes. The inequality 13≥5 and the initial-value/derivative checks all agree.

We can now distinguish where an object finishes from how much it travels. Next we will divide an accumulation by the length of its interval to obtain an average value. That will let us compare changing motion or flow with a constant rate producing the same total.
