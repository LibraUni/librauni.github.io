8.17 A transparent rectangle calculation

The computing work requires P101's functions, loops, conditional expressions and notebook operation, already used in M101 Unit 7. Download the notebook or its plain Python companion. The notebook asks for predictions and explanations as well as outputs. Restart and run it from the beginning when checking your finished work.

The calculation follows the formula directly. Divide the interval into n equal pieces, choose one endpoint on each piece, multiply the sampled height by the width, and add to a running total. In the code, Python's i=0,…,n−1 indexing selects the left endpoint. Adding one selects the right endpoint. The Boolean argument right makes that choice explicit.

"""M101 Unit 8: transparent endpoint sums. Original LibraUni code, MIT."""
from math import isfinite


def rectangles(f, a, b, n, right=False):
    """Endpoint sum on [a,b]; reversing limits negates the same partition."""
    if isinstance(n, bool) or not isinstance(n, int) or n <= 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be a positive integer")
    if not isfinite(a) or not isfinite(b):
        raise ValueError("Use finite endpoints")
    if a == b:
        return 0.0
    if b < a:
        return -rectangles(f, b, a, n, right)
    width = (b - a) / n
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        x = a + (i + (1 if right else 0)) * width
        value = f(x)
        if not isfinite(value):
            raise ValueError("The sampled value must be finite")
        total += value * width
    return total

The first guards reject a nonpositive or noninteger number of pieces and nonfinite endpoints. Equal endpoints give zero. Reversed endpoints are handled by swapping them and negating the result, so the interval orientation is exact in the implementation. After swapping, “left” still means the geometrically left endpoint of each piece. The remaining loop works on an increasing interval.

The code also rejects a nonfinite sampled output. These guards do not prove continuity or detect every interior singularity. Before calling the function, check the mathematical domain on the whole interval. The program evaluates only the points requested of it.

Discussion of Activity 8.17

The width is 1. Left inputs 0 and 1 give products 0 and 1, total 1. Right inputs 1 and 2 give products 1 and 4, total 5. def rate(t):
    return 2 + t

The square test should give 1 and 5, and the linear test should match the table. Exercise 8.1 · Contributions and units

A rate is 8 mL/s for 15 s, then 5 mL/s for 24 s. Find the total delivery and explain why an unweighted average of the rates is inappropriate.

Hint for Exercise 8.1

Pair each rate with its duration.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.1

The contributions are 8(15)=120 mL and 5(24)=120 mL, so the total is 240 mL. The two rates act for different durations. Their unweighted mean, 6.5 mL/s, would give 253.5 mL over 39 s; it weights the shorter interval too strongly.

Hint for Exercise 8.3

Worked solution to Exercise 8.3

Exercise 8.4 · What refinement requires

A partition divides the first half of [0,2] into more and more pieces, but always keeps [1,2] as one piece. Explain why the number of pieces tending to infinity is insufficient for the integral definition.

Hint for Exercise 8.4

Inspect the largest width.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.4

The largest width stays at least 1. The unsplit interval can retain a nonvanishing approximation error. The definition requires the largest width to tend to zero, ensuring every part is refined.

Exercise 8.5 · Signed geometry

The graph of f consists of straight segments through (0,0),(2,3),(4,0),(6,−2). Sketch it and find ∫06f(x)dx, ∫60f(x)dx, and the total geometric area.

Hint for Exercise 8.5

Compute each triangular area before assigning signs.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.5

The positive triangle has area 4(3)/2=6. The negative triangle has area 2(2)/2=2. The signed integral is 4, the reversed integral −4, and total geometric area 8. Reversal changes the integral sign; it does not change geometric area.

Exercise 8.11 · Definite evaluations

Evaluate (a) ∫−12(x2−1)dx, (b) ∫0π/2cosxdx, and (c) ∫1edx/x. Explain why a zero answer in (a) does not mean the integrand is zero.

Exercise 8.12 · Audit a theorem application

A calculation claims ∫−22x−2dx=[−1/x]−22=−1. Identify the failed condition. Explain why the endpoint arithmetic cannot justify the claimed integral.

Hint for Exercise 8.12

Inspect the whole interval, not only its ends.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.12

The integrand is undefined and unbounded at zero. It is not continuous on the stated closed interval, so the evaluation theorem taught here does not apply. The endpoint subtraction is arithmetic using formulas valid separately on two intervals; it does not establish an integral across the singularity. The negative result also conflicts with the positive integrand. A separate improper-integral theory would be needed.

Exercise 8.13 · Recover and interpret

A vessel has V′(t)=4−t L/min on [0,6], and V(2)=9 L. Find V(t), V(6), the net change from 2 to 6, and the time and value of the maximum volume. Assume the model applies throughout.

Hint for Exercise 8.13

Determine the constant from the supplied time, then use the rate sign.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.13

The family is V(t)=4t−t2/2+C. At 2, 8−2+C=9, so C=3. Thus V(6)=9 L, and the net change from 2 to 6 is zero. The rate changes from positive to negative at 4, giving the maximum V(4)=11 L. Endpoint values are V(0)=3, V(6)=9, so the maximum is also the global maximum on this interval.

Exercise 8.14 · Reason without an antiderivative

Let D(x)=∫0x(t−2)2dt on [0,4]. Without evaluating, decide where D increases and where it is concave up or down. Classify its stationary point. Then evaluate D(2) to anchor a sketch.

Hint for Exercise 8.14

The integrand is the first derivative; its derivative is the second.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.14

D′(x)=(x−2)2 is positive except at 2, so D is strictly increasing throughout the interval. D′′(x)=2(x−2), negative before 2 and positive after 2. The stationary point is an inflection, not an extremum. Evaluation gives D(2)=[(t−2)3/3]02=8/3. A sketch has a horizontal tangent there and changes concavity while continuing to rise.

Exercise 8.15 · A jump in a rate

Let f(t)=−1 for 0≤t<2 and f(t)=2 for 2≤t≤4. Define A(x)=∫0xf(t)dt. Find its two formulas, A(2), A(4), and the one-sided slopes at 2. Is A differentiable there?

Hint for Exercise 8.15

Add the accumulated contribution up to the join before continuing.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.15

For 0≤x≤2, A(x)=−x. For 2≤x≤4, A(x)=−2+2(x−2)=2x−6. Thus A(2)=−2 and A(4)=2. The formulas meet, so A is continuous. Their slopes are −1 and 2; the derivative at 2 does not exist. The integrand continuity condition fails there.

Exercise 8.16 · Check a computational claim

A program adds n samples of a constant rate 7 on [2,5] and returns 7n as the accumulated volume. Identify and repair the missing factor. Predict the corrected result for n=1 and n=20, then for reversed endpoints. Explain what this test establishes.

Hint for Exercise 8.16

Each sampled rate must be converted into a contribution.

Worked solution to Exercise 8.16

The width is 3/n, so multiply each sample by it. The corrected total is n(7)(3/n)=21 for either resolution; reversal gives −21. This detects the missing-width error and tests constant-function scaling and orientation. It does not establish accuracy for every varying function or rule out unsampled singularities.

8.20 Conclusion and compact reference

The opening question asked how a changing rate builds a total. A finite sum answers approximately by pairing each rate with an interval width. The definite integral makes the limiting construction precise. Its signs record gains and losses, its orientation records the direction of comparison, and its units identify the quantity accumulated.

The fundamental theorem connects that interval construction with local change. An accumulation function has the original continuous rate as its derivative. Any antiderivative therefore evaluates an accumulated change by endpoint subtraction. An initial value anchors the result when a particular quantity, rather than only its change, is needed.

The computational investigation returned to the original sums and checked them against exact reasoning. These routes support each other: a numerical test can expose a programming mistake, while conditions, derivative checks and interval reasoning establish what the calculation means. Unit 9 builds on this foundation by developing methods for finding antiderivatives.
