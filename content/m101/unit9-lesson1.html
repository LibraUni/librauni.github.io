M101 · Unit 9 · Lesson 1

Recognising elementary antiderivatives

Before choosing a technique, make the structure of the integrand visible.

Unit 8 gave us a small collection of antiderivatives. We now put that collection to work more deliberately. Many expressions that look unfamiliar become elementary after a rearrangement; others need a factor to compensate for a scaled input. In both cases, the derivative is our guide.

Four hours: 100 minutes reading and reconstructing examples, 80 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback and correction, and 20 minutes reserved for the closing unit exercises.

9.1 Start with the question being asked

The notation tells you what kind of answer to seek. An indefinite integral asks for a family of functions on a stated interval. A definite integral with fixed limits asks for an accumulated number. A rate together with an initial value asks for one particular function. The same antiderivative may serve all three tasks, but the last step differs.

For instance, an antiderivative of 2x+4 is F(x)=x2+4x. Differentiating confirms the choice. The indefinite integral is F(x)+C, whereas its definite integral from 1 to 3 is F(3)−F(1)=21−5=16. If the function must instead have value 7 at 1, then 5+C=7, so the particular function is x2+4x+2. The extra information selects the final form of the answer.

Example 9.1 · A rate, a change and a value

A supplied volume model has rate V′(t)=4t+2 litres per minute, with numerical time t in minutes. At t=1, the volume is 9 litres. Find its change from 1 to 3 minutes and its volume at 3 minutes.

The rate is continuous over the interval, so the evaluation theorem applies. Integrating gives 2t2+2t+C. For the change, the constant cancels:

ΔV=[2t2+2t]13=(18+6)−(2+2)=20 L.

The final volume is 9+20=29 litres. Alternatively, V(1)=9 gives C=5, so V(t)=2t2+2t+5 and V(3)=29. Both routes distinguish the starting amount from the added amount.

Before doing algebra, write a short description of your target: “family”, “change over this interval”, or “function satisfying this value”. This helps catch an answer that is mathematically related to the problem but does not answer it.

Activity 9.1 · Three endings to one calculation

For f(x)=3x2−2, find (a) its indefinite integral, (b) its definite integral from −1 to 2, and (c) the antiderivative with value 4 at −1. Explain why only (a) retains an arbitrary constant. Then find the value of the function in (c) at 2 and relate it to (b).

Discussion of Activity 9.1

(a) x3−2x+C. (b) [x3−2x]−12=4−1=3. (c) At −1 the formula has value 1+C, so C=3 and the function is x3−2x+3. Its value at 2 is 7: the initial value 4 plus the accumulated change 3. Differentiation removes an arbitrary constant; endpoint subtraction cancels it; an initial condition determines it.

9.2 Rewrite into elementary terms

The power rule integrates a power of the variable. A square root is such a power, and a reciprocal power can be written with a negative exponent. What matters is the whole term, including its coefficient and domain. For x>0, consider

2x3+3x−4x=2x5/2+3x1/2−4x−1/2.

Each term in the numerator is divided by the same denominator. Subtracting its exponent from each numerator exponent produces three elementary powers. This is a useful algebraic step because integration respects addition and constant multiples.

Result 9.1 · Elementary integration is linear

If F′=f and G′=g on the interval, and a,b are constants, then

∫(af(x)+bg(x))dx=aF(x)+bG(x)+C.

For p≠−1, use ∫xpdx=xp+1p+1+C on an interval where the expression and its derivative are real and defined.

The result follows directly by differentiating the right side. A sum of arbitrary constants is still just one arbitrary constant. Integration does not have a corresponding rule that multiplies the separate antiderivatives of two factors: the derivative of a product contains two terms.

Example 9.2 · A quotient that needs only algebra

Find ∫(2x3+3x−4)/xdx, for x>0.

Use the expansion above. Increase each exponent by one and divide its coefficient by the new exponent:

∫(2x5/2+3x1/2−4x−1/2)dx=47x7/2+2x3/2−8x+C.

For the last term, division by 1/2 doubles the coefficient. Differentiate term by term:

ddx(47x7/2+2x3/2−8x1/2)=2x5/2+3x1/2−4x−1/2.

Returning to a common denominator recovers the integrand. The restriction x>0 remains because the original expression has a square root in its denominator.

Sometimes multiplication, rather than division, hides a simple polynomial. Expanding x(x+2) gives x2+2x, so its integral is x3/3+x2+C. Multiplying x2/2 by x2/2+2x, the separate antiderivatives, would give a different derivative. Always make the algebraic structure explicit before applying a rule.

Activity 9.2 · Rewrite, integrate, check

Find and check the indefinite integral of each expression: (a) (x2+2x+3)/x, for x>0; (b) (x−1)2, for x>0; (c) (x3−2)/x2, for x≠0; (d) (x+1)(x−3). State the interval restrictions in your answers.

Discussion of Activity 9.2

(a) Rewrite as x+2+3/x, giving x2/2+2x+3lnx+C. (b) Expand as x−2x1/2+1, giving x2/2−(4/3)x3/2+x+C. (c) The expression is x−2x−2, giving x2/2+2/x+C on either interval excluding zero. (d) Expand as x2−2x−3, giving x3/3−x2−3x+C for all real x. Differentiating each term returns the displayed expansion; for (c), (2/x)′=−2/x2.

9.3 Match the scale of the input

When a known function is evaluated at ax+b, differentiation introduces the factor a. To reverse that operation, an antiderivative must compensate for it. For example, sin(5x−2) differentiates to 5cos(5x−2). Dividing by 5 gives an antiderivative of cos(5x−2).

The same reasoning works for negative scales. The derivative of e−3x is −3e−3x, so an antiderivative of e−3x has the coefficient −1/3. A negative coefficient here is exactly what is needed to obtain a positive derivative. The graph of −e−3x/3 increases towards zero as x increases.

Result 9.2 · Linear inputs

If F′=f and a≠0 is constant, then

∫f(ax+b)dx=1aF(ax+b)+C.

Choose an interval on which the functions are defined. The chain rule verifies the factor 1/a. When a=0, the integrand is the constant f(b), if defined, and its integral is xf(b)+C.

Example 9.3 · Two different scale factors

Find ∫[4cos(2x−1)+3e−3x]dx.

The cosine term becomes a sine and requires division by 2. The exponential keeps its form and requires division by −3. Thus

∫[4cos(2x−1)+3e−3x]dx=2sin(2x−1)−e−3x+C.

The check gives 2·2cos(2x−1)−(−3)e−3x, exactly the required sum. Trigonometric arguments are in radians.

There is a logarithmic version too. On an interval where ax+b≠0, the derivative of ln|ax+b| is a/(ax+b). Consequently 1/(ax+b) integrates to ln|ax+b|/a+C. Keep the absolute value until the sign of the inner expression has been established.

Activity 9.3 · Signs and scales

Integrate and differentiate back: (a) sin(4x); (b) 5e−2x; (c) 1/(3−2x), on either side of x=3/2; (d) (3x+1)4. Explain the coefficient in each answer rather than only citing a formula.

Discussion of Activity 9.3

(a) −cos(4x)/4+C: differentiating cosine supplies a minus sign and a factor 4. (b) −5e−2x/2+C: the factor −2 cancels −1/2. (c) −ln|3−2x|/2+C: the inner derivative is −2. (d) (3x+1)5/15+C: the outer power supplies 5 and the inner derivative supplies 3. Each constant compensates for all factors introduced by differentiation.

9.4 Check the identity and the interval

Checking means comparing the derivative of the entire proposed answer with the original integrand. It is often easier than finding the answer. It also tells you what sort of correction is needed: a constant multiplier, a sign, a missing term or a genuinely different method.

Example 9.4 · A plausible answer with the wrong derivative

Someone proposes (x2+1)3/3 as an antiderivative of (x2+1)2. Test it.

The outer power rule gives (x2+1)2, but the inner derivative contributes another factor:

ddx((x2+1)33)=2x(x2+1)2.

The unwanted factor 2x varies with x, so a constant coefficient cannot remove it. For this particular polynomial, expand the integrand as x4+2x2+1. A correct answer is x5/5+2x3/3+x+C. The failed guess still teaches us something: it would have worked for an integrand containing the factor 2x. That observation leads to substitution in the next lesson.

A correct derivative on one interval does not permit endpoint evaluation across a gap. The formula ln|x−2| differentiates to 1/(x−2) on each side of 2, but the integrand is not continuous on [1,3]. The evaluation theorem from Unit 8 therefore cannot be applied across that interval. Domain inspection belongs before endpoint substitution.

Activity 9.4 · Diagnose and repair

For each claim, find the first error and supply a valid conclusion. (a) ∫e4xdx=4e4x+C. (b) ∫x−1dx=x0/0+C. (c) ∫022xdx=4+C. (d) ∫02dx/(x−1)=[ln|x−1|]02=0.

Discussion of Activity 9.4

(a) The proposed derivative is 16e4x; use e4x/4+C. (b) The power rule excludes exponent −1; use ln|x|+C on an interval excluding zero. (c) Endpoint subtraction gives the fixed number 4, with no arbitrary constant. (d) The integrand is undefined at the interior point 1. The stated evaluation is invalid under our continuous-integrand theorem; no value follows from that endpoint calculation.

The first choice in integration is often a representation: expand a simple product, divide term by term, or identify a linear input. Once that preparation is complete, the derivative check closes the argument. Next we will handle composite expressions whose inner derivative is already present as a factor.
