Linear approximation and numerical derivatives

A derivative tells us how a quantity is changing at one input. How far can we trust a prediction built from that single rate?

A sensor may be calibrated accurately at one temperature, while the next reading arrives at a slightly different temperature. A position model may supply the velocity now, while we want a position a short time later. In both situations we know something local and want to use it nearby. The simplest prediction holds the current rate fixed. It is useful precisely because it is simple, but its usefulness depends on the size of the change and on the question the answer must settle.

Units 4–6 developed domains, limits, derivatives and the shape of functions. Here we turn those ideas into approximations that we can inspect. First we use a tangent line to predict a nearby output. Then we reverse the task: when function values are available, we use their differences to estimate a derivative. The two directions connect, but they are not identical. One estimates a value from a known slope; the other estimates a slope from known values.

Shorter steps seem an obvious route to greater accuracy. In exact mathematics they often help. A computer, however, stores numbers with finite precision. Subtracting two nearly equal calculated outputs can expose errors that were insignificant in the outputs themselves. You will see the competing effects in a controlled experiment, and learn why a stable-looking answer or many printed digits is insufficient evidence.

The closing lesson draws together Block 2. You will select methods, explain domains and assumptions, test a numerical claim, and use a formative checkpoint to decide what needs further attention. The next block turns from local rates to accumulated changes. Systematic series expansions belong to M 102; they are not prerequisites for this unit.

Preparing to begin

Retrieve the derivative definition and elementary rules from Unit 5, and the distinction between a local statement and an interval statement from Unit 6. You should be able to differentiate a polynomial and a reciprocal, interpret derivative units, and explain why a corner can prevent a derivative from existing. The Python work uses functions, loops, lists and formatted output from P 101. It uses only Python's standard library; plotting is optional. Check that preparation before undertaking the computing activities.

Working through the unit

The four lessons total 14 hours, including 3 hours of Python: 3, 3, 4 and 4 hours respectively. Reserve 25 minutes from Lesson 1, 25 from Lesson 2 and 30 from Lesson 3 for the unit exercises in Lesson 4. All activity attempts, feedback, the embedded iCMA 02 and reference work fit within these allocations. TMA 02 has its own separate six-hour allocation; this unit prepares for it but does not supply its assessment questions. Timings are study estimates, not a measured pace or a deadline.

Keep three columns in your working: what the model says exactly, what you calculated approximately, and what evidence supports the approximation. By the end, you should be able to construct and qualify a local linear model, implement forward and central differences, and explain a step-size experiment without turning its observations into a proof.
