Unit 2
Products, projections and orientation

Two arrows can have the same length and still describe very different movements. One may follow a chosen direction, another may cross it, and a third may point back along it. How can a calculation distinguish those relationships?

Unit 1 gave us ways to describe each arrow separately. Components record its signed contributions along perpendicular axes; magnitude measures its length; a unit vector keeps its direction while removing its scale. We now turn from describing one vector to comparing two. The central question is how their directions and magnitudes work together.

The arm in Figure 2.1 gives the question a physical setting. An endpoint's position does not tell us how the intervening segments are arranged. To describe that arrangement, we must compare directions as well as locations. Even then we must decide which way each arrow points along a segment: reversing an arrow changes its relation to the next arrow, although the metal structure has not moved. Our mathematical diagrams will make these choices explicit.

The first comparison will produce a single signed number, called the scalar product. For two vectors of fixed nonzero lengths, its value distinguishes directions that lean towards one another from directions that oppose one another; a zero value identifies a right angle. We will learn how to obtain this number either from an angle and two magnitudes or directly from components. Agreement between those calculations will connect an algebraic rule to a geometrical meaning.

Next we will ask how much of one vector lies along a specified direction. This leads to projection. Imagine choosing an axis that points along that direction and reading the signed component on it. We will turn that idea into a method that works even when the chosen direction is not one of the original coordinate axes, and then separate a vector into parallel and perpendicular parts.

Not every comparison should return a number. In three dimensions we sometimes need a direction perpendicular to the plane of two vectors, together with a measure of how they spread that plane out. The cross product will supply both. Its direction depends on the order of the two inputs, so orientation will become part of the calculation. No determinant method is assumed; that subject belongs to the next unit.

These products answer different questions. A central task will be choosing one deliberately, rather than treating “multiply the vectors” as a complete instruction. Later in this unit, supplied definitions of work and torque will illustrate the distinction. Their physical development belongs to P101; you will not need to infer unfamiliar physical laws to do the mathematics here.

Keep paper and a calculator available. Before Lesson 1, you should be able to add and subtract vector components, calculate a magnitude using Pythagoras' theorem, and connect a component to a cosine. You should also recognise that the zero vector has no direction. If one of those ideas is uncertain, use the relevant Unit 1 example and make a fresh attempt before proceeding. No new computer setup or programming is needed for Lessons 1–4.

Lesson 5 includes one hour of Python using scalar arithmetic, lists, conditions and a short returning function. It relies on the programming foundations of P101 Unit 1. Being able to open a notebook is only part of that readiness: you should be able to explain what its small calculations do. The final lesson also brings together the unit's cumulative exercises, feedback, conclusion and concise reference resources.

The times include attempts, corrections and reading worked solutions; they are planning estimates rather than deadlines. Sketch before calculating, and write a sentence interpreting a result. A negative scalar product, a zero projection and a reversed cross product will each carry particular information. Learning to read that information is as important as obtaining the number.
