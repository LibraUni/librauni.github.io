TMA 02: Change, approximation and evidence

A derivative can describe a local change, help explain the shape of a function, or connect two quantities that vary together. This assessment asks you to move between these uses, justify the conditions behind your calculations, and decide what a numerical experiment does—and does not—establish.

Units 4–7 · 100 marks · Six hours. Complete all five questions. The six hours are the separate assessment allocation after Block 2, beyond its 72 study hours.

No plotting package or new physical law is needed.

Equivalent forms and valid alternative methods receive credit unless a particular method is requested.

Question 1 · Limits and joins (15 marks)

Allow about 40 minutes.

  1. Consider the real-valued function

    f(x)=x+3−2x−1.

    State its natural domain. Evaluate limx→1f(x) by an algebraic argument, explaining why the expression used for the limit agrees with the original function near the point. Define a continuous extension at x=1 and justify the value you assign there. [7]

  2. A second function is defined by

    p(x)={x2+2x,x≤1,ax+b,x>1.

    Find all real pairs (a,b) that make p continuous at 1. Determine which of those pairs also make it differentiable there, using the one-sided difference quotients at the join to justify your conclusion. Give one pair for which the function is continuous but not differentiable, and explain why it has this behaviour. [8]

Question 2 · Choosing and combining methods (25 marks)

Allow about 65 minutes.

In this question, make the structure of each calculation visible: identify the relevant differentiation rules and show how they act on the functions given.

  1. For

    F(x)=ln(1+x2)x+2,

    state the real domain and find F′(x). Explain which rules you use and where your derivative formula applies. [7]

  2. Let G(x)=esinx. Find G′(x) and G″(x). Evaluate both at x=π/2, and use your values to explain why the second derivative is not, in general, the square of the first derivative. [7]

  3. Consider the implicit curve

    x2+xy+2y2=4.

    Verify that (2,0) lies on it. By implicit differentiation, find the gradient and equation of the tangent at that point, and find d2y/dx2 there. Show the condition needed when you divide to obtain dy/dx, and check that it holds at the stated point. [7]

  4. Starting from the difference quotient, rather than a differentiation rule, find the derivative of H(x)=1/(x+3) at x=1. Show the algebra before taking the limit and state the restrictions on the increment in that algebra. [4]

Question 3 · Shape and global conclusions (20 marks)

Allow about 60 minutes.

Throughout this question, consider

q(x)=x4−4x2,−1≤x≤3.

  1. Find every stationary point in the interior of the stated interval. Establish where q is increasing and decreasing, and classify each interior stationary point as a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither. Support your classifications with reasoning. [8]

  2. Find the global maximum and global minimum on [−1,3]. Give both the input and function value for each, and explain why your comparison includes all necessary candidates. [4]

  3. Determine the intervals of upward and downward concavity within the domain. Give the input at each inflection point and justify that the concavity changes there. [4]

  4. Apply the mean value theorem on the smaller interval [0,2]. State why its hypotheses hold, calculate the average rate of change, and find every c∈(0,2) satisfying the theorem’s conclusion. [4]

Question 4 · Rates and a local model (20 marks)

Allow about 50 minutes.

The first part concerns changing geometry; the remaining parts concern a resistance that changes with temperature. In each setting, distinguish a rate at an instant from a finite change over an interval.

  1. A sphere has volume V=4πr3/3 and surface area A=4πr2. At an instant when its radius is 2cm, its volume is increasing at 12πcm3/s. Find the rates of change of its radius and surface area at that instant. Show how time differentiation connects the quantities and include units. [6]

For parts (b)–(d), the numerical value of a sensor’s resistance in ohms is modelled by

R(T)=100[1+0.004(T−20)+0.00001(T−20)2],

where T is the numerical value of temperature in degrees Celsius and 0≤T≤50. Time in part (d) is measured in minutes.

  1. Find the tangent-line model L(T) about T=20 and interpret its slope with units. Use it to estimate both the change in resistance and the resistance itself when temperature rises from 20∘C to 22∘C. [5]

  2. Derive an exact expression for R(T)−L(T). Find the largest symmetric temperature interval about 20∘C, within the given domain, on which the absolute error of the tangent-line model is at most 0.01Ω. Explain why your argument covers every temperature in this interval, not just a selection of test values. [5]

  3. At an instant when the temperature is 20∘C, it is decreasing at 0.5∘C/min. Use the model R, with a time-dependent temperature, to find the instantaneous rate of change of resistance. Interpret the sign and include units. [4]

Question 5 · Testing numerical evidence (20 marks)

Allow about 90 minutes.

Write your own short Python investigation of forward and central difference estimates. Use ordinary Python functions, conditions and loops; do not use symbolic differentiation or a library’s numerical derivative routine. A plain-text table is sufficient. Keep full precision in calculations and round only for display.

The first two functions and the evaluation point are

f(x)=11+x,
g(x)=106+f(x),
a=1.

  1. Before running your code, find the exact derivative at a for each function. Calculate by hand the forward and central difference estimates for f at a with h=0.2, together with their absolute errors. Keep this prediction in your answer so you can compare it with the program’s output. [4]

  2. Starting in a blank file or notebook, write reusable functions for the two difference estimates. Each should accept a function, an evaluation point and a step. Require a positive step and check that each shifted floating-point input required by the method is distinct from the evaluation point. If a check fails, return or report a clear status instead of a derivative estimate. Use finite inputs in this investigation; a general-purpose input-validation library is not required. [5]

  3. For both f and g, run both methods at a=1 with each of the steps

    h=0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 10−4, 10−8, 10−12, 10−16.

    Present a labelled table giving the function, method, step, estimate and absolute error, or the status if no estimate is produced. A rejected case must not stop the remaining cases from running. Compare with your hand calculation. Discuss how the two methods behave at the larger steps, what happens as the step becomes very small, and why adding a constant can affect the numerical result although it leaves the exact derivative unchanged. Identify a useful tested step for each function, stating which method you mean and why you chose it. Base conclusions on your actual output. [5]

  4. Now let k(x)=|x−1|+x. Use your central-difference function at a=1 with h=0.1 and h=0.01. Independently determine the left and right derivatives there. Explain whether the numerical results establish that k is differentiable at this point. [3]

  5. Run the complete investigation in a fresh process, or restart the notebook kernel and run all cells in order. Save the outputs, include brief run instructions and the Python version, and write a short conclusion distinguishing the exact results, the numerical evidence and what the experiment cannot prove. [3]

How your work will be marked

Marks are shown beside each part and total 100. The criteria below describe the evidence being assessed; a private worked marking guide supports consistent marking.

  • Question 1 (15): valid domains and limit reasoning; continuity separated from differentiability; one-sided arguments at a join.
  • Question 2 (25): correct rule selection and intermediate differentiation, attention to conditions, an explained implicit calculation, and a genuine first-principles argument.
  • Question 3 (20): justified sign and concavity analysis, complete global comparison, and correct use of the mean value theorem on its specified interval.
  • Question 4 (20): time-dependent reasoning with units; construction and interpretation of a tangent model; an algebraically justified error interval.
  • Question 5 (20): independent reusable code and guards, agreement with hand checks, labelled reproducible evidence, and conclusions that distinguish a numerical observation from a proof.

Credit is awarded for valid method and reasoning even when an arithmetic slip affects a later result. A consistent carried-forward error is not repeatedly penalised, provided the later work remains meaningful; an invalid domain or a missing theorem hypothesis cannot be repaired by a correct-looking final number. Unsupported answers cannot earn the marks assigned to explanation. Numerical outputs may vary slightly between environments: interpretation should match the submitted run, not an expected last digit. No marks depend on decorative formatting or plotting.

Feedback will identify what is secure, what needs correction, and a focused next step. This assessment does not introduce a new pass threshold or change the module’s approved assessment weighting.
