Connecting mathematical representations

A moving object can leave several kinds of record: positions on a map, two graphs against time, a pair of instrument readings and a calculated total distance. Each record tells part of the story. How do we decide whether they describe the same motion, and which one will answer the question we actually have?

The drifter gives a concrete reason to connect geometry and change. A map can show where a tracked object went, but without timing it cannot tell us how quickly it moved. An instrument may record combinations of coordinates rather than the coordinates themselves. A numerical estimate of distance can be plausible even when it was calculated from the wrong quantity. The task is to keep the meaning of the quantities intact as we move between representations.

We will work with a deliberately simple, supplied planar journey. Its position functions are specified exactly on a short interval, so we can establish analytical benchmarks before trusting a numerical investigation. The journey is an invented teaching model, not a fit to the photographed drifter. No theory of ocean circulation is needed. This choice lets us concentrate on the mathematical decisions: recover components, identify a rate, accumulate the appropriate quantity and check the result.

Unit 12 asked why competing answers might disagree. Here we take responsibility for the chain of calculations that produces the answers. In Lesson 1, the same journey appears as a vector, a matrix relation, a table and graphs. Lesson 2 connects local motion to accumulated displacement and distance. Lesson 3 combines the calculations in a short reproducible investigation, with checks chosen for different purposes. Lesson 4 changes the setting to transport between two stores, then provides a formative synthesis checkpoint and the cumulative unit exercises.

Bring the vector and matrix methods of Units 1–3, differentiation and local approximation from Units 5–7, and accumulation and quadrature from Units 8–11. You should be able to solve two simultaneous linear equations, differentiate a polynomial, use a definite integral and explain a trapezoidal sum. For the computing work, use the P101 foundation of functions, loops, lists and a clean notebook run. The relevant methods are recalled in context; the aim is to choose and connect them with increasing independence.

Keep a short reasoning record as you work: the quantity requested, the representation chosen, one check and the conclusion that check supports. Reconstruct the worked examples on paper before consulting their working, and use the activity discussions to identify the first point where your reasoning differs. Time for these attempts and corrections is included in the lesson allocations. The full unit takes 18 hours, including four hours of Python; the module's separate revision and final-assessment allocations remain separate.

By the end of this unit you should be able to explain a complete mathematical route through an unfamiliar supplied model. The closing checkpoint will help you decide which connections are secure and which need more work. Reading the material or running the notebook is a starting point for that judgement; the evidence lies in the reasoning you can produce.
