M101 · Unit 10 · Lesson 4

Volumes by slices and supplied work models

A small contribution need not be an area in a coordinate diagram. A thin slice contributes area times thickness to a volume. A force acting through a small displacement contributes work according to a supplied physical relationship. We will build each integral by describing that contribution first.

Four hours: 100 minutes reading and reconstructing examples, 70 minutes activities, 50 minutes feedback and correction, and 20 minutes reserved for closing exercises.

10.13 From thin slices to volume

A prism with a constant cross-sectional area A and length L has volume AL. This includes a cylinder: its circular cross-section has area πr2, giving volume πr2L. The same multiplication suggests a way to handle a shape whose cross-section varies along its length.

Choose an axis through the solid and call its coordinate x. A cross-section is the shape made by a plane perpendicular to that axis. Let A(x) be its area at position x. A thin slice of thickness Δxi has approximately the volume A(xi)Δxi, using a representative section within the slice. Adding the slices and making the largest slice thickness tend to zero gives

V=limn→∞∑i=1nA(xi)Δxi=∫abA(x)dx.

This is the same sum-to-integral construction used for accumulated rates. Here the product has dimensions length squared times length, giving volume. We use continuous cross-sectional areas on a finite interval; a solid built from several such pieces can be handled by adding their separate volumes.

Key idea 10.4 · A slice model

Choose the slicing direction and specify the cross-sectional area A(x), not just a width or radius. For a solid extending from a to b, with a<b,

V=∫abA(x)dx,A(x)≥0.

Use consistent length units for the section and its thickness.

Example 10.10 · A supplied area profile

A solid extends from x=0 to x=3 cm. Its cross-sectional area is A(x)=2+x cm², with x numerical in centimetres. Find its volume and mean cross-sectional area.

The contribution is area times a small length along the axis, so

V=∫03(2+x)dx=[2x+x22]03=212 cm3.

The mean area is volume divided by total length: (21/2)/3=7/2 cm². This lies between the endpoint areas 2 and 5 cm². The volume likewise lies between 2×3=6 and 5×3=15 cm³, as it should.

We did not need to know the exact shape of each section. Different section shapes with the same area profile give the same volume. What matters to this integral is how much area each plane cuts through.

Activity 10.14 · Check the integrand

A solid has circular cross-sections of radius r(x), measured in metres, along a two-metre axis. A proposed volume is ∫02r(x)dx. Explain the dimensional error and write the correct integral. If the radius is a constant 0.1 m, evaluate it.

Discussion of Activity 10.14

Radius times thickness has units m², so the proposal has the dimensions of area. A circular section has area πr(x)2, giving V=∫02πr(x)2dx. For the constant radius, V=π(0.1)2×2=0.02π m³. This recovers the familiar cylinder formula.

10.14 Constructing the cross-sectional area

Often a question supplies a length describing each section, so we must turn that into an area. The shape determines how: square sections use side squared, circular sections use π times radius squared, and triangular sections use half the base times perpendicular height. Only after constructing that area should we integrate along the solid.

Example 10.11 · Expanding square sections

A solid of length 4 cm has square sections perpendicular to its axis. At coordinate x cm, the square's side is 1+x/2 cm. Calculate the volume.

The cross-sectional area is A(x)=(1+x/2)2 cm². Expanding first exposes terms we can integrate directly:

V=∫04(1+x+x24)dx=[x+x22+x312]04=523 cm3.

The side grows from 1 to 3 cm, so the area grows from 1 to 9 cm². The volume, approximately 17.33 cm³, lies between 4 and 36 cm³. Using only the mean of the endpoint areas would give 20 cm³; the area varies quadratically, so that endpoint shortcut is not exact.

Example 10.12 · A cone from its tip

A right circular cone has height H>0 and base radius R. Place x=0 at the tip and x=H at the base, along the axis. Derive its volume.

In a plane through the axis, similar triangles show that the radius grows in the same proportion as distance from the tip: r(x)/R=x/H. Thus r(x)=Rx/H, and a perpendicular section has area A(x)=πR2x2/H2. Consequently

V=πR2H2∫0Hx2dx=πR2H3.

The result is one third of the volume of a cylinder with the same base and height. For fixed height, doubling every radius makes every section four times as large and hence multiplies the volume by four. This agrees with the factor R2 in the result.

Activity 10.15 · Radius, diameter and units

  1. A short cylindrical sample has diameter 8 mm and length 5 cm. Calculate its external volume in cm³.
  2. A tapered solid has circular sections with radius 2+x cm for 0≤x≤3, where x is in cm. Find its volume.
  3. In Example 10.12, choose distance z from the base instead of from the tip. Write the radius and volume integral in z, and show that the answer is unchanged.
Discussion of Activity 10.15

(a) Radius is half the diameter, 4 mm = 0.4 cm. Volume is π(0.4)2(5)=0.8π cm³. (b) Use area π(2+x)2. Its integral is π[(2+x)3/3]03=39π cm³. (c) The radius is R(1−z/H) on [0,H]. Integrating πR2(1−z/H)2 gives πR2[H−H+H/3]=πR2H/3. Both coordinates describe the same slices in opposite order.

10.15 A supplied work model

We now change what the small contribution means. For one-dimensional motion, take the following physical model as supplied: the work done by a force over a small signed displacement is approximately its component along the coordinate axis times that displacement. If the component depends continuously on position, the total work from position a to position b is

W=∫abF(x)dx.

Here F(x) is a signed force component. With force in newtons and position in metres, work has units newton metres, called joules (J). For motion towards increasing x, positive force gives positive work and negative force gives negative work. The sign records whether the force acts with or against that displacement.

The component matters: Unit 2's scalar product distinguishes a force component along a displacement from the whole force magnitude. Our supplied one-dimensional formula has already made that selection. We are using it to practise the mathematical model; no additional force law or energy theorem is needed.

Example 10.13 · Work with a sign change

A supplied force component is F(x)=12−3x N for numerical position 0≤x≤6 m. Find its work as an object moves from 0 to 6 m.

Work retains the force's sign. An antiderivative gives

W=[12x−32x2]06=18 J.

The force changes sign at 4 m. Over the first part the work is 24 J; over the second it is −6 J. Adding them gives 18 J. Integrating |F| would instead give 30 J, the sum of the magnitudes of the two contributions. It would not answer the stated work question.

Example 10.14 · A supplied elastic loading law

An actuator exerts a positive force component F(x)=200x N while increasing an extension x, measured in metres. Find its work from extension 2 cm to 5 cm.

Convert both endpoints before substituting: 0.02 m and 0.05 m. The coefficient has units N/m, so the product gives newtons. Then

W=∫0.020.05200xdx=100(0.052−0.022)=0.21 J.

The force increases linearly from 4 to 10 N over 0.03 m. Its average over position is 7 N, giving the independent check 7×0.03=0.21 J. The force is the actuator's specified component; a restoring force in the opposite direction would have the opposite sign.

Activity 10.16 · Identify the force and displacement

For the same extension interval, a supplied restoring-force component is Fr(x)=−200x N. Find its work during increasing extension. Then find its work for a return from 0.05 m to 0.02 m, assuming the same position-dependent force law. Explain both signs.

Discussion of Activity 10.16

Increasing extension gives −0.21 J: the force acts against the positive displacement. Reversing the endpoints gives +0.21 J: both force and displacement point towards decreasing x. This calculation relies on the stated force being the same function of position on both journeys.

10.16 Limits, units and physical admissibility

A coordinate describes where we slice or where the force acts. The bounds must therefore be coordinates in that same variable. A solid extending from x=2 to x=5 has length 3, but the correct bounds in an area function expressed in x are 2 and 5. Using 0 and 3 requires a new coordinate and a corresponding rewritten function.

Dimensions help test the small contribution: section area times thickness gives volume; force times displacement gives work. They also reveal why an integral of force over time is not the supplied work model. Force times time has different dimensions. Before calculating, write down what your independent variable measures and how the integrand was defined.

For nonnegative cross-sectional area, the volume lies between the smallest and largest section areas multiplied by the solid's length. For work with a changing signed force, the corresponding signed integral bounds still apply, but work itself can be negative. An imposed absolute value would change the quantity.

Activity 10.17 · A shifted coordinate

A solid has area A(x)=x2 cm² for 2≤x≤5, with x in cm. Find its volume. Then let u=x−2, rewrite the area and bounds, and verify the same result. Explain what ∫03u2du would model instead.

Discussion of Activity 10.17

The volume is [x3/3]25=(125−8)/3=39 cm³. In the new coordinate, x=u+2, giving ∫03(u+2)2du=39 cm³. The integral of u2 would have volume 9 cm³ and describe sections growing from area zero to 9 cm². The original sections grow from 4 to 25 cm², so changing the bounds without changing the function describes a different solid.

We have now built several integral models from small contributions. Their algebra often looks similar, yet the integrand's meaning and the interval determine the answer's interpretation. The final lesson brings these decisions together and asks you to test a complete model before accepting its calculated result.
