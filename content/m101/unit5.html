Derivatives and differentiation methods

A function tells us how much. A derivative tells us how rapidly that amount changes when we change its input.

A spacecraft may be far from Earth and barely changing its distance, or much closer and moving rapidly away. Knowing a distance does not settle a question about its rate of change. The same distinction appears in a laboratory: the temperature of an apparatus and the rate at which it is warming are different quantities. Their units differ, and each answers a different question.

Unit 4 gave us the language needed to make a local rate precise. We can compare a function at two nearby inputs and ask what happens as their separation tends to zero. The limit must be taken with care: setting the separation equal to zero immediately would leave a division by zero. The derivative is the finite value approached by those rates when the limit exists.

Our first task is to understand this operation well enough to derive a simple result from its definition. After that, we will build a usable collection of methods. Some functions belong to familiar families: powers, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometric functions. Others combine these families by addition, multiplication, division or composition. Choosing the right rule requires reading the structure of the expression before doing algebra. A long expression can be manageable when its structure is clear; a short expression can conceal a domain restriction or a point where no derivative exists.

The final lessons extend the idea in two directions. An equation may describe a curve without giving its vertical coordinate explicitly as a function of its horizontal coordinate; implicit differentiation lets us work locally with that relation. And a derivative is itself a function, so we can ask how rapidly the first rate changes. This gives a second derivative, which connects position, velocity and acceleration in a supplied motion model. We will calculate such rates here; systematic questions about extrema and shape belong to Unit 6.

Preparation and ways of working

You should be comfortable with the domains, composition, finite limits, one-sided approaches and continuity developed in Unit 4. Keep its difference-of-squares and conjugate methods available. Algebraic fluency and radians are also needed; reminders appear where the calculus uses them. If a limit calculation is difficult, separate the algebra from the new interpretation as a derivative. There is no requirement to have studied calculus already.

The unit has six lessons, each allocated four hours, including attempts and correction. In Lessons 1–5, reserve the indicated final half-hour for the cumulative exercises in Lesson 6. The unit therefore contains 24 hours overall, including two hours of scalar Python investigation. Work on paper first, read feedback after an attempt, then redo the first step that went wrong. The computer work requires the functions, loops and basic numerical habits taught in P101 Unit 1; it uses no arrays or plotting packages. If that preparation is unfinished, complete it before the coding part rather than treating a supplied program as evidence that you can write and explain one.

By the end, you should be able to derive a simple derivative, explain a choice of rule, retain its conditions, distinguish a function value from a derivative value, and compare a calculation with an independent numerical check. The closing exercises mix these demands. A concise web reference and printable PDF collect the results worth recalling and the conditions worth consulting.

Numerical agreement will be useful evidence, but it will never replace the definition or make an invalid point part of a domain. That distinction is the thread joining this unit to both its predecessor and the later study of numerical derivatives.
