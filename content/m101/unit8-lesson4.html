M101 · Unit 8 · Lesson 4

Antiderivatives, evaluation and initial values

A function with the right derivative provides an efficient route from a rate to its accumulated change.

The fundamental theorem has shown that accumulation creates a function whose derivative is the integrand. We now ask how to find such a function from known differentiation rules, and why its endpoint values determine an integral.

Four hours: 90 minutes reading and examples, 90 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback, and 20 minutes reserved for the unit exercises.

Use the practice allocation actively: after studying each worked example, cover its solution and reconstruct the method on paper, then attempt the numbered activities. Use the feedback time to locate and correct the first difference in your reasoning.

8.13 Antiderivatives and the constant

An antiderivative of f on an interval is a function F satisfying F′(x)=f(x) there. For example, x3 is an antiderivative of 3x2. So are x3+2 and x3−7, because differentiation removes a constant.

Are there any others, beyond vertical shifts? Suppose F and G have the same derivative on an interval. Their difference has derivative zero. By the mean value theorem from Unit 6, a function with derivative zero throughout an interval is constant there: between any two points, its secant slope equals some derivative, hence is zero. Therefore G=F+C for a constant C.

Definition 8.5 · Indefinite integral

If F′=f on an interval, the family of all its antiderivatives there is written

∫f(x)dx=F(x)+C.

The absence of limits signals a family of functions. A definite integral with fixed limits gives a number. The constant may differ on disconnected parts of a domain.

The interval qualification matters for functions such as 1/x, whose domain excludes zero. A constant on the negative half-line need not equal a constant on the positive half-line. The mean value theorem cannot compare points across an excluded gap.

Example 8.8 · Test an antiderivative

A proposed antiderivative of 6x2−4x+5 is 2x3−2x2+5. Is it correct?

Differentiating the proposal gives 6x2−4x. The constant 5 differentiates to zero, so the required final term is missing. A correct antiderivative is 2x3−2x2+5x, and the complete family adds C. The derivative check identifies the error immediately.

Activity 8.13 · Same rate, different values

Find all functions with derivative 2x−3. Select the one satisfying F(1)=4, and verify both conditions.

Discussion of Activity 8.13

The family is F(x)=x2−3x+C. At 1, 1−3+C=4, so C=6. The derivative is 2x−3 and F(1)=4, as required. The derivative alone would not determine the constant.

8.14 Reversing elementary derivative rules

The power rule tells us that differentiating xp+1 gives (p+1)xp. Divide by p+1 to remove the extra factor:

∫xpdx=xp+1p+1+C,p≠−1.

Use this on an interval where the powers are real and differentiable. For arbitrary real powers, the positive half-line is a safe common domain; integer powers allow broader domains as appropriate. The exception p=−1 would divide by zero. Its antiderivative comes instead from the logarithm rule.

For x>0, the derivative of lnx is 1/x. For x<0, differentiate ln(−x): the chain rule gives (1/(−x))(−1)=1/x. Both cases are collected by ln|x|, on intervals excluding zero.

Example 8.9 · Rewrite before integrating

Find ∫(3x−2/x2+4/x)dx for x>0.

Write the first two terms as 3x1/2−2x−2. The first exponent increases to 3/2; the second to −1. The reciprocal term uses the logarithm:

F(x)=2x3/2+2x−1+4lnx+C.

Checking gives F′(x)=3x1/2−2x−2+4/x. The positive coefficient of 2x−1 is correct: differentiation supplies the minus sign through its exponent.

A scaled input requires a scaled coefficient. Since d(ekx)/dx=kekx, one antiderivative of ekx is ekx/k for nonzero constant k. Similarly, sin(kx)/k differentiates to cos(kx). These are direct checks with the chain rule. Unit 9 will turn the recognition of more general composite expressions into systematic methods.

Activity 8.14 · Fluency with a check

Find the indefinite integrals of (a) 4x3−6x+2, (b) 2cosx−3ex, and (c) e3x. Differentiate each answer. State the role of the constant in each.

Discussion of Activity 8.14

(a) x4−3x2+2x+C. (b) 2sinx−3ex+C. (c) e3x/3+C. The power, sine and exponential rules return the respective integrands; in (c) the factor 3 from the chain rule cancels the denominator. Each C is an arbitrary constant because its derivative is zero.

8.15 The evaluation part of the theorem

Let f be continuous on [a,b], and let F be any antiderivative of f. The accumulation function A(x)=∫axf(t)dt also has derivative f. Thus F−A is constant. At a, the accumulation is zero, so this constant is F(a). Therefore A(x)=F(x)−F(a), and at the final endpoint,

Theorem 8.6 · Evaluating accumulation

For continuous f on [a,b], and an antiderivative F on that interval,

∫abf(x)dx=F(b)−F(a).

This is the evaluation part of the fundamental theorem. The same endpoint formula respects reversed limits by orientation. The notation [F(x)]ab means “upper endpoint value minus lower endpoint value”.

The result is powerful because the original total was defined by a limit of many contributions. Once an antiderivative is known, just two values determine it. The constant in F+C cancels: [F(b)+C]−[F(a)+C]=F(b)−F(a). We can therefore choose the simplest antiderivative for a definite calculation.

Example 8.10 · A nonzero lower endpoint

Evaluate ∫13(2x+1)dx.

An antiderivative is F(x)=x2+x, verified by differentiation. Then

∫13(2x+1)dx=[x2+x]13=(9+3)−(1+1)=10.

The lower value is 2, not zero. A geometric check uses the trapezium with endpoint heights 3 and 7 and width 2: its area is (3+7)2/2=10. The integrand is positive, so a positive answer is consistent.

Example 8.11 · Signs at both ends

Evaluate ∫0π/2sinxdx.

Use −cosx, because its derivative is sinx. The result is

[−cosx]0π/2=−cos(π/2)−(−cos0)=0−(−1)=1.

The graph is nonnegative over this interval, and lies between 0 and 1. Its integral must lie between 0 and π/2; the exact answer 1 passes that check.

Check the interval before applying the theorem. The expression 1/x2 has antiderivative −1/x on either side of zero. Subtracting its values at −1 and 1 would give −2, but the function is undefined and unbounded at zero inside that interval. The continuity conditions fail, so this is not a valid evaluation of a definite integral under the theory developed here. Integrals involving such singularities require a separate limiting treatment later.

Activity 8.15 · Evaluate and interpret

Find (a) ∫02(3x2−2)dx, (b) ∫20(3x2−2)dx, and (c) ∫14dx/x. Check the relation between (a) and (b), and state why (c) satisfies the theorem's conditions.

Discussion of Activity 8.15

(a) [x3−2x]02=8−4=4. (b) Reversal gives −4. (c) [lnx]14=ln4, since ln1=0. The reciprocal is continuous throughout [1,4]; zero is outside the interval.

8.16 Recovering a quantity from an initial value

Suppose Q′(t)=q(t), with a continuous known rate and a specified value Q(t0)=Q0. The integral gives the change from the starting time, so

Q(t)=Q0+∫t0tq(s)ds.

This formula passes both required checks. At t=t0, the integral vanishes and the value is Q0. Differentiating it returns q(t). Alternatively, find a family F(t)+C of antiderivatives and choose C using the initial condition. Both methods lead to the same function.

Example 8.12 · Starting the clock after the process begins

A supplied model has net inflow q(t)=6−2t L/min, where t is measured in minutes. At t=1, the vessel contains 10 L. Find the volume at t=3 and its formula on [1,4], assuming the model applies there.

An antiderivative of the rate is 6t−t2. Anchor its change at the known time:

V(t)=10+(6t−t2)−(6−1)=5+6t−t2.

Thus V(3)=14 L. The net gain from 1 to 3 is 4 L, not 14 L. Differentiating gives the required rate, and substituting 1 returns 10 L. The rate becomes negative after 3, so the volume then decreases; at 4 it is 13 L. The model stays positive over the stated interval.

Activity 8.16 · An initial value selects one curve

A position model satisfies s′(t)=3t2−4 m/s, with the numerical time t in seconds, and s(1)=2 m. Find s(t), the position at 2 s and the displacement from 1 to 2 s. Check the derivative and the initial value.

Discussion of Activity 8.16

The family is s(t)=t3−4t+C. The condition gives 1−4+C=2, hence C=5. Then s(2)=5 m and the displacement is 5−2=3 m. The derivative is 3t2−4, and s(1)=2. Position and displacement share units, but differ by the starting position.

The unit's central connection is now complete. Accumulation differentiates to the rate, while an antiderivative evaluates the accumulated change. An initial value supplies the information differentiation cannot retain. In the closing lesson we return to finite sums and test a short program against these exact results.
