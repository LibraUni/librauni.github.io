M101 · Block 1 · Unit 2: Products, projections and orientation

Lesson 1
Scalar products and angles

Suppose two straight directions have been recorded by their components. Their lengths are easy to calculate using Unit 1. Their angle is less obvious, especially in three dimensions, where a drawing can be misleading. We need a way to recover that geometrical relationship from the numbers.

The scalar product will provide it. We will first decide what angle we mean, then build a signed product from magnitudes and a cosine. Components will give us a second way to calculate the same quantity. Finally, we will reverse the reasoning and use the product to find an unknown angle.

Allow about three hours, including the Unit 2 introduction: approximately one hour for explanations and worked examples, ninety minutes for the numbered activities, and thirty minutes for checking, correction and recall. The suggested activity times are guides to first attempts; feedback is included separately. Read with paper and a calculator beside you. Unless stated otherwise, the abstract vectors in this lesson are dimensionless and all components use the same perpendicular unit directions.

2.1 Comparing directions

In Unit 1, a direction angle in a plane was measured from the positive horizontal axis. Here the reference is another vector. Slide two nonzero arrows until their tails meet, keeping their lengths and directions unchanged. The angle between the vectors is the smaller angle between the two outward-pointing arrows, chosen from 0° to 180°, including both endpoints. We call it θ.

Two arrows pointing the same way have θ=0∘, even if one is much longer. Oppositely directed arrows have θ=180∘. Perpendicular arrows have θ=90∘. The comparison does not record clockwise versus anticlockwise turning. Exchanging the names of the arrows leaves this angle unchanged.

Figure 2.2 distinguishes two questions that can look deceptively similar. If a route arrives along a and leaves along b, the angle between those displacement vectors is found by translating them tail-to-tail. The interior angle at the corner of the route uses the backward-pointing arrival direction, −a, and the departure direction, b. Except for a right angle, these are different angles.

Key idea 2.1 · An angle needs two directions

For two nonzero vectors, use their tail-to-tail angle 0∘≤θ≤180∘. Translating an arrow preserves its direction; reversing it does not. There is no angle between a nonzero vector and the zero vector, because the latter supplies no direction.

In space, two nonparallel directions define a plane. We can compare their arrows within that plane even when the plane is tilted relative to our coordinate axes. A flat photograph of a three-dimensional object, such as Figure 2.1, generally does not display that plane without distortion. This is one reason a calculation from measured components can be more useful than measuring the angle in a picture.

Example 2.1 · Crossing the zero-degree direction

Two nonzero plane vectors have standard direction angles 350° and 20°. Find the angle between them.

Solution

A sketch puts one arrow just below the positive horizontal axis and the other just above it. Subtracting the stated direction angles gives 330°, which describes the longer turn. The turn across the zero-degree direction is

θ=360∘−330∘=30∘.

The angle between the vectors is therefore 30°. Neither vector's length is needed. This also shows why “subtract the angles” needs a check against the required range.

Discussion of Activity 2.1

The displacement directions east and north-east differ by 45°. At the corner, the backward arrow points west, so its angle with north-east is 135°. Reversing the first arrow changes the comparison. If the first displacement is zero, neither a direction for it nor a backward direction is available: neither comparison defines an angle with that displacement. A dot in the sketch does not stand for an arrow pointing east.

2.2 A signed product

Begin with a familiar component. Let a nonzero vector b point along a temporary positive horizontal axis. Place a second nonzero vector a at the same origin. From Unit 1, its signed horizontal component is |a|cosθ. The bars mean magnitude, so |a| is positive; any negative sign in this component comes from the cosine.

Now multiply that component by the magnitude of b. This gives a quantity that depends on both vector lengths and on the angle between them:

a·b=|a||b|cosθ.

This is the scalar product, also called the dot product. Read the centred dot as “dot”. The result is a scalar, not a new arrow. It is not the same operation as multiplying one vector by a scalar: 3a is a vector, whereas a·b is a number, with units when the inputs have units.

For an acute angle, the component along the chosen positive axis is positive. For an obtuse angle, the perpendicular foot lies behind the origin, giving a negative component. The sign is not an instruction to replace a negative length with a positive one: it is information about the relative directions. Lesson 2 will develop this signed component into a general projection method.

Definition 2.2 · Scalar product

For nonzero vectors, a·b=|a||b|cosθ, with the tail-to-tail angle θ in the range 0° to 180°. If either vector is zero, define the scalar product to be zero. This extends the product to all vectors without assigning a direction or angle to the zero vector.

The zero case needs that separate sentence. We cannot put “the angle of the zero vector” into the cosine formula, because there is no such angle. The product nevertheless has a well-defined zero value, as the component calculation in the next section will also show.

Example 2.2 · The same lengths, different relationships

Two displacement vectors have lengths 5 m and 2 m. Find their scalar product when their angle is 60°, then when it is 120°.

Solution

The product of the magnitudes is 10m2. Cosine supplies the directional information:

a·b=(5m)(2m)cos60∘=5m2.

For 120°, the cosine is −12, giving a·b=−5m2. The lengths have stayed positive. The sign changed because the directions now form an obtuse angle. The unit is square metres, since we multiplied two lengths; this does not by itself make the answer the area of a surface.

A useful pair of limiting cases is exact alignment and exact opposition. Their cosines are 1 and −1, so the products are respectively |a||b| and −|a||b|. For fixed lengths these are the largest and smallest possible values. At 90°, cosine is zero and the product vanishes, however large the nonzero vectors are.

Activity 2.2 · Rotate without changing length

Allow about 10 minutes.

Take |a|=3 and |b|=8. Sketch the tail-to-tail arrows and calculate the dot product at 0°, 60°, 90°, 120° and 180°. Explain the sign changes. Then double only |b|: which answers change, and does the angle change?

Discussion of Activity 2.2

The magnitude product is 24, so the five results are 24, 12, 0, −12 and −24. The first two have a component along the positive direction of b; the obtuse and opposed cases have a component against it. Perpendicularity leaves no component along that direction. Doubling |b| gives 48, 24, 0, −24 and −48. Every product is multiplied by two, including the zero product, which stays zero. Lengthening an arrow by a positive factor does not change its direction or its angle with the other arrow.

2.3 Calculating from components

If components are given, finding the angle first would be an awkward way to compute the scalar product—particularly because finding the angle is one of our eventual aims. There is a much simpler calculation:

a·b=axbx+ayby+azbz.

The subscripts identify components on the same perpendicular unit axes. In two dimensions, omit the third term. Multiply corresponding components and then add the scalar results. Do not multiply every component by every other component, and do not leave the three products inside vector brackets. We will first use this rule and then explain why it agrees with the geometric definition.

Example 2.3 · Three components, one number

Find the scalar product of a=⟨2,−1,3⟩ and b=⟨−4,2,1⟩.

Solution

Match the first entries, then the second, then the third. Keep brackets around negative entries to make their role visible:

a·b=2(−4)+(−1)(2)+3(1)=−8−2+3=−7.

The third pair contributes positively, but it does not cancel the two negative contributions. The result is the scalar −7, not the vector ⟨−8,−2,3⟩. Since both inputs are nonzero, its negative sign tells us already that their angle is obtuse.

2.3.1 Why the two formulas agree

Place the two vectors tail-to-tail and label their tips A and B. The vector from B to A is a−b. Its squared length can be computed in the original coordinate system by squaring and adding its components. In a plane, expanding the two squares gives

|a−b|2=(ax−bx)2+(ay−by)2=ax2+ay2+bx2+by2−2(axbx+ayby).

The first pair of squares is |a|2 and the second pair is |b|2. Thus the component calculation gives

|a−b|2=|a|2+|b|2−2(axbx+ayby).

Now compute that same distance using convenient axes in the plane of the arrows. Put b along the positive horizontal direction and take r=|a| and s=|b| for the two positive lengths. The tips then have coordinates (rcosθ,rsinθ) and (s,0). Pythagoras gives

|a−b|2=(rcosθ−s)2+(rsinθ)2=r2(cos2θ+sin2θ)+s2−2rscosθ=r2+s2−2rscosθ.

Here cos2θ+sin2θ=1 is Pythagoras on the unit circle, recalled from components in Unit 1. This distance relation is often called the cosine rule; we have obtained the needed form rather than assuming it as a new prerequisite. Negative cosine values cause no difficulty because the signed horizontal component was retained.

Both calculations describe the squared distance between A and B. Equating them cancels the two squared magnitudes, leaving

axbx+ayby=rscosθ=a·b.

This also explains why choosing a different perpendicular coordinate system cannot change the answer: the lengths and the angle are geometrical features of the same two arrows. Both vectors must be described in the same system. Combining the first vector's room-based components with the second vector's components measured along a tilted instrument would not be the calculation we have derived.

In three dimensions, the first calculation has one more square, (az−bz)2, and therefore one more paired product, azbz. The geometrical calculation still takes place in the plane containing the two directions. Parallel vectors can be placed in any plane through their common line; the same formula includes 0° and 180°. If an input is zero, every corresponding component product is zero, agreeing with the separate definition.

Key idea 2.3 · Match components on common axes

On common perpendicular unit axes, multiply like components and add:

a·b=axbx+ayby+azbz.

For quantities with units, use consistent units within each vector before adding products. Omitting a zero component is harmless; shifting another entry into its place is not.

Discussion of Activity 2.3

The products are 3(2)+(−2)(3)=0, 3(−1)+(−2)(4)=−11, and 2(−1)+3(4)=10. All vectors are nonzero, so the angles are respectively right, obtuse and acute. Also p·p=9+4=13=|p|2. The proposed vector contains the paired products, but these must be added to give the scalar −11. Adding zero third components adds 0(0) to the first calculation, leaving zero. We changed the representation, not the arrows' relationship.

2.4 What a zero product tells us

Activity 2.3 produced a zero scalar product from two nonzero vectors. The cancellation was meaningful: their directions were perpendicular. To see why the converse is also true, start from a·b=|a||b|cosθ. For nonzero vectors, the product of magnitudes is strictly positive. Therefore a zero result forces cosθ=0. In the specified range, the only possible angle is 90°.

This gives an exact perpendicularity test, often useful when a sketch is unreliable. Orthogonal is another word used for perpendicular directions. We will keep the nonzero condition visible when drawing a conclusion about an angle.

The same reasoning interprets other signs. A positive product corresponds to 0∘≤θ<90∘; a negative one corresponds to 90∘<θ≤180∘. At the endpoints, the arrows are aligned or opposed. “Positive means acute” is convenient shorthand only after excluding exact alignment, since a zero-degree angle is not acute.

Example 2.4 · Constructing a perpendicular direction

Find a nonzero vector of the form v=⟨t,3⟩ perpendicular to u=⟨2,−4⟩.

Solution

Neither vector can be zero: the second component of each is nonzero. We can therefore require their dot product to vanish:

u·v=2t−12=0,2t=12,t=6.

The vector is ⟨6,3⟩. Check by substitution: 2(6)+(−4)(3)=12−12=0. Its direction is perpendicular to u. Any nonzero scalar multiple of this answer is another perpendicular vector; the question's prescribed second component selects this particular one.

Contrast that calculation with u·0=0. The product is zero, but there is no second direction to place at right angles to the first. In later abstract mathematics, “orthogonal” is commonly defined by a zero dot product and therefore includes the zero vector. That algebraic convention still does not assign the zero vector a geometrical direction. Here, a statement about a right angle always requires two nonzero vectors.

Discussion of Activity 2.4

One answer is ⟨4,−3⟩, giving 12−12=0. Another is ⟨−8,6⟩, giving −24+24=0. There are infinitely many possibilities; acceptable answers are nonzero and satisfy the dot-product check. The program's zero product is correct, but its angle is undefined: it must check for a zero magnitude before trying to infer an angle.

2.5 Rules that preserve the meaning

The scalar product has some familiar algebraic behaviour, but we should establish it rather than transfer every rule for ordinary multiplication to vectors. Start with symmetry. In components, axbx=bxax, and the same is true for each other pair. Their sums agree, so

a·b=b·a.

The geometric definition says the same thing: exchanging the arrows leaves both the angle and the product of magnitudes unchanged. This property is called commutativity. It applies to the scalar product; later we will meet a vector product for which order matters.

For a sum, the component rule gives a second useful result. In two dimensions, expand each ordinary numerical bracket and regroup:

a·(b+c)=ax(bx+cx)+ay(by+cy)=(axbx+ayby)+(axcx+aycy)=a·b+a·c.

The extra terms in three dimensions group in exactly the same way. This is distributivity: taking the scalar product with a sum gives the sum of the two scalar products. Multiplying one input by a real number k also multiplies every paired component product by k, so (ka)·b=k(a·b). This works for negative k and for zero, as well as positive values.

Example 2.5 · Checking distributivity with small vectors

Use a=⟨2,1⟩, b=⟨1,3⟩ and c=⟨−2,1⟩ to calculate a·(b+c) in two ways.

Solution

Adding first gives b+c=⟨−1,4⟩, so the dot product is 2(−1)+1(4)=2. Taking the two products first gives

a·b=5,a·c=−3,

and their sum is again 2. Agreement checks this calculation. The general component expansion above explains why the rule holds for all vectors; one numerical example alone could not establish that.

2.5.1 A vector dotted with itself

Putting the same vector into both positions gives a sum of squares:

a·a=ax2+ay2+az2=|a|2.

This is never negative, and it is zero only when every component is zero. In the nonzero case the geometrical explanation is that the vector makes angle 0° with itself. For the zero vector, the component calculation establishes the result without inventing an angle. Taking the square root therefore recovers magnitude: |a|=a·a.

Distributivity and symmetry connect that result to a vector sum:

|a+b|2=(a+b)·(a+b)=a·a+a·b+b·a+b·b=|a|2+2a·b+|b|2.

Each bracket is a vector sum; each dot product is a scalar. If the vectors are perpendicular, the middle term disappears and Pythagoras reappears. If they lean towards the same direction, the positive middle term increases the squared length. If they oppose, it reduces it. This supplies a reason why adding magnitudes usually fails to give the magnitude of a sum.

Key idea 2.4 · Useful scalar-product rules

The product is symmetric and distributes over vector addition. A scalar factor may be taken outside the product. In particular, a·a=|a|2. Keep the types of quantities visible: there is no operation “divide by a vector” that undoes a dot product.

Allow about 14 minutes.

Let a=⟨1,2⟩ and b=⟨3,−1⟩.

  1. Find a·b, then predict b·a, (−2a)·b and (2a)·(3b) without recalculating every component.
  2. Use the squared-sum identity to find |a+b|2, then check by forming the vector sum.
  3. A learner knows a·v=0 and concludes v=0. Give a counterexample and explain why scalar cancellation is not valid.
Discussion of Activity 2.5

The first product is 1(3)+2(−1)=1. Symmetry gives 1 in reversed order; the scalar factors give −2 and 6. The squared magnitudes are 5 and 10, so the squared-sum identity gives 5+2(1)+10=17. Directly, a+b=⟨4,1⟩, whose squared magnitude is also 17. For the last part, choose v=⟨2,−1⟩: it is nonzero but a·v=2−2=0. A zero product can arise from perpendicular directions, so it cannot be cancelled as though a were an ordinary nonzero number.

2.6 Recovering an angle

We can now return to the problem that opened the lesson. For two nonzero vectors, the component calculation gives the scalar product and Pythagoras gives both magnitudes. Rearrange the geometrical definition by dividing by their positive product:

cosθ=a·b|a||b|.

The quotient removes the effect of length, leaving only the directional comparison. Its value lies between −1 and 1 because it is a cosine. If your quotient falls outside that interval, stop before using inverse cosine: check the dot product, magnitudes, units and rounding. An impossible input is evidence of a problem, not an unusual angle.

Use inverse cosine, written arccos or cos−1, to recover the unique angle in the range 0° to 180°. Here the superscript −1 means the inverse function, not the reciprocal 1/cosθ. If you want an answer in degrees, set the calculator accordingly. A radian answer is equally valid when identified as such, but it has a different numerical value.

Example 2.6 · An angle in three dimensions

Find the angle between u=⟨1,2,2⟩ and v=⟨2,−1,2⟩, to the nearest tenth of a degree.

Solution

Both vectors are nonzero. Their scalar product is 1(2)+2(−1)+2(2)=4, so the angle must be less than 90°. Each magnitude is 3:

|u|=12+22+22=3,
|v|=22+(−1)2+22=3.

Divide by both magnitudes, retaining the exact fraction until the final calculation:

cosθ=43×3=49,
θ=arccos(49)≈63.6∘.

The result is acute, as its positive dot product predicted. A three-dimensional perspective drawing is unnecessary for the numerical angle. Notice that dividing by only one magnitude would give 4/3, which cannot be a cosine.

Example 2.7 · Reversing a direction

Replace v in Example 2.6 by −v. Find the new angle without repeating all the component work.

Solution

Reversal changes the dot product to −4 but leaves both magnitudes at 3. The new cosine is −4/9. Therefore

θnew=arccos(−49)≈116.4∘.

The new angle is 180∘−θ, where θ is the original unrounded angle. One arrow now points the other way along its line. Keeping only the absolute value of the dot product would have lost exactly this distinction.

A positive rescaling behaves differently. Doubling v doubles the numerator and doubles its magnitude in the denominator, so the quotient stays unchanged. This is why a raw dot product is not, by itself, a measure of angle: a larger value can result from longer arrows. Only after dividing out both lengths do we have a pure directional comparison.

  2. Replace b by 5b, then by −b. Explain the effect on the angle in each case.

Hint for Activity 2.6

Calculate the two magnitudes independently. Keep the negative sign in the numerator when forming the cosine, and compare the calculator’s answer with your obtuse-angle prediction.

Discussion of Activity 2.6

The dot product is 2(−1)+(−1)(2)+2(1)=−2, so the angle is obtuse. The magnitudes are 3 and 6. Thus cosθ=−236 and θ≈105.8∘. Positive rescaling leaves that angle unchanged: the factor of five cancels from the quotient. Reversal changes the cosine to 236 and gives approximately 74.2∘, the supplementary angle. A cosine of 1.2 lies outside the allowed range and indicates a calculation or input error. A zero-vector input instead makes the denominator zero and leaves no angle to calculate; it does not give an aligned direction.

2.7 Choosing and checking a calculation

There are now two routes to a scalar product. If lengths and a tail-to-tail angle are supplied, the geometrical formula uses them directly. If common-axis components are supplied, paired multiplication and addition are usually simpler. If the question asks for an angle, the component product is an intermediate step, followed by division by the two magnitudes and inverse cosine. Naming the requested output first prevents a correct intermediate number from being mistaken for the final answer.

Example 2.8 · Directions along a jointed structure

In a fictional measurement of two connected straight segments, the successive displacements are a=⟨2,1,2⟩m and b=⟨−1,2,2⟩m. Find the angle between the successive displacement vectors. Then find the interior angle at the joint, between the directions pointing back along the first segment and forward along the second.

Solution

The dot product is

a·b=[2(−1)+1(2)+2(2)]m2=4m2.

Both lengths are 3 m. The units cancel in the quotient:

cosθ=4m2(3m)(3m)=49.

Thus the displacement vectors make an angle of about 63.6°. At the joint, however, the backward direction is −a, so the required interior angle is about 180∘−63.6∘=116.4∘. We have answered two different questions about the same geometry. These values are invented for the calculation; they are not measurements of the Canadarm2 photograph.

A wider view · optional context

Seeing a direction is not measuring it

Imagine walking around a bent wire held still in front of you. Its outline changes as you move, even though the wire itself does not. At one viewpoint a bend may look almost straight; from another it may look sharp. A picture preserves a view of the object, not every spatial relationship within it.

That distinction matters in Example 2.8. The three components describe each displacement in space. The dot product recovers the angle between those directions independently of how a sketch is tilted on the page. It still matters which endpoints define each arrow: changing a viewing direction does not reverse a displacement, but exchanging its start and finish does.

There is a useful discipline behind these checks. Confirm first that the data describe comparable components in a common system. Retain the signs. Check whether the answer should be a scalar or an angle, and whether its units fit. Use a simple geometrical prediction where possible, then use a second calculation that genuinely tests the first. Re-entering the same mistaken expression on a calculator is not an independent check.

  1. Sketch the points and predict the angle POQ. Calculate the dot product and use it to confirm the angle.

Discussion of Activity 2.7

The first vector lies on the positive horizontal axis; the second has equal positive components and lies at 45°. The scalar product is 12m2, and the lengths are 4 m and 18m:

cosθ=12418=12,θ=45∘.

The vector from P to Q is q−p=⟨−1,3⟩m, whose squared magnitude is 10m2. The distance identity gives 16+18−2(12)=10 in square metres. It uses the reverse difference, which has the same length. The learner stopped before adding the paired products; their sum is the scalar 12 square metres. A scalar product is not an angle. Recovering the angle requires the dimensionless quotient and inverse cosine.

Activity 2.8 · What information has been lost?

Allow about 8 minutes.

A fixed vector is a=⟨2,0⟩. Find two nonzero vectors b and c such that a·b=a·c=4, but their angles with a are different. Explain why the equal dot products do not contradict the angle formula.

Hint for Activity 2.8

The dot product fixes the first component of the other vector. Does it fix its second component or its magnitude?

Discussion of Activity 2.8

One pair is b=⟨2,0⟩ and c=⟨2,2⟩. Both products equal 4. The first vector is aligned with a, giving 0°, while the second makes 45°. Its greater magnitude compensates for its smaller cosine. More generally, any vector ⟨2,t⟩ gives the same dot product with a. A single product retains one comparison; it does not preserve the whole second vector or specify an angle without magnitude information.

Looking back and ahead

We began with the question of finding an angle from components. The scalar product makes that possible because it joins two descriptions: a sum of paired component products and a product of magnitudes with a cosine. Its sign tells us how the directions relate, while division by the two magnitudes isolates the angle. The zero-vector case reminds us that a defined scalar product need not correspond to a defined angle.

Before moving on, close the discussions and explain aloud why a·a is never negative, why a zero dot product needs a nonzero check before it implies a right angle, and why reversing one arrow changes the comparison. Use the remaining feedback time to correct your least secure activity and redo the key step without looking.

In Lesson 2 we will keep one direction fixed and ask how much of another vector lies along it. The signed component that motivated today's definition will become the starting point for scalar and vector projections, and for a perpendicular remainder that you will know how to check.
