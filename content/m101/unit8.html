Accumulation and the fundamental theorem

Knowing how quickly something changes is one kind of knowledge. Recovering how much has changed over an interval is another. Integration connects them.

Suppose water enters a vessel through a pipe. A flow meter tells you the volume entering each second. If the reading stays fixed, multiplication gives the volume delivered: rate times elapsed time. If the reading rises and falls, multiplying one reading by the whole duration no longer represents the process. We need a way to collect the contributions from all parts of the interval.

There is a practical starting point. Divide the time into short intervals, estimate the delivery in each one, then add. The shorter the intervals, the more faithfully a constant rate can represent the changing rate within each. A definite integral gives a precise mathematical meaning to the limiting total. Its sign records whether contributions add to or subtract from the quantity being tracked, and its units come from multiplying the units of the rate by those of the input.

This approach initially appears quite different from differentiation. A derivative concentrates on a shrinking neighbourhood of one point; an integral collects contributions across an interval. The central result of the unit, the fundamental theorem of calculus, brings these two viewpoints together. Differentiating an accumulation function recovers the rate. Conversely, finding a function with a prescribed derivative gives an efficient way to calculate an accumulated change.

That connection does not determine an initial amount. Two vessels can receive the same changing inflow yet contain different volumes because they began with different amounts. The constant in an antiderivative records precisely this freedom. An initial value selects the member of the family appropriate to a particular situation.

Preparation and route

Use Units 4–7 to retrieve continuity, limits, elementary differentiation, the chain rule and the mean value theorem. You should be comfortable with signed changes, powers, radians, and units. We introduce sum notation here rather than assume it. The five lessons move from finite sums through signed integrals and accumulation functions to antiderivatives, initial values and a small computational investigation.

The unit takes 20 hours, including one hour of Python. Each lesson has four hours. Lessons 1–4 each reserve 20 minutes for the cumulative exercises in Lesson 5; that gives 120 minutes of closing attempts when combined with Lesson 5's own 40 minutes. Feedback and correction are included. The computing activity uses the functions and loops already introduced in P101 and used in M101 Unit 7; it needs no plotting library. If that preparation is missing, complete it before attempting the notebook independently.

Keep both a calculation and an interpretation in your working. Ask what a small contribution represents, what its sign means, and what unit the total should have. Unit 9 will develop integration techniques; Unit 10 will build more extensive physical and geometric models; Unit 11 will examine numerical integration in depth. Here our task is to establish the connection on which those methods depend.
