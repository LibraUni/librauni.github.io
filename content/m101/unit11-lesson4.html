11.13 From a number to an inspectable argument

Begin a numerical report with the quantity being accumulated. If a supplied power model P(t) is measured in watts and time in seconds, then ∫abP(t)dt represents energy in joules. That statement fixes the units and bounds before any numerical rule is chosen. The same discipline distinguishes displacement from distance, or a final stored amount from a net change.

Next explain the representation. With a formula, you can evaluate new sample points. With a table, you must say how you model the intervals between readings. State the rule and resolution so another reader can reproduce the calculation. Then give checks that ask different questions: a hand panel checks the weights, a known integral checks the implementation, refinement tests resolution, and examination of the function or data addresses missed features and model limits.

Example 11.8 · A compact report with a justified bracket

A supplied power model is P(t)=4/(1+t) W for 0≤t≤1 s, where the numerical value of t is in seconds. Estimate the energy and explain the evidence.

Using 32 trapezoidal panels gives approximately 2.77283283 J. The model is positive and decreasing, so the right and left sums bracket its integral. Their difference is h(P(0)−P(1))=(1/32)(4−2)=0.0625 J. Since T32 is their average, its error is at most 0.03125 J in exact arithmetic.

An analytical benchmark is available: E=4ln2≈2.77258872 J. The actual trapezoidal error is about 0.00024411 J, comfortably within that bound. Midpoint and trapezoidal refinement provide additional implementation evidence, while the exact antiderivative supplies the strongest check here. These statements concern the supplied power model; using it for a real device would also require evidence that the power model fits the device.

The numerical calculation was not needed to evaluate this particular integral. That is precisely why it is useful as a benchmark. Once the implementation behaves correctly on cases whose answers we know, we can apply it more confidently to cases without a convenient exact answer, while retaining the checks appropriate to their features.

Activity 11.13 · A report someone else can check · 60 minutes Python

A dimensionless response model is f(x)=1/(1+x4) on [0,2]. Estimate its integral using midpoint and trapezoidal rules. Choose and justify a sequence of panel counts. Include a hand calculation for one panel, a separate exact benchmark for your routines, a domain/shape check and an explicit uncertainty statement. Then demonstrate the missed-pulse failure from Lesson 3 in the same report and explain why it does not invalidate every numerical calculation.

Write a short conclusion that distinguishes the evidence for the smooth response model from the evidence for the pulse. Do not report an unproved error estimate as a guaranteed bound.

Hint for Activity 11.13

The response is positive and decreasing on [0,2], since f′(x)=−4x3/(1+x4)2≤0. Endpoint sums therefore provide a bracket even though the curve changes concavity. The midpoint and trapezoidal outputs need not bracket the answer merely because they did so for a quadratic.

Discussion and assessment criteria for Activity 11.13

One-panel midpoint quadrature samples x=1 and gives 1. One trapezoid gives 2(1+1/17)/2=18/17. Those are useful hand checks, not high-resolution answers. At 64 panels, midpoint is approximately 1.07013219 and trapezoidal is approximately 1.07011868; at 128 panels they are approximately 1.07012882 and 1.07012544. Values suggest about 1.07013. A monotonicity bound is available independently: at n=128, the endpoint bracket width is (2/128)(1−1/17)=1/68, so its trapezoidal centre is within 1/136 of the integral in exact arithmetic.

A strong report provides runnable code, a labelled table, checks with predicted answers and a limitation matched to the evidence. It distinguishes the conservative bracket from a smaller refinement-based estimate. It shows why the pulse's known width and position matter, and uses the geometric area 0.004 to diagnose the failure. There is no single required panel count or prose wording; the quality lies in the reasoning and reproducibility.

Exercise 11.1 · Count panels and samples

For ∫25f(x)dx with n=3, list the endpoints, left samples and midpoint samples. State the common width.

Hint for Exercise 11.1

There is one more endpoint than panel.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.1

The width is (5−2)/3=1. Endpoints are 2,3,4,5; left samples are 2,3,4; midpoint samples are 2.5,3.5,4.5. Each rule uses three contributions, although the trapezoidal rule needs four endpoint values.

Return to Exercise 11.1

Exercise 11.2 · A complete small comparison

For f(x)=x2 on [0,2], calculate L2,R2,M2,T2 and the exact integral. Explain the ordering.

Hint for Exercise 11.2

Write each set of sample positions first.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.2

With h=1, L2=0+1=1, R2=1+4=5, M2=0.25+2.25=2.5, and T2=(0+4)/2+1=3. The exact integral is 8/3. Hence L2<M2<I<T2<R2. Increase gives the endpoint bracket; upward curvature explains the trapezoidal overestimate. Direct evaluation establishes all parts of the displayed ordering.

Return to Exercise 11.2

Exercise 11.3 · Guarantee a tolerance

A nondecreasing rate on [0,3] h has endpoint values 2 and 6 L/h. Using equal panels, how many panels suffice to bound the left-rule error by 0.06 L? How many suffice for the trapezoidal error, using only the endpoint bracket?

Hint for Exercise 11.3

The bracket width is width times endpoint difference; its centre needs only half that bound.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.3

The bracket width is (3/n)(6−2)=12/n L. For the left rule, 12/n≤0.06 requires n≥200. Trapezoidal quadrature is the bracket centre, so 6/n≤0.06 requires n≥100. These are sufficient counts based on monotonicity, not claims of minimality.

Return to Exercise 11.3

Exercise 11.4 · Signed linear change

For f(x)=2x−3, calculate one-panel midpoint and trapezoidal estimates on [0,2], and then reverse the bounds. Explain why both are exact.

Hint for Exercise 11.4

A straight line is exactly represented by its chord; its mean equals its midpoint value.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.4

The midpoint is 1 with value −1, giving −2. The endpoint values are −3 and 1, giving 2(−3+1)/2=−2. Direct integration gives [x2−3x]02=−2. Reversed bounds give +2 for both methods. The total is signed; crossing zero does not invalidate the line representation.

Return to Exercise 11.4

Exercise 11.5 · Different widths

An invented flow record gives rates 2,4,1 L/min at times 0,2,5 min. Estimate the total by trapezoids. Explain the model between readings.

Hint for Exercise 11.5

Use two widths, not a single average spacing.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.5

The first panel contributes 2(2+4)/2=6 L and the second 3(4+1)/2=7.5 L, totalling 13.5 L. The model joins adjacent rates by straight lines. The calculation is exact for that interpolant, while the actual unsampled rate may differ.

Return to Exercise 11.5

Exercise 11.6 · Midpoints versus averages

A program calls (f(a)+f(b))/2 the midpoint height. Give a one-panel quadratic counterexample on [0,2], calculate the two resulting estimates and name the rule that the program actually implements.

Hint for Exercise 11.6

A midpoint is an input coordinate, before evaluation.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.6

For f(x)=x2, the midpoint height is f(1)=1, giving M1=2. The endpoint average is (0+4)/2=2, giving 4. The latter is the one-panel trapezoidal rule. Evaluating after averaging and averaging after evaluating do not generally commute.

Return to Exercise 11.6

Exercise 11.7 · Read the error pattern

A smooth-model calculation gives Q10=1.842, Q20=1.8105, Q40=1.802625. Check the ratio of successive differences. Under a C/n2 error model, estimate the error in Q40. What still needs checking?

Hint for Exercise 11.7

Use the finer pair for its error estimate.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.7

The differences are 0.0315 and 0.007875, a ratio of four. The finer error estimate is 0.007875/3=0.002625. This supports the proposed error pattern but is not a bound. Inspect the domain, resolved features, implementation benchmarks and further refinement; a finite table alone does not establish the model.

Return to Exercise 11.7

Exercise 11.8 · A missed contribution

A symmetric triangular pulse of height 3 has support [0.401,0.405] and is zero elsewhere on [0,1]. Both midpoint and trapezoidal rules with n=10 and n=20 return zero. Check that claim, find the exact integral and propose a repair if the pulse position is known.

Hint for Exercise 11.8

Locate nearby endpoints and midpoints; use triangle area.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.8

The nearby endpoints are 0.4 and at least 0.45; relevant midpoints include 0.425 or 0.45. None lies strictly inside the support. The exact integral is 12(0.004)(3)=0.006. Split at 0.401,0.403,0.405 and integrate the linear pieces. Agreement at zero missed the whole contribution.

Return to Exercise 11.8

Exercise 11.9 · Endpoint values at a jump

A rate is 2 for x<0.4 and 5 for x≥0.4, on [0,1]. Find its integral. Explain why applying one trapezoid to the left piece using endpoint values 2 and 5 gives a wrong contribution.

Hint for Exercise 11.9

Use the left-hand extension on the left piece.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.9

The integral is 0.4(2)+0.6(5)=3.8. The left piece contributes 0.8. Using 2 and 5 over [0,0.4] instead gives 0.4(7)/2=1.4, creating an artificial ramp. A single endpoint value does not change the integral, but its nonzero quadrature weight changes that finite approximation.

Return to Exercise 11.9

Exercise 11.10 · Calibration and refinement

Every rate reading in a 3 h record may share an offset of magnitude at most 0.1 L/h. Bound its effect on the trapezoidal total. Would inserting linearly interpolated readings reduce it?

Hint for Exercise 11.10

Test the quadrature weights on a constant function.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.10

The weights sum to 3 h, so the total offset magnitude is at most 0.3 L. Interpolating retains the same common offset and the same integrated straight segments; it does not reduce this bound or supply new measurements.

Return to Exercise 11.10

Exercise 11.11 · Find the indexing defect

A trapezoidal routine begins with total=(f(a)+f(b))/2, then uses for i in range(n): total += f(a+i*h). Find the defect and design a constant-function test that exposes it.

Hint for Exercise 11.11

Which endpoint has already been counted?

Worked solution to Exercise 11.11

The loop adds f(a) again; the correct interior range is range(1,n). On [0,2] with f=3 and n=4, the faulty routine returns 0.5(3+4×3)=7.5, rather than 6. A zero-valued constant would fail to expose the mistake.

Return to Exercise 11.11

Exercise 11.12 · An unsuitable relative error

A signed integral is exactly zero. A numerical approximation gives 10−8 J. Explain why a relative-error percentage is inappropriate. What could you report instead?

Hint for Exercise 11.12

The usual denominator is the absolute exact value.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.12

Relative error divides by |I|=0, so it is undefined. Report absolute error 10−8 J and compare it with a tolerance justified by the task. Inspect positive and negative contributions if cancellation is substantial; a small net value alone does not describe their sizes.

Return to Exercise 11.12

Exercise 11.13 · A numerical value outside the domain

A routine evaluates 1/(x−0.2) at finite sample points on [0,1] that avoid 0.2. It prints a plausible decimal. Identify the missing check and explain whether adding more decimal places fixes it.

Hint for Exercise 11.13

Inspect the formula over the whole interval.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.13

The integrand is undefined at an interior point and unbounded near it. The finite-interval rules taught here do not justify treating the finite sum as the requested integral. More displayed digits cannot repair the domain failure. A separate analysis of singular integrals would be needed.

Return to Exercise 11.13

Exercise 11.14 · Design a defensible check

You receive a numerical energy estimate from a supplied power model on a finite time interval. Propose four distinct checks, stating what each could expose. Include one failure that a smooth benchmark would not expose.

Hint for Exercise 11.14

Separate interpretation, implementation, resolution and model features.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.14

One suitable set is: (1) units and bounds, to catch minutes used with watts without conversion; (2) a hand panel and exact constant/linear benchmark, to catch weights or indexing; (3) a refinement table retaining unrounded values, to examine resolution; (4) inspection of the formula and sample positions near a pulse or jump, to expose missed structure. Reversal and zero-interval tests add useful coverage. A smooth benchmark can pass while a narrow pulse is entirely missed. Other checks earn the same role if their diagnostic purpose and limits are explained.

Return to Exercise 11.14

11.17 Conclusion and reference

The unit began with a practical gap: a few values of a rate do not yet give its accumulated total. Rectangle, midpoint and trapezoidal rules fill that gap by choosing a simple representation on each panel. Their formulas are records of those choices. Panel widths carry units; sample positions and endpoint weights determine which information enters the sum.

Exact benchmarks and refinement tables then showed how these methods can be checked. The monotonicity bracket gave a bound with stated conditions. The quadratic example explained a factor-of-four error reduction, and the missed pulse showed why that pattern cannot simply be assumed. A jump, a calibration offset and finite-precision arithmetic each required a different response. The useful question was always what information or assumption supported the next conclusion.

Across Block 3, integration has developed from a limit of signed contributions into a family of analytical and numerical tools. Unit 8 established accumulation and its connection to differentiation. Unit 9 developed antiderivatives and method selection. Unit 10 asked what an integral represents in a model. Unit 11 has made the computational checks explicit. Keep those strands together: formulate the quantity, choose a method, calculate, then interpret and test the answer.

Spend 20 minutes identifying one reliable method you can now reconstruct and one difficulty that needs further practice. Use your corrected work to prepare for TMA 03 when its separate questions are available; its six hours remain outside this unit. Unit 12 begins the final block on approximation, validation and mathematical judgement. Neither a completed reading nor this checkpoint by itself establishes mastery.
