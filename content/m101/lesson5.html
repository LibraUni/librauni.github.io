Lesson 5
Vector fluency and unit review

A route can be described as a sequence of instructions, a line on a map, or a list of changes in coordinates. These descriptions need not look alike. Yet if they refer to the same journey, they must agree about where it finishes. That agreement gives us both a way to solve problems and a way to question our answers.

Throughout this unit we have built the translations between these descriptions. We began by locating points, then distinguished a position from a change in position. We combined changes, measured their lengths and separated length from direction. Finally, angles gave us another way to specify direction in a plane. Here we bring those ideas together. The question is no longer simply “Can I use this formula?” It is “Which information do I have, what am I being asked to find, and which description will connect the two?”

This lesson closes Unit 1. There is a short synthesis with two worked examples, followed by a mixed exercise set covering all four preceding lessons. Keep your first attempts: comparing them with the solutions is part of the work. You will finish by assembling a compact reference and returning to the question that opened the unit.

Allow about three hours: 30 minutes for the opening, synthesis and conclusion; 90 minutes for Activity 1.32 and the unit exercises; 30 minutes for checking and correcting; and 30 minutes for recall and reference-sheet work. These are planning estimates, not a time limit. You can divide the lesson across several sessions. Have paper and a calculator available; no programming is needed.

1.28 Choosing a useful description

Suppose two points are marked on a plan of a room. Their coordinates tell us where each point lies relative to the origin. If we want to move from one to the other, however, the two position vectors are intermediate information: it is their difference that describes the required change. Once we have that difference, we can ask how long it is or which way it points.

It helps to name the requested quantity before doing arithmetic. A final position needs the starting position as well as the displacement. A displacement needs both endpoints but does not record the route between them. A magnitude keeps the length and discards the direction. A unit vector does the reverse: it retains direction while removing the scale. Each description answers a different question.

Example 1.28 · Start with the quantity being asked for

A marker moves from A = (4, −1) to B = (−2, 7), with coordinates measured in metres. Find its displacement and a vector of length 5 m pointing in the same direction. Is B’s position vector sufficient for either calculation?

Solution

The displacement points from A to B, so its horizontal component must be negative and its vertical component positive. Subtract the initial coordinates from the final ones:

dx=(−2−4)m=−6m,
dy=(7−(−1))m=8m,
d=⟨−6,8⟩m.

Its magnitude is (−6)2+82m=10m. The requested 5 m vector is half as long, so it is ⟨−3,4⟩m. We could reach the same answer by first normalising:

u=d|d|=⟨−35,45⟩,
q=(5m)u=⟨−3,4⟩m.

The metres cancel when forming the dimensionless unit vector; they return when we multiply by 5 m. B’s position vector, ⟨−2,7⟩m, points from O to B. It does not give the direction from A to B. The starting point matters.

Notice that no angle was needed. An angle would give another correct description, but would add an inverse trigonometric calculation to a problem already settled by components. In contrast, if the initial instruction had been a length and a bearing, resolving into components would have been a useful first step. Fluency includes recognising when a conversion helps and when the existing description already contains what we need.

Key idea 1.25 · Let the question guide the representation

Use endpoints to find a change, components to combine changes, a magnitude to express length, and a unit vector or a stated angle convention to express direction. Keep the coordinate axes and units consistent when moving between descriptions.

1.29 Checking more than the arithmetic

A calculation can be internally tidy and still answer the wrong question. Subtracting in the wrong order, normalising a position vector instead of a displacement, or treating a bearing as a standard angle can each produce neat-looking numbers. Repeating the same calculation may repeat the same mistake.

A more useful check draws on information that the calculation has not yet used. Predict the component signs from a sketch. Substitute a displacement back into the starting position. Reconstruct a supplied magnitude from the components. For a unit vector, square and add the components to check that the magnitude is one. These checks test different parts of the argument.

Example 1.29 · A plausible angle in the wrong direction

For v=⟨−5,−12⟩m, a calculation gives a magnitude of 13 m and a standard direction angle of 67.4°. Diagnose the result.

Solution

The magnitude is correct. The squared components contribute 25 square metres and 144 square metres:

|v|=25+144m=13m.

But both components are negative, so the arrow points into the third quadrant. Its standard angle must be between 180° and 270°. The inverse tangent of the positive ratio 12/5 supplies the acute angle in the component triangle, not the full turn from the positive x-axis:

θ=180∘+arctan(125)≈247.4∘.

There is a direct check. A 13 m vector at 67.4° would have two positive components, approximately 5 m and 12 m. It would be the opposite of the specified vector. The corrected angle gives the required negative components.

Not every check is a proof. Many wrong vectors can share the same magnitude, and a sign check cannot distinguish two vectors in the same quadrant. Use the check that bears on the likely error, then ask whether the working actually establishes the requested result. Agreement between a sketch, the component calculation and a reconstructed length is stronger than three repetitions of the same arithmetic.

Activity 1.32 · Three results to question

Allow about six minutes. For each statement, identify the first fault and repair it. Explain what your check tests.

  1. “From A = (−1, 2) to B = (3, −1), the displacement is ⟨−4, 3⟩ m.” Coordinates are in metres.
  2. “A unit vector along ⟨2, −2⟩ is ⟨1, −1⟩, because both components have been reduced to size one.”
  3. “An arrow with components ⟨0, −7⟩ has no direction because its horizontal component is zero.”
Hint

Try adding the proposed displacement to A. For the second claim, calculate the magnitude of the proposed unit vector. For the third, sketch the arrow before using any angle formula.

Discussion

(a) The subtraction has been reversed. Final minus initial gives ⟨4,−3⟩m. Adding this to ⟨−1,2⟩m gives B’s position vector, as required. The proposed answer would carry B back to A.

(b) The proposed vector has magnitude 2, not one. Divide the original vector by its whole magnitude, 22:

u=⟨12,−12⟩.

Its squared components add to one. (c) Only the zero vector lacks a direction. Here the arrow points down the negative y-axis, at standard angle 270°. A zero horizontal component prevents using a ratio with that component in the denominator; it does not prevent interpreting the arrow.

Exercise 1.1 · A change of origin

About 4 minutes for a first attempt

Along a line, positions A and B have coordinates −1.8 m and 0.6 m, respectively. Positive coordinates run to the right.

  1. Find the displacement from A to B and the distance between the points.
  2. A new origin O′ is placed at old coordinate 2.0 m, with the positive direction and units unchanged. Find the new coordinates of A and B, then recalculate the displacement.
  3. Explain why the two displacement results agree.
Hint for Exercise 1.1

Subtract the old coordinate of O′ from each old coordinate. Apply the same change to both endpoints.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.1

(a) Final minus initial gives 0.6−(−1.8)=2.4 m to the right. The distance is the positive length 2.4 m. (b) The new coordinates are −3.8 m and −1.4 m, giving

Δx=(−1.4+3.8)m=2.4m.

(c) Both positions acquire the same offset. When we subtract them, that offset cancels. We have relabelled the line, without moving either point or changing the positive direction.

Return to Exercise 1.1

Exercise 1.2 · Equal arrows, different starting points

About 4 minutes for a first attempt

In a plane, A = (−2, 1), B = (4, −3), C = (1, 5) and D = (7, 1), with coordinates in centimetres.

  1. Calculate AB→ and CD→. Express the first using coordinate unit vectors i and j as well. Are the two displacements equal vectors? Explain.
  2. Write D’s position vector. Explain why it differs from CD→.
  3. Without recalculating from coordinates, write DC→.
Hint for Exercise 1.2

An arrow need not start at the origin. Compare final minus initial for the two arrows, keeping the coordinate order.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.2

(a) Both changes are ⟨6,−4⟩cm: for AB we subtract (−2, 1) from (4, −3); for CD we subtract (1, 5) from (7, 1). In coordinate-unit-vector notation, AB→=(6cm)i−(4cm)j. They are equal because corresponding components agree in the same frame and units. Their locations differ, but their directed changes do not.

(b) D’s position vector is ⟨7,1⟩cm, starting at O. CD starts at C, which is not O. (c) Reversing CD changes every component’s sign:

DC→=⟨−6,4⟩cm.

Return to Exercise 1.2

Exercise 1.3 · Put the units in order

About 5 minutes for a first attempt

Two successive displacements are a=⟨0.8,−0.3⟩m and b=⟨−20,50⟩cm.

  1. Find the resultant in metres.
  2. Find its magnitude, exactly and to three decimal places.
  3. A calculation instead gives components −19.2 and 49.7 and labels the answer “metres”. Identify the fault before doing another calculation.
Hint for Exercise 1.3

Express both vectors in metres before adding. One centimetre is one hundredth of a metre.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.3

(a) In metres, b=⟨−0.2,0.5⟩m. The sum is r=⟨0.6,0.2⟩m. (b) Both components use the same length unit, so

|r|=0.62+0.22m=105m≈0.632m.

(c) The faulty calculation added numerical values measured in different units. Attaching “metres” afterwards does not repair that mismatch. The resultant of two sub-metre displacements also cannot have a length of tens of metres; this gives a separate scale check.

Exercise 1.4 · An unknown vector

About 5 minutes for a first attempt

Let a=⟨2,−1,3⟩ and b=⟨−4,2,1⟩ be dimensionless vectors.

  1. Find 2a−b.
  2. Find the vector c for which a+c=b.
  3. Check your second answer by substitution into the original equation.
Hint for Exercise 1.4

In part (a), multiply all three components before subtracting. In part (b), isolate the whole vector c first.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.4

(a) 2a=⟨4,−2,6⟩, so subtracting corresponding components of b gives ⟨8,−4,5⟩. In particular, the first subtraction is 4−(−4), and the second is −2−2.

(b) Rearranging gives c=b−a=⟨−6,3,−2⟩. (c) Substituting:

a+c=⟨−4,2,1⟩=b.

The component sums are 2 − 6 = −4, −1 + 3 = 2 and 3 − 2 = 1. Substitution checks the sign and order in the rearrangement, as well as the arithmetic.

Return to Exercise 1.4

Exercise 1.5 · A negative multiplier

About 5 minutes for a first attempt

For a=⟨2,−4⟩m, let b=−32a.

  1. Find b and its magnitude.
  2. State how its length and direction compare with those of a.
  3. Find the unit vector along b. Check that its magnitude is one.
Hint for Exercise 1.5

The scalar is negative, but a length cannot be negative. Separate the effect of its sign from the effect of its absolute value.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.5

(a) Multiplying both components gives b=⟨−3,6⟩m, whose magnitude is 9+36m=35m. (b) Vector a has magnitude 25m. Thus b is one and a half times as long and points in the opposite direction.

(c) Dividing by the whole magnitude gives

ub=⟨−15,25⟩.
|ub|=15+45=1.

The unit vector is dimensionless. Its signs are those of b, not a.

Return to Exercise 1.5

Exercise 1.6 · Equal heights at the endpoints

About 6 minutes for a first attempt

A sensor starts at A = (1, −2, 4) and finishes at B = (−3, 1, 4), with coordinates in metres and positive z vertically upwards.

  1. Find its displacement, magnitude and direction unit vector.
  2. Can you conclude that the sensor stayed at the same height throughout its journey? Explain.
Hint for Exercise 1.6

Subtract all three coordinates. Distinguish a statement about two endpoints from a statement about every point on the route.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.6

(a) The displacement is d=⟨−4,3,0⟩m. Its magnitude is 16+9+0m=5m, and its unit vector is

u=⟨−45,35,0⟩.

(b) No. The zero third component tells us that the initial and final heights agree. The sensor might have risen and descended between those observations. For example, a route via (1, −2, 6) has the same endpoints but does not stay at height 4 m.

Exercise 1.7 · A chosen length in a chosen direction

About 6 minutes for a first attempt

A direction is specified by the nonzero dimensionless vector v=⟨2,−3,6⟩.

  1. Find a unit vector in that direction.
  2. Construct a displacement of length 21 cm in that direction, and a displacement of length 7 cm in the opposite direction.
  3. Explain why the same instruction could not be carried out if the given direction vector were zero.
Hint for Exercise 1.7

Find the magnitude of v before applying either requested length. The opposite direction changes the sign of the whole unit vector.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.7

(a) The magnitude is 4+9+36=7, so the unit vector is ⟨2/7,−3/7,6/7⟩. (b) Multiplying by 21 cm gives ⟨6,−9,18⟩cm. Multiplying its negative by 7 cm gives ⟨−2,3,−6⟩cm. Their magnitudes are respectively 441=21 cm and 49=7 cm.

(c) The zero vector supplies no direction, and division by its zero magnitude is undefined. There are infinitely many vectors of the requested positive length; zero does not select one of their directions.

Return to Exercise 1.7

Exercise 1.8 · Two angle units

About 6 minutes for a first attempt

Resolve each displacement into components, sketching its quadrant first.

  1. Length 8 cm at standard angle 240°.
  2. Length 6 cm at standard angle 7π/6 radians.
  3. For part (b), state a negative angle in degrees with the same final direction.
Hint for Exercise 1.8

Convert the radian angle if helpful. In each case use cosine for the horizontal component and sine for the vertical component, with their signs.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.8

(a) The angle is in the third quadrant. Since cos240∘=−1/2 and sin240∘=−3/2, the components are ⟨−4,−43⟩cm. Their squared values add to 64, recovering the specified length 8 cm.

(b) Multiplying 7π/6 by 180/π gives 210°. Hence

dx=6cos210∘cm=−33cm,
dy=6sin210∘cm=−3cm.

(c) Subtract one full turn: 210° − 360° = −150°. This describes a different turn ending in the same direction.

Return to Exercise 1.8

Exercise 1.9 · Recovering directions

About 5 minutes for a first attempt

Find the magnitude and standard direction angle of each dimensionless vector:

(a) ⟨−3,1⟩; (b) ⟨0,−2⟩; (c) ⟨0,0⟩.

For (a), explain why a calculator result of −30° from inverse tangent is not the requested direction.

Hint for Exercise 1.9

Use the component signs to choose the quadrant. Handle vectors on an axis, and the zero vector, directly.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.9

(a) The magnitude is 3+1=2. The arrow points upper left. Its acute angle above the negative x-axis is 30°, so its standard angle is 150°. The ratio 1/(−3) is also the tangent of −30°, but that direction points lower right. Tangent alone cannot distinguish opposite arrows.

(b) The magnitude is 2 and the direction is 270°, straight down. (c) The magnitude is zero; there is no direction angle. Reporting 0° would assign an eastward direction that the zero vector does not possess.

Return to Exercise 1.9

Exercise 1.10 · Read the reference direction

About 6 minutes for a first attempt

Take positive x east and positive y north. A displacement has length 10 m and bearing 150°.

  1. Find its components and standard direction angle.
  2. Which description is equivalent: “30° east of south” or “30° south of east”? Explain with a sketch.
  3. Find the components for the other description, keeping the length 10 m.
Hint for Exercise 1.10

A bearing starts at north and turns clockwise. In a phrase such as “east of south”, begin with the last named direction.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.10

(a) A bearing of 150° points southeast, nearer south than east. The components are

dx=10sin150∘m=5m,
dy=10cos150∘m=−53m.

The standard angle is 90∘−150∘=−60∘, equivalent to 300°. (b) “30° east of south” begins at south and turns towards east, giving bearing 150°. (c) “30° south of east” begins at east and turns towards south, giving standard angle 330° and components ⟨53,−5⟩m. The signs agree in both descriptions, but their relative component sizes do not.

Exercise 1.11 · What can be added?

About 6 minutes for a first attempt

Two displacements are a=⟨3,4⟩m and b=⟨−3,4⟩m.

  1. Find a+b, its magnitude and direction.
  2. Compare that magnitude with |a|+|b|. Explain the difference geometrically.
  3. Find a−b. Explain why its direction differs from that of the sum.
Hint for Exercise 1.11

Keep the horizontal and vertical additions separate. For the difference, reverse b before adding it.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.11

(a) The horizontal components cancel and the vertical components add, giving ⟨0,8⟩m. Its magnitude is 8 m and its standard direction 90°. (b) Each original vector has magnitude 5 m, so the sum of lengths is 10 m. Two head-to-tail legs of length 5 m form a bent route; the straight displacement between its endpoints is shorter, at 8 m.

(c) Subtraction gives ⟨6,0⟩m, pointing along positive x. Reversing b turns its upward component downwards; the vertical components now cancel, while the horizontal components reinforce each other. Adding the lengths would miss both of these directional effects.

Return to Exercise 1.11

Exercise 1.12 · A field route and a return instruction

About 12 minutes for a first attempt

A small survey cart starts at A = (1, −2) m on a flat site. It travels 8 m due east to B, then 6 m on bearing 330° to C. Take positive x east and positive y north. Both stages are straight. The diagram supplies the route, not the answers.

  1. Find B and C, giving exact coordinates.
  2. Find the total displacement from A to C, its magnitude, and the total distance travelled.
  3. Give the displacement for a direct return from C to A, first in components and then as a length and bearing.
  4. If the origin is moved to A with axis directions and units unchanged, which of your answers change? Explain.
Hint for Exercise 1.12

Resolve the second stage in the same east–north frame as the first. The sum of those stage vectors is a change, not C’s position. For the return, reverse the total change before reconstructing its direction.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.12

(a) Adding ⟨8,0⟩m to A gives B = (9, −2) m. For BC, the east component is 6sin330∘=−3 m and the north component is 6cos330∘=33 m. Thus

C=(6,−2+33)m.

(b) Adding the stage vectors, or subtracting A from C, gives

AC→=⟨5,33⟩m.
|AC→|=25+27m=213m.

The straight separation is approximately 7.21 m. The two-stage distance is 8 + 6 = 14 m. The cart’s westward second-stage component cancels part of its initial eastward progress; the travelled lengths cannot be added to obtain the resultant length.

(c) The direct return vector is ⟨−5,−33⟩m, with the same magnitude 213m. It points southwest. Its angle west of south is arctan(5/(33)), so its bearing is

α=arctan(533)≈43.9∘,
β=180∘+α≈223.9∘.

(d) The point coordinates change to A′ = (0, 0), B′ = (8, 0), and C′ = (5,33), all in metres. Every displacement, length and bearing found above is unchanged: neither the relative positions nor the axis directions have changed.

Return to Exercise 1.12

Exercise 1.13 · Above the plane

About 8 minutes for a first attempt

An endpoint B is specified relative to A = (2, 1, −1) m as follows: the horizontal displacement has length 6 m at standard angle 120°, and the vertical displacement is 8 m upwards. The horizontal axes are x and y; positive z is upwards.

  1. Find the displacement AB, B’s coordinates, the displacement magnitude and its unit vector.
  2. Replace “8 m upwards” with “8 m downwards”. Which of those answers change? Does the horizontal angle and full magnitude alone specify a unique three-dimensional direction?
Hint for Exercise 1.13

Use 6 m, not the full three-dimensional length, to resolve the horizontal components. Then include the vertical component in Pythagoras’ theorem.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.13

(a) The horizontal components are 6cos120∘=−3 m and 6sin120∘=33 m. Hence

AB→=⟨−3,33,8⟩m,
B=(−1,1+33,7)m.

The magnitude is 9+27+64m=10m. Dividing the displacement by 10 m gives

u=⟨−310,3310,45⟩.

(b) Only the vertical displacement component changes sign. The new displacement is ⟨−3,33,−8⟩m, and the endpoint is (−1,1+33,−9)m. The magnitude stays 10 m because the vertical component is squared; the unit vector’s third component becomes −4/5. The two directions differ despite having the same horizontal angle and full magnitude. Those two pieces of information alone are insufficient in three dimensions.

Exercise 1.14 · How much does one component tell you?

About 6 minutes for a first attempt

A dimensionless vector has the form u=⟨3/5,t⟩, and its magnitude is one.

  1. Find every possible real value of t. Sketch the corresponding directions.
  2. Explain why “magnitude one” does not by itself specify a unit vector’s direction.
  3. Two nonzero equal-length vectors are said to add to zero. Is equal length sufficient for this? State the additional condition and give a counterexample showing why it is needed.
Hint for Exercise 1.14

Square the magnitude equation. Remember both signs when recovering t from its square. For (c), compare vectors in the same direction with vectors in opposite directions.

Worked solution to Exercise 1.14

(a) The squared magnitude gives

925+t2=1,t2=1625.

Therefore t=4/5 or t=−4/5. The vectors point respectively above and below positive x, at standard angles approximately 53.1° and 306.9°. (b) Both have magnitude one, but their vertical components differ. Normalisation removes scale, not the need to specify direction.

(c) The vectors must also point in opposite directions: b=−a. Equal length alone is insufficient. For example, ⟨1,0⟩ and ⟨0,1⟩ each have length one but add to ⟨1,1⟩, not zero. If two vectors do add to zero, neither can be longer than the other: reversing one must reproduce the other exactly.

1.32 Unit 1 reference and revision

A useful reference sheet preserves conditions as well as formulas. “Divide by the magnitude” needs the condition that the vector is nonzero. The sine and cosine component formulas need the angle convention. Removing these qualifications would make the sheet shorter but less dependable.

Use the final 30 minutes in three stages. First, spend about ten minutes recalling the main methods on a blank sheet, with this table covered or out of view. Next, compare your version with the table and spend ten minutes explaining two results: why a fixed origin shift cancels in a displacement, and why dividing a nonzero vector by its magnitude gives length one. Finally, use ten minutes to annotate your reference with a condition or an error you want to remember. Consult the PDF during this last stage if a printed copy suits you.

Coordinates and displacementL01 §§1.1–1.6

Choose an origin, positive axis directions and units. A point P=(x,y,z) has position vector rP=⟨x,y,z⟩.

AB→=rB−rA.

Final minus initial, component by component. A fixed origin shift cancels if axes and units are preserved. Endpoint data do not determine the route.

Vector arithmeticL02 §§1.8–1.13
a±b=⟨ax±bx,ay±by⟩.

Include the third component in 3D. Use a common frame and compatible units. Subtraction adds the opposite; a minus sign changes every component. Head-to-tail arrows show addition.

Magnitude and travelled distanceL03 §§1.15–1.17

Axes are mutually perpendicular; components use consistent units. Magnitude is nonnegative and has the vector’s units. Add straight-stage lengths for a route’s distance; take the magnitude of their vector sum for its endpoint separation.

Scaling and directionL02 §1.12; L03 §1.17
|kv|=|k||v|.

For nonzero v and nonzero k: positive k preserves direction; negative k reverses it. Zero k gives the zero vector. Moving an arrow parallel to itself does not scale it.

Unit vectorsL03 §§1.18–1.20
u=v|v|,v≠0.
|u|=1,d=Lu(L>0).

u is dimensionless and points along v. Multiply by a desired length L to construct d; use −Lu for the opposite direction. The zero vector has no direction and cannot be normalised.

Coordinate unit vectorsL03 §1.19
i=⟨1,0,0⟩,j=⟨0,1,0⟩,
k=⟨0,0,1⟩.
v=vxi+vyj+vzk.

These basis vectors are dimensionless. Physical units belong to the coefficients. In 2D omit the third term and use two-component i and j.

Standard angles and componentsL04 §§1.23–1.24, 1.27

Standard angle θ starts at positive x and turns anticlockwise. For a plane vector of magnitude L:

vx=Lcosθ,vy=Lsinθ.

Direction unit vector: ⟨cosθ,sinθ⟩. Check the component signs against the quadrant. A horizontal angle describes only the horizontal part of a 3D vector; use its horizontal length in these formulas.

Recovering a directionL04 §1.25

Find the quadrant from the signs first. When both components are nonzero, find the acute reference angle

α=arctan(|vy||vx|).

In quadrants I, II, III, IV the standard angle is respectively α, 180° − α, 180° + α, 360° − α. On the axes: +x → 0°, +y → 90°, −x → 180°, −y → 270°. For zero, no direction is defined.

Bearings and angle unitsL04 §§1.22, 1.26

With x east and y north, bearing β turns clockwise from north:

vx=Lsinβ,vy=Lcosβ.
θ=90∘−β.

Add or subtract whole turns as needed. One turn = 360° = 2π radians. Degrees → radians: multiply by π/180; radians → degrees: multiply by 180/π. Match calculator mode to angle units. An arc of length s on radius r subtends θ=s/r radians.

A short checking routineL01 §1.7; L03 §1.21; L04 §1.27

Name the required quantity. Fix the axes and units. Sketch signs and direction before calculating. Use components for a sum, then reconstruct length or angle if requested. Keep extra digits until the end.

Check a displacement by adding it to the initial position; check components by recovering the supplied magnitude; check a unit vector by squaring and adding its components. A successful check supports the argument but does not replace it.

1.33 Unit conclusion

At the beginning of Unit 1, a distance from a station was not enough to identify a meeting place. We needed a direction and a reference. We can now supply that missing structure in several connected ways: coordinates locate a point, a position vector connects it to an origin, and a displacement compares it with another point. Components let us calculate with those directed changes; magnitudes, unit vectors and angles let us interpret the result.

A wider view · optional context

A map is already a choice

In the Manhattan photograph, the grid is easy to recognise, but the view is oblique and streets do not fill the whole island. Our ideal diagrams deliberately remove those complications. Equal axis scales and perpendicular directions make Pythagoras’ theorem useful; the real photograph does not automatically have those properties.

This is a small example of a recurring distinction in physics. A mathematical description is chosen for a purpose. A displacement can correctly describe the change between two positions while leaving out the route, elapsed time and obstacles. Knowing what the description omits is part of knowing how to use it.

The next unit asks how two vectors relate to one another: how much one points along another, how their angle can be found, and how orientation enters a calculation. Those questions will introduce vector products and projections. The groundwork is the work you have done here: retaining signs, choosing a common coordinate system, distinguishing length from direction, and checking what an answer actually describes.

Before leaving this unit, you should be able to choose a representation for a displacement, combine vectors in components, recover length and direction, and explain the zero-vector exception. Use your exercise attempts and corrections to judge which of those capabilities are secure and which need another visit. Keep the reference sheet nearby; its purpose is to support the reasoning you can now reconstruct.
