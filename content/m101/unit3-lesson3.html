M101 · Unit 3 · Lesson 3

Solving simultaneous equations
by elimination

We know the output. Which input could have produced it? The task is to simplify the equations without losing the information that identifies their shared solution.

Allow about 4 hours: 75 minutes of reading and worked examples, 135 minutes of written activities, and 30 minutes of feedback and correction. Work in several sittings if helpful. No programming is required.

Have paper beside you. Keep a copy of each original system: you will return to it to check your answer. We use signed arithmetic, elementary algebra and the matrix notation from Lessons 1–2.

3.14 From a known output to unknown inputs

So far, a matrix and an input vector have been given, and our job has been to calculate an output. Many questions run in the other direction. We may know totals made from several contributions and want to recover the contributions themselves. One total usually leaves several possibilities. A second relationship can narrow them down, provided we use the same unknown quantities in both relationships.

Imagine two types of laboratory block, A and B. Every A block has the same mass, and every B block has the same mass. One A block together with two B blocks has total mass 8 kg. Two A blocks together with one B block have total mass 7 kg. Packaging is excluded. These are invented, exact totals for a mathematical model; they are not experimental readings.

Example 3.14 · Give each unknown one meaning

Represent the two totals as equations, then check whether masses of 2 kg for A and 3 kg for B fit both totals.

Let x be the numerical value of one A block's mass in kilograms, and y the numerical value for one B block. Thus the masses themselves are xkg and ykg. The given relationships become

x+2y=8,2x+y=7.

The coefficients count blocks. In the first equation the coefficient of x is one, even though we do not write that one. The same x and y occur in the second equation: they do not acquire new values for the second weighing.

For (x,y)=(2,3), the first left-hand side is 2+2(3)=8, and the second is 2(2)+3=7. Both equal their stated totals. So the proposed pair fits the model. We have checked a candidate; we have not yet shown how to find it systematically.

A collection of equations imposed together is a system of equations. A simultaneous solution supplies one value for each unknown that satisfies every equation in the system. The word “simultaneous” refers to the requirements holding together; it does not mean that calculations must be performed at the same instant.

These equations are linear: the unknowns enter as constant multiples added together. Neither an unknown squared nor a product such as xy appears. A linear equation in two unknowns can be written ax+by=c, with given coefficients a,b and given right-hand side c. For a straight line, at least one of a,b must be nonzero.

Lesson 1 lets us collect the same system into a matrix equation:

(1221)(xy)=(87).

In the shorter notation Av=b, A is the known coefficient matrix, b is the known output, and v is the unknown input. Reading the two rows reproduces the two original equations. Reading the columns asks which amounts x and y of the two columns combine to make b. There is no need to invent a division of one matrix into another; we will solve the component equations.

Each line in Figure 3.14 contains all pairs satisfying one equation. A point on only one line is not enough. The common point represents agreement with both requirements. A graph helps us interpret the answer and notice an implausible calculation, but reading a small graph is rarely the best way to obtain exact fractions. Algebra will give us a method whose precision does not depend on the drawing.

3.15 Eliminate a variable, keep the information

We could rearrange the first block equation as x=8−2y and substitute that expression into the second. Elimination organises the same idea by combining whole equations. We choose a multiple of one equation that cancels an unknown in another. The aim is a simpler system with exactly the same solutions, not just a simpler isolated equation. Systems with exactly the same solutions are called equivalent systems.

Example 3.15 · Cancel the first unknown

Solve the block system from Example 3.14. Label its equations E1 and E2:

E1:x+2y=8,E2:2x+y=7.

Twice the first equation has the same x-term as the second. Subtract it from the second, including the right-hand side:

(2x+y)−2(x+2y)=7−2(8),−3y=−9.

Keep E1 and replace E2 by this new equation. The simpler system is

x+2y=8,−3y=−9.

The second equation gives y=3. Put this value back into the first: x+2(3)=8, so x=2. This is back substitution: once a later equation supplies an unknown, use it in an earlier equation to recover another.

Return to the original equations to check 2+2(3)=8 and 2(2)+3=7. The answer in context is a mass of 2 kg for A and 3 kg for B.

Why does the replacement preserve solutions? Any pair satisfying the original equations also satisfies their difference, because subtracting equal quantities from equal quantities preserves equality. This establishes one direction: an old solution is still a solution. We must also ensure that the new system has not admitted extra pairs.

The reverse step supplies that assurance. Keep E1, and add twice it to the new second equation. This recovers the old E2. Every pair satisfying the new system must therefore satisfy the old one too. The operation is reversible. This two-way argument is stronger than checking that one convenient pair survives.

The second line changes in Figure 3.16, but its intersection with the retained first line does not. Elimination preserves the common solutions of the equations taken together; it need not preserve all the points on each individual line. In particular, the equation y=3 by itself allows every x. If we throw away E1, we lose the information that chooses x=2.

Key idea 3.6 · Simplify reversibly

The following operations preserve the solutions of a system: exchange two equations; multiply an entire equation by a nonzero constant; or add a constant multiple of one equation to a different equation, replacing only that second equation. For replacement, retain the equation whose multiple you use. Apply every change to both sides.

Each operation has an undoing. An exchange is undone by exchanging again. Multiplication by a nonzero k is undone by multiplication by 1/k. Adding k times a retained equation is undone by subtracting k times that equation. Multiplying an equation by zero has no such undoing: it changes the requirement into 0=0, which every pair satisfies.

Only the first step is a valid replacement as stated. Add twice the retained first equation to recover the old second.

For the second proposal, both new equations merely say x+y=5. The pair (0,5) satisfies both of them but fails the original equations. The sum follows from the originals, but repeating it does not preserve both original restrictions.

The third proposal discards the second restriction: (8,0) satisfies the retained first equation and 0=0, but fails 2x+y=7. In the fourth proposal only the left side was doubled. The original solution (2,3) gives 16 on that new left side, not 8. A valid scaling would double the right side too, making it 16.

These failures illustrate why “the new equation follows from the old equations” is not sufficient. The entire new system must also let us recover the old requirements.

3.16 Write the system as an augmented matrix

During elimination the variable names remain in a fixed order while their coefficients change. We can reduce copying by recording just the coefficients and right-hand sides. The gain is useful only if we preserve the meaning of each position. A missing coefficient must be written as zero; otherwise later entries slide into the wrong columns.

The vertical separator in Figure 3.17 divides the coefficients from the right-hand sides. The resulting array is called an augmented matrix: we have added the output column to the coefficient matrix. It is a compact record of a system, not a new multiplication. With two equations and two unknowns, the coefficient matrix is two-by-two, while the augmented array has two rows and three columns.

Write the column order before beginning. In this lesson it is x,y, or x,y,z when there are three unknowns. Row 1 records equation 1, row 2 equation 2, and so on. Exchanging rows changes the order of equations. We write R1↔R2 for exchanging rows 1 and 2. Exchanging coefficient columns without relabelling the unknowns would change their roles; that is not one of our permitted row operations.

Row operations on an augmented system preserve its solutions while changing its equations. This differs from applying a transformation to a vector in Lesson 1, where the vector's components generally changed. The similarity of the notation makes this distinction worth stating. Here the unknown input stays the same throughout the rewriting.

3.17 Choose a usable pivot

In the examples so far, a coefficient of one made it easy to eliminate the unknown below it. Such a coefficient is convenient, but it is not guaranteed. The number we use to eliminate entries below it is called a pivot. It must be nonzero. Before dividing by a coefficient or using its reciprocal, look at its actual value.

A zero in the upper-left position does not by itself mean that the system is unsolvable. It may simply mean that the first equation does not contain x. If another equation does contain x, exchanging the equations puts a usable coefficient in the first position. The exchange changes presentation, not the requirements on the unknowns.

Example 3.17 · A zero is a reason to inspect the rows

Solve 2y=6 and 3x−y=3. In x,y order, the augmented matrix is

[0263−13].

There is no reciprocal of the first entry, zero. Exchange rows with R1↔R2:

[3−13026].

The system is already arranged for back substitution. The lower row gives y=3. The upper row then says 3x−3=3, giving x=2. In the original equations, 2(3)=6 and 3(2)−3=3.

We could instead have read y directly from the original first equation and substituted it into the second. That is equally valid. The exchange illustrates how to arrange the rows systematically; it is not a demand to perform unnecessary arithmetic.

When a nonzero coefficient is awkward, you have a choice. You may divide its entire row by that coefficient, or keep it and use suitable multiples to eliminate another entry. Exchanging rows to bring a one or minus one into the working position often keeps the numbers simpler. None of these choices changes the solution, provided each operation is valid and all entries, including the constants, are updated.

For more than two equations, work from the latest displayed array. After a replacement, R2 means the new row 2. If a later step needs the earlier row, it must say so explicitly. Writing one updated array at a time is slower than mental arithmetic on a good day, but much easier to audit when a sign has gone wrong.

Key condition 3.7 · Division needs a nonzero coefficient

Choose a nonzero pivot before eliminating below it. Exchange with a suitable lower row if necessary. A pivot need not equal one. If no suitable nonzero entry is available, do not divide by zero or invent one: read the remaining equations. Lesson 4 will examine those cases.

3.18 Choose efficient arithmetic and keep exact values

The method does not promise that the unknowns will be whole numbers. A fraction is an ordinary exact answer. Replacing it too soon by a rounded decimal can make later substitutions harder to interpret: a small discrepancy might then be due either to rounding or to a wrong calculation. Keep exact fractions through these small systems and decide about decimal approximations only if the context asks for them.

Example 3.18 · Fractions are part of the solution

Solve

6x+4y=7,3x−2y=1.

The first x-coefficient is twice the second. We can eliminate x from the first equation by replacing it with E1−2E2, while retaining E2:

(6x+4y)−2(3x−2y)=7−2(1),8y=5.

Thus y=5/8. The retained second equation now gives

3x−2(58)=1,3x=1+54=94,x=34.

You need not always eliminate from a lower equation; here the choice removed x from the upper equation. The retained equation still supplies x once y is known. Check against both original equations:

6(34)+4(58)=92+52=7,3(34)−2(58)=94−54=1.

Both checks are exact. The numerical size of a denominator is not evidence that an answer is wrong; the equations decide that.

Sometimes neither coefficient is a whole-number multiple of the other. You can still arrange cancellation by scaling equations to a common coefficient. This is just the permitted scaling and replacement used in sequence. Keep the original system visible so that the larger intermediate numbers do not obscure what you are solving.

Example 3.19 · Create a common coefficient

Consider

2x+3y=7,3x−2y=4.

Multiply the first equation by 3 and the second by 2:

6x+9y=21,6x−4y=8.

Retain the first of these and subtract it from the second. The replacement gives −13y=−13, hence y=1. Substituting in the original first equation yields 2x+3=7, so x=2. Both originals check: 2(2)+3(1)=7 and 3(2)−2(1)=4.

Alternatively, eliminate y by multiplying the original first equation by 2, the second by 3, and adding. The result is 13x=26. Two valid routes can look different while recovering the same pair. A clear record should show which route you chose, not leave the reader to reconstruct it from a final answer.

There is also a compact fractional route: leave the original first row unchanged and replace the second by R2−32R1. The second x-entry becomes zero. This is legitimate because the first pivot, two, is nonzero. For hand work, choose the route that reduces opportunities for error; avoiding every fraction at all costs is not itself a mathematical principle.

3.19 Three unknowns, one stage at a time

Adding an unknown makes the bookkeeping longer, but does not change the reason elimination works. We still replace a system by an equivalent one using reversible operations. The useful target is a sequence in which the last equation contains only the last unknown, the equation above contains the last two, and the top may contain all three.

The first pass works downwards, cancelling the first unknown below the first row and then the second unknown below the second row. This is forward elimination. Back substitution then works upwards. Keep these two directions distinct: we first simplify the equations, then use them to recover values.

The nonzero entries used in succession are stepping to the right as we move down the array. This staircase arrangement is often called row-echelon form. For our three-unknown example, the important feature is that there are zeros below each pivot and one nonzero pivot for each unknown. That is enough for back substitution; we do not have to create zeros above every pivot or make the last pivot equal one before reading the equations.

The solution is unique here for a specific reason. The bottom equation forces one value of z; with that value fixed, the middle forces one value of y; and the top then forces one value of x. Since every preceding operation was reversible, the original system has exactly that same single solution. Merely counting three equations and three unknowns would not supply this argument.

3.20 Check the answer and the argument

It is tempting to stop once a tidy pair or triple appears. But a copied sign can lead through several internally consistent steps to the wrong system. Substitution into the original equations checks the result against the question that was actually asked. Checking only the final simplified row risks repeating an error already built into that row.

Example 3.21 · One successful check is not enough

For x+2y=8 and 2x+y=7, someone proposes (x,y)=(4,2) and reports the successful calculation 4+2(2)=8.

That check is correct but incomplete. In the second equation, 2(4)+2=10, which differs from 7. The candidate is not a simultaneous solution. It lies on the first line, but not at the intersection. We should report the failed equation explicitly rather than say only that the answer “looks wrong”.

The valid pair (2,3) passes both originals. That verifies it is a solution. To conclude that there is no other solution, refer to the reversible elimination that forced y=3 and then x=2. Verification of a candidate and justification of uniqueness answer different questions.

A complete solution record need not be long, but it must let another reader follow the decisions: identify the unknowns, copy the system accurately, state the row operations, read the resulting equations, and check the answer. Where a context supplies units or restrictions, return to them. A negative coordinate can be meaningful; a negative mass for the blocks in our opening model would require us to re-examine the model or the working.

Discussion of Activity 3.22

The two component equations and matrix form are

2u+v=7,u−v=−1,
(211−1)(uv)=(7−1).

One route replaces the first equation by the sum, retaining the second. This gives 3u=6, hence u=2. The retained equation then gives 2−v=−1, so v=3.

Check horizontally: 2(2)+3=7. Check vertically: 2−3=−1. Two times A's displacement plus three times B's gives (7,−1) centimetres. The factors themselves have no units.

The candidate (1,5) gives a horizontal component 2(1)+5=7, but a vertical component 1−5=−4, not −1. It therefore fails. For the actual solution, the transformed first equation forces u=2 and the retained second then forces v=3. The replacement can be undone, so that unique pair solves the original system.

Return to Activity 3.22

We have turned an unknown-input question into an organised chain of equivalent systems. Elimination removes an unknown from an equation; back substitution uses the simplified equations to recover values. The row notation shortens the writing, while reversibility explains why the method works. Checking every original equation connects the result back to the question.

Not every system ends with one nonzero pivot for each unknown. A step may instead leave an equation with no unknowns at all. In Lesson 4 we will ask what such an equation is telling us, and why it can indicate either incompatible requirements or information already contained elsewhere. The instruction remains the same: read the equation before attempting to divide.
