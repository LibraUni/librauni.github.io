Block 1 · Vectors and linear relationships

Unit 3
Matrices, transformations and simultaneous equations

What happens to a whole diagram when we apply the same rule to every point? And, if we know the result, can we recover where a point began?

So far, a vector has usually described one position, displacement or direction. We have added vectors, separated their components and compared them through products. Now we turn to rules that act on vectors. A rule might double every horizontal component while leaving each vertical component alone. Another might reflect a figure across an axis. Each rule can change many arrows and points, but we would like to describe it once, clearly enough to calculate any of their new positions.

There is a second kind of question hiding behind the first. Suppose two readings depend on the same pair of unknown quantities. Calculating both readings from known inputs may be straightforward. Working backwards means finding inputs that satisfy both relationships at once. There might be exactly one answer, several possible inputs, or no input consistent with the readings. Learning to distinguish those cases matters just as much as obtaining numbers.

A matrix gives us a way to organise the coefficients in these relationships. Its arrangement in rows and columns preserves which input contributes to which output. The notation will therefore connect two perspectives: changing a geometrical figure and solving a set of coupled equations. We will begin with concrete component calculations, so that the symbols acquire meaning before we ask them to do more demanding work.

The route through the unit

In Lesson 1, Matrices acting on vectors, we build an output by scaling and adding the columns of a matrix. That one operation lets us investigate stretches, reflections and shears. We also learn to check the shape of a matrix before calculating: not every array can act on every vector.

Lesson 2, Composing transformations in order, asks what happens when one transformation follows another. We derive matrix multiplication from that question and compare the two possible orders. The intermediate figure will help us see why exchanging operations can change the final result.

In Lesson 3, Solving simultaneous equations by elimination, the output is specified and the input becomes unknown. We develop operations that preserve the solutions of a system, beginning with two unknowns and extending to a guided three-unknown calculation. Lesson 4, When solutions fail or are not unique, examines what a contradiction or a redundant equation tells us. A calculation that does not end in one pair of numbers may still have reached a perfectly definite conclusion.

Lesson 5, Determinants, invertibility and computational checks, connects reversibility with the geometry of a transformed square. We introduce a two-dimensional determinant and use transparent code to check calculations. Finally, Lesson 6, Block synthesis, iCMA 01 and unit review, brings together the methods of all three units, the Unit 3 exercises, a formative assessment and a compact reference. TMA 01 follows with its own separately allocated study time.

What to bring with you

You should be able to read signed coordinates, add and scale vectors, use the coordinate unit vectors and calculate a scalar product from components. You will need those ideas from Units 1 and 2, together with ordinary signed arithmetic and elementary algebra. A pencil and squared paper are useful: predicting a shape before calculating its vertices is part of the mathematical work.

Lesson 1 needs no programming. Before the later computing activities, you should be able to run a notebook independently, index a list, evaluate scalar expressions, use a condition and define and call a function that returns a result, as taught in P101 Unit 1. If that preparation is pending, continue the mathematical work and leave the coding activities pending until you are ready.

At each stage, ask what your check actually establishes. A correct result for one vector need not identify the whole transformation. Substitution can show that a proposed solution works without showing that it is the only one. These distinctions continue the habit developed in Unit 2: choose a check that addresses the question you are trying to settle.
