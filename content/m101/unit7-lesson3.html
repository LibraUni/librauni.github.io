Why smaller is not always better

A smaller step moves us towards the limiting definition. It can also make the computer subtract numbers it can barely distinguish.

The cube calculations in Lesson 2 gave exact reasons for the errors to decrease as the step shrank. If every operation used exact real numbers, we could continue that process indefinitely. The program uses a different representation: finitely many stored digits and rounded operations. We now separate the approximation introduced by the formula from the errors introduced while evaluating it.

7.11 Establish the exact-arithmetic baseline

The word truncation error here means the difference between a finite-difference formula evaluated in exact arithmetic and the derivative it approximates. It is present before any computer rounding. For the cube at 1, ordinary multiplication gives

D+(h)=3+3h+h2,D0(h)=3+h2.

We will consistently define signed error as estimate minus target. Thus the two truncation errors are 3h+h2 and h2. For small positive h, the forward error is approximately proportional to h, while the central error is exactly proportional to h2. This is a statement about this example. General error formulas require additional smoothness assumptions and later methods; we do not need them to investigate these calculations.

Example 7.6 · Predicting an error ratio

For the central cube estimate, changing the step from 0.02 to 0.01 changes the exact error from 0.0004 to 0.0001: the error is quartered. For the forward estimate, the corresponding errors are 0.0604 and 0.0301. Their ratio is about 2.0066, close to 2 but not exactly 2 because the h2 term remains.

We can test the program against these predictions at moderate steps. If the central output instead has errors proportional to h, inspect the implementation before blaming floating point. A predictable disagreement at large, ordinary steps is often a formula or input error.

Discussion of Activity 7.11

Expanding gives (2+h)3=8+12h+6h2+h3 and (2−h)3=8−12h+6h2−h3. The forward expression is 12+6h+h2 and the central expression 12+h2, compared with f′(2)=12. The central error is still h2 for this polynomial. The forward error has changed. Neither observation establishes a base-point-independent rule for all functions.

7.12 Nearly equal outputs

A finite representation cannot store every real number exactly. In ordinary Python floating-point arithmetic, many simple decimal fractions are rounded when stored. Operations can round again. The displayed decimal is a readable representation of the stored value, not a promise that the original real number was represented exactly.

Subtraction makes this important. Suppose the true outputs are 1.234567 and 1.234561. Their difference is 0.000006. If a toy instrument reports only four digits after the decimal point, both outputs become 1.2346 and their reported difference is zero. Each output error is small compared with the output; the error in the difference is the whole difference. This decimal illustration explains the mechanism without pretending that Python stores exactly four decimal places.

Subtraction need not itself create a large rounding error. It can expose rounding already present in the two operands by cancelling their common leading digits. Dividing the small residual by a small step then magnifies its absolute error. This is often called subtractive cancellation.

Example 7.7 · An error in the outputs becomes an error in the slope

Suppose two outputs used in a forward difference each have an error no larger than δ in absolute size. Write their errors as e1,e0. Ignoring additional arithmetic errors for this calculation, the change in the difference estimate is

(f(a+h)+e1)−(f(a)+e0)h−D+(h)=e1−e0h.

Because |e1−e0|≤|e1|+|e0|≤2δ, its absolute size is at most 2δ/h. With δ=10−6 and h=10−3, this bound is 0.002. Reducing the step to 10−5 raises the bound to 0.2.

This bounds the contribution from the stated output errors, not the total derivative error. Truncation error and any further arithmetic errors remain separate. The actual output errors may cancel, so the bound need not be attained.

Another failure occurs before the outputs are evaluated: the stored value of a+h can equal the stored value of a. The program then has no new input to compare. The guards in Lesson 2 catch this particular failure. Distinct stored inputs do not guarantee useful distinct outputs, and neither guard certifies accuracy.

Derive the corresponding output-error contribution bound for a central difference, assuming both sampled outputs have absolute errors at most δ. Evaluate it for δ=0.001m and h=0.1s. If both output errors are identical, what contribution do they make?

Discussion of Activity 7.12

The perturbation is (e+−e−)/(2h), so its magnitude is at most 2δ/(2h)=δ/h. The stated numbers give 0.01ms−1. Equal errors cancel exactly in the numerator. Thus the worst-case bound is not a prediction that every estimate is wrong by that amount, and it is not a probability or confidence interval.

7.13 Two effects compete

For the cube, the mathematical central error decreases like h2. Meanwhile, a fixed-sized perturbation in each output can contribute an error proportional to 1/h. A large step can be dominated by the approximation; a very small step can be dominated by finite arithmetic. Between them there may be a useful range where neither effect is too large.

This picture is a guide to interpreting an experiment, not a universal smooth error curve. Rounding changes discretely, signs can cancel and an individual step may be accidentally accurate. The best step depends on the function, the input scale, the formula, the arithmetic and the required tolerance. No single decimal step, such as 10−8, is best for every problem.

Key idea 7.3 · Name the error being tested

Truncation compares an exact finite-step formula with a derivative. Computing error compares the implemented numerical evaluation with that exact formula. Measurement error concerns the data supplied to the formula. Their contributions can reinforce or cancel. Agreement at one step does not identify which effects are small.

Adding a constant offers a useful diagnostic. The exact derivative of x3+B is still 3x2, and the constant cancels from exact difference numerators. But a very large stored offset can make the small output differences harder to represent. If the code behaves differently after adding a constant, that change has an arithmetic explanation, not a changed derivative.

7.14 A reproducible step-size investigation

The starter notebook and companion script include this complete experiment. It uses the same formulas as Lesson 2. The loop tries steps from 10−1 to 10−17 at a=1. The target derivative 3 is obtained analytically and is exactly representable, so it provides a particularly clear reference.

try runs the calculation; except ValueError records the guard message and moves to the next step. A guarded failure is evidence to discuss, not a result of zero. Formatting with .1e displays a number in scientific notation; .12g displays up to twelve significant digits. Formatting changes the display, not the stored values used in the calculation.

def forward(f, a, h):
    if h <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Use a positive step.")
    if a + h == a:
        raise ValueError("The forward input did not change.")
    return (f(a + h) - f(a)) / h

def central(f, a, h):
    if h <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Use a positive step.")
    if a + h == a or a - h == a:
        raise ValueError("A sampled input did not change.")
    return (f(a + h) - f(a - h)) / (2 * h)

def cube(x):
    return x ** 3

def shifted_cube(x):
    return 100000000.0 + x ** 3

def investigate(f):
    print("h, forward, central, forward error, central error")
    for exponent in range(1, 18):
        h = 10.0 ** (-exponent)
        try:
            dp = forward(f, 1.0, h)
            dc = central(f, 1.0, h)
            print(f"{h:.1e}, {dp:.12g}, {dc:.12g}, "
                  f"{abs(dp - 3.0):.3e}, {abs(dc - 3.0):.3e}")
        except ValueError as problem:
            print(f"{h:.1e}, not evaluated: {problem}")

print("Cube at x = 1; analytical derivative = 3")
investigate(cube)
print("Shifted cube at x = 1; analytical derivative still = 3")
investigate(shifted_cube)

Discussion of Activity 7.14

For the cube, moderate-step errors should agree closely with 3h+h2 and h2. At sufficiently small steps, rounding disturbs the pattern; an isolated unexpectedly small error may result from cancellation. Typical binary floating-point runs reject the last steps because adding them no longer changes 1.0. Record your actual output rather than forcing it to match a prescribed final digit.

The shifted cube has exactly the same truncation errors in real arithmetic. Its outputs are near 108, so small increments are lost much earlier. You may see estimates of zero despite distinct stored inputs: the two outputs have rounded to the same value. Zero is then an arithmetic result, not evidence of a stationary point. The input guard cannot catch every output cancellation.

A sound choice names the formula, function, input, tested step and observed error against 3. It can identify the smallest observed error in the finite table, but cannot claim a universal optimum or a bound for another function. A cluster of useful nearby steps is stronger practical evidence than one lucky row. Your clean run should reproduce the table and retain the explanations alongside it.

In Figure 7.7, the vertical labels are powers of ten. Equal vertical distances therefore represent equal multiplicative changes in error, rather than equal additions. The horizontal number is the exponent in the chosen step: 3 means h=10−3. Read the scales before comparing shapes. The plotted values also remain available in the program's table.

7.15 What the experiment does and does not establish

For a known polynomial, we can calculate the derivative independently and measure the numerical error at each tested step. For a more complicated model without an available derivative, we may compare several steps, both difference rules and simple limiting cases. Agreement increases confidence, but the corner example shows why agreement alone is not proof. Knowledge of the domain, smoothness and model remains essential.

Measured data add another limitation. If adjacent position readings have limited resolution, taking their difference over a very short time can magnify that uncertainty. Collecting readings more frequently does not automatically make every estimated velocity more accurate. The bounds in Example 7.7 and Activity 7.12 describe one controlled error contribution; a full measurement analysis would also need information about timing, calibration and correlations.

Activity 7.15 · A defensible conclusion

Two central-velocity estimates use h=0.2 s and h=0.02 s. Each endpoint position is known within 0.002 m. Compute the two worst-case position-error contributions. Explain why these numbers alone cannot identify the better estimate of the instantaneous velocity.

Discussion of Activity 7.15

The bounds are δ/h=0.01 m/s and 0.1 m/s. The smaller step amplifies the allowed position errors more. But its truncation error may be smaller if a smooth motion model applies, and the actual endpoint errors may partly cancel. Without information about the motion and data errors, the two contribution bounds do not settle the total errors. A useful report states both effects and what further evidence is needed; it does not equate a worst-case contribution with an observed error.

A wider view · More computing is not infinite precision

Choosing a numerical method and validating its result remain mathematical tasks even when a large computer is available. Higher-precision arithmetic can change the useful range of steps, but it must be requested and checked; repeatedly printing more decimal places does not perform a higher-precision calculation.

The habit to carry into the block review is to separate the mathematical claim, the computation and the evidence. A limit can justify convergence in exact arithmetic; it cannot grant a finite-precision program unlimited resolution. Conversely, a numerical failure need not refute the underlying derivative. We need to identify which object has failed.
