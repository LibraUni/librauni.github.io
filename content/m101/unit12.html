Validation, sensitivity and competing answers

Suppose one calculation says that a tank receives 6.75 litres of water, another gives 6.625 litres, and a third reports 16.75 litres. Before choosing the most convincing number, we need to ask what each calculation describes. The first two might use different approximations; the third might include water already in the tank. A difference becomes informative once we understand how it arose.

This unit develops that investigation. We will follow a simple supplied flow model through hand calculations, graphs and code. Keeping one model in view lets us separate changes in the question from changes in the method. Other examples—almost cancelling flows, nearly parallel equations and a bounded response integral—will show which parts of the reasoning transfer.

The wind-tunnel facilities in Figure 12.1 illustrate the distinction between calculating a model’s consequences and testing its connection with physical behaviour. Scale experiments and mathematical checks provide different evidence. Our supplied flow model will let us examine that distinction with calculations we can follow completely.

In Lesson 1 you will make the quantity, interval, units and assumptions explicit, then select independent checks. Lesson 2 asks how much an answer changes when an input changes. The tangent approximation from Unit 7 becomes a tool for predicting that response, while the equations from Unit 3 show why a small substitution error need not imply a nearby solution. Lesson 3 uses those distinctions to diagnose and repair a numerical calculation. Lesson 4 brings the evidence into a concise report and closes with cumulative exercises and reference resources.

You should be able to solve a small linear system, differentiate elementary functions, interpret a definite integral and construct a trapezoidal sum. Units 3, 7 and 11 are the principal prerequisites. The Python tasks use functions, loops, lists and a notebook that runs from a fresh start, as developed in P101 and used in earlier M101 units. If those operations remain unfamiliar, revisit the earlier examples before attempting the code investigations; the written development remains readable on its own.

The 18-hour allowance includes reading, calculation, four hours of Python, feedback and revision. Lessons 1–3 reserve 80 minutes for the cumulative work in Lesson 4; the lesson openings explain the allocation. Keep predictions and corrections with your work. By the end, the useful outcome is an argument you can inspect: what was calculated, which tests were chosen, what they revealed and what remains dependent on a model assumption.
