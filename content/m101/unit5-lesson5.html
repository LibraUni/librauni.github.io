Implicit and second derivatives

An equation may describe a curve without choosing a single branch. A derivative can also become the function we differentiate next.

The chain rule allows us to differentiate y2 with respect to x even when all we know is that y depends on x. This makes it possible to work directly with relations between coordinates. We will then differentiate rates themselves, keeping track of which quantity and independent variable each derivative describes.

Four hours: 75 minutes reading/examples, 100 minutes activities, 35 minutes correction and 30 minutes reserved for the unit-end set. The last section connects this lesson back to continuity in Unit 4.

5.21 Differentiating a relation

The circle x2+y2=10 contains both (1,3) and (1,−3). The relation therefore does not define a single value of y for each x on the whole circle. Near either of those points, however, we can follow one branch: y=10−x2 near the upper point, or its negative near the lower point. Each branch is differentiable there.

Rather than isolate a branch first, suppose that y=y(x) is a differentiable branch satisfying the equation locally. Differentiating both sides with respect to x gives

2x+2ydydx=0.

The extra derivative factor in the second term is the chain rule: the square is being applied to y(x), not directly to x. Solving gives y′=−x/y where y≠0. At (1,3) this is −1/3; at (1,−3) it is 1/3. The same relation gives different slopes on different branches.

Key idea 5.9 · Implicit differentiation

On a differentiable branch y=y(x), differentiate every term with respect to x, including the dependence hidden in y. Collect the terms containing y′, then solve where the coefficient you divide by is nonzero. Check that any requested point lies on the original relation.

ddxyn=nyn−1y′,ddx(xy)=y+xy′.

These are applications of the chain and product rules, not new independent-variable conventions.

Example 5.17 · A relation containing a product

Find the tangent to x2+xy+y2=7 at (1,2).

Working. The point lies on the relation because 1+2+4=7. Differentiating gives 2x+y+xy′+2yy′=0. Collect rather than substitute immediately:

(x+2y)y′=−(2x+y),y′=−2x+yx+2y,x+2y≠0.

At (1,2), the slope is −4/5. The tangent line is y−2=−45(x−1). The numerator uses the point's values; it is not a formula assigning an independent value to y everywhere. Locally the selected curve branch determines y.

Activity 5.21 · Do not lose the inner derivative (20 minutes)

Find derivatives with respect to x of y3, x2y and siny, assuming y=y(x). Then differentiate x2+2y2=9 and find the tangent slope at (1,2). Check the point first.

Discussion of Activity 5.21

The first derivatives are 3y2y′, 2xy+x2y′ and cos(y)y′. The point satisfies 1+2(4)=9. Differentiating the relation gives 2x+4yy′=0, hence y′=−x/(2y) for y≠0 and slope −1/4 at the point. Writing merely 3y2 would differentiate with respect to y instead of x.

5.22 Tangents, branches and exceptional points

A zero denominator in an implicit derivative formula calls for inspection, not automatic cancellation or a claim that the curve has ended. On the circle, at (10,0) the equation 2x+2yy′=0 cannot hold with a finite y′. The tangent is vertical, x=10, and the curve is not locally a differentiable graph y(x) through that point. Describing x as a function of y instead is possible there; it answers a different derivative question.

A formula of the form 0/0 is even less decisive. The relation y2=x2 consists of the two lines y=x and y=−x. Differentiation gives 2yy′=2x, but at the origin this reduces to 0=0 and determines no unique slope. The two branches have slopes 1 and −1. The correct conclusion follows from the original relation; the indeterminate formula does not choose between them.

Example 5.18 · Recovering the logarithm rule

Use the inverse relation ey=x to explain the derivative of y=lnx on x>0.

Working. Differentiating with respect to x gives eyy′=1. Since ey=x>0, division is allowed and y′=e−y=1/x. This is an explanation using the known differentiability of the logarithm and the chain rule, not a full proof that an inverse function is differentiable. It also makes the positive domain visible.

Implicit differentiation does not introduce a second freely varying input. We are following a one-dimensional branch and differentiating with respect to a single independent variable. Multivariable differentiation, in which several inputs may vary independently, belongs later in the programme.

Activity 5.22 · Diagnose the exceptional case (20 minutes)

For x2+y2=10, find slopes at (3,1) and (3,−1). Describe the tangents at (0,±10) and (±10,0). Explain why substituting the point (2,2) into −x/y does not find a tangent to this circle. Finally, what does implicit differentiation determine at the origin of y2=x2?

Discussion of Activity 5.22

The slopes are −3 and 3. The top and bottom have horizontal tangents y=±10 because x=0 and y≠0. The left and right have vertical tangents x=±10, with no finite derivative y′. The point (2,2) gives x2+y2=8, so it is not on this circle. At the origin of the crossing lines, the differentiated equation gives no unique slope; the separate branches have slopes ±1.

5.23 Second derivatives as new rates

The derivative f′ assigns a rate to each input where it exists. We can ask how that rate changes with the same input. Its derivative is the second derivative, written f″ or d2f/dx2. The notation records two successive differentiations; it does not mean (df/dx)2.

Key idea 5.10 · Differentiate the first derivative

f″(x)=ddx(f′(x)).

If f has output units U and input units X, then f′ has units U/X and f″ has units U/X2. For position s(t), velocity is v=s′ and acceleration is a=v′=s″, where these derivatives exist. Dots, s˙ and s¨, are also used for time derivatives.

Example 5.19 · The position, rate and change of rate

A supplied straight-line position model is s(t)=t3−3t2+2t+1, in metres for numerical time t in seconds, on 0≤t≤3. Find the velocity and acceleration at one second.

Working. Differentiate before substituting:

v(t)=3t2−6t+2,a(t)=6t−6.

At t=1, position is 1 m, velocity is −1ms−1 and acceleration is 0ms−2. Zero acceleration at this instant does not mean the object is stationary. It says that the velocity's instantaneous rate of change is zero there. Nor does it say acceleration is zero at later times.

A wider view · A photograph is one frame

To estimate successive rates from observations, positions must be associated with times and a coordinate convention. Differentiating a supplied smooth function avoids some measurement difficulties but does not remove the need to check its physical interpretation. The mathematical rate belongs to the model first.

Activity 5.23 · A second operation (20 minutes)

Find first and second derivatives of (a) x4−2x2+5; (b) e−3x; (c) lnx; (d) Asin(ωt+ϕ) with constant parameters. State domains and, for a displacement amplitude A, the units of the two time derivatives in (d). Compare f″ with (f′)2 in (a) at x=1.

Discussion of Activity 5.23

(a) f′=4x3−4x, f″=12x2−4, all real inputs. At 1 these give f″=8 but (f′)2=0. (b) −3e−3x then 9e−3x, all real inputs. (c) 1/x then −1/x2, x>0. (d) Aωcos(ωt+ϕ) then −Aω2sin(ωt+ϕ); for length amplitude and inverse-time ω, the units are length/time and length/time squared. The same chain factor appears at each differentiation.

5.24 Differentiating an implicit derivative again

When a first derivative contains y, that symbol remains dependent on x during a second differentiation. Treating it as a constant would undo the central idea of implicit differentiation. There are often two useful routes: differentiate the solved first-derivative formula, or differentiate the earlier unsolved relation.

Example 5.20 · A second derivative on a hyperbola

For the branch of xy=6 through (2,3), find y″ at that point.

Working. First differentiation gives y+xy′=0, so y′=−y/x, with x≠0. Differentiate the unsolved equation again:

y′+(y′+xy″)=0,2y′+xy″=0.

Hence y″=−2y′/x=2y/x2. At (2,3), this is 6/4=3/2. An explicit check is available: y=6/x, so y′=−6/x2 and y″=12/x3, which at 2 gives the same result. The factor 2 in the unsolved route comes from two separate occurrences of y′.

For the circle, start from y′=−x/y. Quotient differentiation gives

y″=−y+xy′y2.

Substitute the first derivative, then use the original relation:

y″=−y2+x2y3=−10y3,y≠0.

The second derivative is negative on the upper branch and positive on the lower. In Unit 6 we will connect such signs to concavity. Here the task is to calculate the second rate correctly and respect the branch on which it is defined. The apparent simplicity of the last formula must not conceal the excluded points.

Activity 5.24 · Differentiate before evaluating (20 minutes)

For x2+2y2=9, use the differentiated equation to obtain y″ and evaluate it at (1,2). Check by differentiating the explicit upper branch. Explain why substituting y′=−1/4 before differentiating again would give a false result.

Discussion of Activity 5.24

From 2x+4yy′=0, another derivative gives 2+4(y′)2+4yy″=0. Thus y″=−[1+2(y′)2]/(2y). At (1,2), y′=−1/4, giving y″=−9/32. Substitution of y′=−x/(2y) also simplifies the general formula to −9/(4y3) where y≠0. Differentiating y=(9−x2)/2 twice produces the same value. The number −1/4 is the rate at one point, not a formula for the changing rate on a neighbourhood. Differentiating that isolated constant would discard the variation we seek.

5.25 Smooth joins and limits of the rules

Unit 4 joined formulas by matching their limiting values. A differentiable join requires the additional agreement of finite one-sided difference-quotient limits. For the polynomial pieces used below, each extends smoothly to the boundary, so its endpoint derivative gives the corresponding one-sided slope once the common value has been matched.

Example 5.21 · Match both value and rate

Choose a,b so that

f(x)={x2,x≤1,ax+b,x>1

is differentiable at 1.

Working. Differentiability requires continuity first, so a+b=1. With that condition, the left quotient is [(1+h)2−1]/h=2+h for h<0, tending to 2; the right quotient is a for h>0. Matching rates gives a=2 and then b=−1. The resulting function has derivative

f′(x)={2x,x<1,2,x≥1.

Its second derivative at 1 does not exist: the difference quotient for f′ tends to 2 from the left and 0 from the right. A smooth first-order join need not have a second derivative.

This example also explains why “differentiate each branch and match” is unsafe if the original values have not been matched first. A jump in values rules out the derivative even when the branch slopes happen to be identical. Always ask what the actual function assigns at the join.

Activity 5.25 · Design and diagnose a join (20 minutes)

Let p(x)=x2+1 for x≤2 and p(x)=ax+b for x>2. Choose a,b for differentiability and decide whether a second derivative exists at 2. Then explain why the function equal to x for x<0 and x+1 for x≥0 has no derivative at zero even though both branch formulas have derivative 1.

Discussion of Activity 5.25

Continuity requires 2a+b=5. The left slope at 2 is 4, so matching gives a=4 and b=−3. The first derivative has left rule 2x and right rule 4, with value 4 at the join. Its one-sided rates there are 2 and 0, so no second derivative exists at 2. The final function has a jump from limiting value 0 on the left to value 1 at zero; differentiability would force continuity, so the equal branch slopes cannot rescue it.
