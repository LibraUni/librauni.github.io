M101 · Unit 6 · Lesson 4

The mean-value idea

Under suitable conditions, a rate averaged over an interval must occur as an instantaneous rate somewhere inside it.

We have used derivative signs to describe whole intervals, and in related rates we calculated instantaneous changes at a chosen time. The mean value theorem connects these two scales of description. Its value is not simply another equation to solve: its conditions explain when local rate information can support a claim about an interval.

Three hours: 60 minutes reading/examples, 65 minutes activities, 25 minutes feedback and correction, and 30 minutes reserved for the cumulative exercises. The theorem is stated and interpreted here; a full real-analysis proof is outside this unit.

6.15 A tangent parallel to a secant

For a function defined at two inputs a<b, the secant through the endpoint positions has slope [f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a). If f describes position as a function of time, that is the average velocity. A tangent slope f′(c) describes an instantaneous rate at an interior input. There is no reason for these rates to agree at the midpoint, but smooth behaviour over the interval forces agreement somewhere.

Key idea 6.8 · Mean value theorem

If a<b, f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), then there exists at least one c with a<c<b such that

f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−a.

The tangent at c is parallel to the endpoint secant. The theorem guarantees existence, not a unique input, not the midpoint, and not the actual value of c without further information.

One geometric way to understand the result is to subtract the endpoint secant line from the graph. The resulting difference is zero at both ends. If it is not zero everywhere, it must have a positive interior maximum or a negative interior minimum, because it is continuous on the closed interval. At that differentiable interior extremum its derivative is zero. Subtracting the line subtracts its constant slope, so zero derivative of the difference means equal slopes of the original curve and line. This explains the mechanism using the extreme-value and stationary-point ideas you already know.

Example 6.14 · More than one matching tangent

Apply the theorem to f(x)=x3 on [−2,2] and find all matching inputs.

Working. A polynomial is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable on its interior. The secant slope is (8−(−8))/(2−(−2))=4. Solving f′(c)=3c2=4 gives

c=±23.

Both inputs lie strictly between −2 and 2, so both qualify. The midpoint is zero, where the tangent slope is zero rather than 4. A solution that automatically substitutes the midpoint confuses an existence theorem with a symmetry rule. The two tangent lines are parallel to the secant but generally do not pass through its endpoints.

Activity 6.15 · Check, calculate, locate (20 minutes)

For f(x)=x2+2x on [−1,3], verify the hypotheses and find the matching input. Then consider the upper semicircle g(x)=4−x2 on [−2,2]. Does its lack of a finite derivative at the endpoints prevent the theorem from applying? Find a matching input and explain your answer geometrically.

Discussion of Activity 6.15

The polynomial's endpoint values are −1 and 15, giving secant slope 4. Its derivative 2c+2=4 gives c=1, inside the interval. The semicircle is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable at every interior input; endpoint differentiability is not required. Its endpoint values are both zero, so the matching slope is zero. Since g′(x)=−x/4−x2 for −2<x<2, the matching input is 0. The tangent at the top of the semicircle is horizontal, parallel to its diameter.

6.16 Why the hypotheses matter

Each hypothesis prevents a way of evading the conclusion. The function f(x)=|x| on [−1,1] is continuous, and its endpoint secant has slope zero. But its derivative is −1 on the left and 1 on the right; at the corner it does not exist. There is no point where the derivative is zero. The graph changes direction at a corner instead of passing through a horizontal tangent.

Continuity at the endpoints matters too. Let g(x)=x for 0≤x<1, but define g(1)=2. Every interior derivative equals 1, while the endpoint secant has slope 2. There is no matching derivative. The interior graph is perfectly smooth; the failure is the mismatch between the limiting value at the right endpoint and the value used to compute the secant.

“The theorem does not apply” is different from “the conclusion is false”. A theorem with unmet hypotheses supplies no guarantee; a particular function may still have a matching derivative by coincidence. To say there is no match, you must inspect the derivative equation and domain, not merely point to a failed assumption.

Example 6.15 · A conclusion can hold without the guarantee

Define q(x)=x2 except that q(0)=7, on [−1,2]. Does the theorem apply? Is there nevertheless an interior input where the derivative equals the endpoint secant slope?

Working. The function is discontinuous at zero, so the theorem does not apply. Its endpoint values are 1 and 4, giving slope 1. Away from zero, q′(x)=2x, so c=1/2 gives q′(c)=1 and lies in the interval. The matching tangent exists, but it was found by direct calculation, not guaranteed by this theorem. Changing the value at zero did not change the derivative near 1/2.

Activity 6.16 · Guarantee versus direct evidence (15 minutes)

For r(x)=1/x on the proposed interval [−1,1], identify the failed hypothesis and calculate the endpoint secant slope. Can any allowed interior input have that derivative? Contrast this with Example 6.15. Finally explain why a theorem check must include the whole interval, rather than just the two endpoints.

Discussion of Activity 6.16

The function is undefined at zero, so it is not continuous on [−1,1] or differentiable throughout (−1,1). Its endpoint secant slope is (1−(−1))/2=1, whereas r′=−1/x2<0 for every allowed input. Thus the derivative equation has no solution. In Example 6.15 the hypotheses also fail, but a direct solution does exist. Endpoint values alone reveal neither an interior hole nor a corner, so checking them is insufficient.

6.17 From local rates to interval claims

We can now supply the reasoning behind Lesson 1's derivative sign test. Suppose f is differentiable on an interval and f′>0 there. Choose any two inputs x1<x2. Differentiability implies continuity, so the mean value theorem applies between them. It gives an interior input c with

f(x2)−f(x1)=f′(c)(x2−x1).

Both factors on the right are positive, so the output at x2 is greater. Since the two inputs were arbitrary, the function is increasing throughout the interval. A negative derivative reverses the conclusion; a nonnegative derivative allows equality and gives nondecrease. Continuity at included endpoints extends the argument to those endpoints.

If f′=0 throughout an interval, the same equation gives zero change between every pair of inputs, so f is constant there. This conclusion cannot be obtained from f′(c)=0 at just one input. Nor can it be extended automatically between separate components of a disconnected domain. For instance, the function equal to 0 for x<0 and 5 for x>0 has derivative zero everywhere it is defined, but it is not one constant across the union of those two intervals.

Key idea 6.9 · Same derivative, constant difference

If differentiable functions f and g have f′=g′ throughout an interval, then f−g is constant there. Differentiate the difference and apply the zero-derivative result. One shared function value then determines the constant. The interval condition remains essential.

Example 6.16 · Information a derivative cannot supply

Two differentiable sensor models satisfy A′(t)=B′(t) for 0<t<10 and are continuous at the endpoints. At t=2, A(2)−B(2)=0.7 in their common output unit. What can be said about their readings?

Working. Set D=A−B. Then D′=0 throughout the interval. The mean value theorem gives a constant difference, including the endpoints by continuity. The one known comparison determines that constant: A(t)=B(t)+0.7 for 0≤t≤10. Equal derivatives mean equal changes, not equal readings. The 0.7 offset is invisible to differentiation.

Activity 6.17 · Use the theorem as a reason (15 minutes)

Show that f(x)=x+ex is strictly increasing on the real line and hence has at most one zero. Then suppose differentiable u,v on the same interval have equal derivatives and satisfy u(1)=4, v(1)=−2. Find their difference throughout the interval. State why equal derivatives at just x=1 would not suffice.

Discussion of Activity 6.17

f′=1+ex>0 everywhere, so the interval sign argument proves strict increase. Two distinct zeros would contradict strict increase; this proves at most one zero, without yet proving existence. For the second question, the constant difference is u(1)−v(1)=6, so u−v=6 throughout. Equality of derivatives at a single input says only that the tangent slopes agree there; the derivatives can differ elsewhere and the difference can then vary.

6.18 Bounds and what the theorem does not say

The exact matching input is often unnecessary. Suppose m≤f′(x)≤M throughout (a,b), with the usual continuity and differentiability conditions. The matching derivative must also lie between m and M. Multiplying the mean-value equation by the positive interval length gives

m(b−a)≤f(b)−f(a)≤M(b−a).

Thus a bound on every instantaneous rate bounds the overall change. If instead |f′(x)|≤K, then |f(b)−f(a)|≤K(b−a). This is an exact implication of the assumed derivative bound, not an approximation based on sampling a few rates.

Example 6.17 · Check whether two readings are compatible

A temperature model is continuous over a 20 s interval, differentiable in its interior, and satisfies |T′(t)|≤0.3Ks−1 throughout. Can its endpoint temperatures differ by 8 K?

Working. The bound gives |ΔT|≤0.3(20)=6K. An 8 K difference is incompatible with the stated assumptions. At least one of the readings, the all-times derivative bound or the smooth model description must be revised. The result does not identify which assumption failed. If only a few sampled derivatives lay below 0.3, no such guaranteed bound would follow.

For a differentiable straight-line position model, the mean value theorem guarantees an instantaneous velocity equal to the average velocity, which uses displacement. If motion reverses, distance travelled and displacement differ, so average speed and average velocity need not agree. Do not substitute distance divided by time into an argument about position change. The theorem also does not make the whole journey uniform. These interpretations depend on which function was differentiated. A constant average over one interval supplies much less information than a constant derivative at every instant.

A wider view · A reading is not a journey

The theorem belongs to an ideal differentiable model. A displayed instrument reading is rounded and may be based on measurements over a short interval. Do not mistake the instrument's limited precision for a counterexample to the theorem, or the theorem for a guarantee that a display must show the exact mathematical matching value.

Activity 6.18 · Bound, do not overclaim (15 minutes)

A differentiable position model has −2≤s′(t)≤3 in metres per second throughout the interior of a 5 s interval and is continuous at its endpoints. Bound its displacement. Does this alone bound its final position? If its displacement is 10 m, what instantaneous velocity is guaranteed somewhere inside? Does the theorem say when, or that this is its greatest speed?

Discussion of Activity 6.18

The displacement lies between −10 m and 15 m. A final position needs an initial position: s(b)=s(a)+Δs. A 10 m displacement gives average velocity 10/5=2ms−1, so an interior instantaneous velocity equals 2 under the hypotheses. The theorem does not locate its time or identify a maximum speed. Other velocities can occur before or afterwards within the allowed bounds.

During correction, write the hypotheses beside each use of a theorem and separate three outcomes: a guarantee applies; the guarantee does not apply but direct calculation succeeds; or direct calculation rules out the conclusion. The final lesson tests this care against plotted evidence. A computer can draw a convincing curve from a finite list of samples; your task will be to identify which mathematical claims the picture actually supports.
