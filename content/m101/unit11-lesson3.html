Missed features and trustworthy comparisons

The refinement tables in Lesson 2 were encouraging because the samples resolved the shape of the functions. We now examine what happens when they do not. The aim is to learn how to choose a useful next check when a numerical answer looks convincing.

11.9 A pulse between the samples

Imagine a short burst added to an otherwise zero rate. Its contribution depends on both its height and its duration. A grid can miss that contribution completely if no sampled time falls inside the burst. Increasing the number of panels helps only when the new sample positions actually encounter it.

Use the following dimensionless triangular pulse on [0,1]. Its centre is c=0.37, and its half-width is w=0.004. It rises linearly from zero at c−w=0.366 to height 1 at c, then falls to zero at c+w=0.374:

p(x)=max(0,1−|x−c|w).

The notation max(0,z) means take whichever of 0 and z is larger. Thus the formula is zero outside the short interval [0.366,0.374]. The total area is known from the geometry of a triangle:

∫01p(x)dx=12(2w)×1=w=0.004.

With 8, 16 or 32 equal panels, none of the trapezoidal endpoints lies inside the pulse. Neither do the midpoint samples at those resolutions. Both methods return zero each time. Their agreement is exact, and their successive differences are zero, yet the entire integral has been missed. Dividing a zero difference by three does not turn this into a reliable error estimate.

Activity 11.9 · Locate the information gap

For n=32, find the two adjacent trapezoidal endpoints surrounding 0.37. Locate the midpoint between them. Explain why neither rule sees the pulse. Then check whether an endpoint or a midpoint samples inside the pulse when n=64.

Discussion of Activity 11.9

At n=32, the neighbouring endpoints are 11/32=0.34375 and 12/32=0.375. Their midpoint is 0.359375. The pulse ends before 0.375 and begins after 0.359375. At n=64, the endpoints still miss it: the neighbouring values are 0.359375 and 0.375. The midpoint is now 0.3671875, inside the pulse. There is no endpoint inside at this resolution. Midpoint quadrature first detects a positive contribution here, while trapezoidal quadrature still reports zero.

The lesson is about information, not about this particular triangle. Given finitely many sample positions, there is a gap between two of them. A continuous bump can fit within that gap while leaving all sampled values unchanged. Consequently, those samples alone cannot give a general guarantee for every continuous function. A guarantee needs additional information, such as monotonicity, a derivative bound, or a known feature scale.

For this supplied pulse, we know where to look. Split the interval at 0.366, 0.37 and 0.374. Trapezoids then integrate each straight piece exactly. In a less explicit problem, inspecting the defining formula, refining near known changes and comparing grids with different sample positions can expose missed structure. These are purposeful checks; none turns finite sampling alone into a universal proof.

11.10 Split at a known change

A jump creates a different difficulty. Suppose a rate is 1 before x=0.3 and 3 afterwards, on [0,1]. Its total is 0.3×1+0.7×3=2.4. A panel straddling the jump replaces the abrupt change by either a constant height or a sloping segment. That panel can dominate the error even while all other panels are exact.

Example 11.6 · A jump hidden in a panel

Define f(x)=1 for x<0.3, and f(x)=3 for x≥0.3. With four panels, the trapezoidal endpoint values are 1, 1, 3, 3 and 3. Hence

T4=14(12+1+3+3+32)=2.25.

The midpoint samples give heights 1, 3, 3 and 3, so M4=2.5. The exact integral 2.4 lies between them, but the reason is this calculation; we have not established a general midpoint–trapezoid bracket for functions with jumps.

If the jump position is known, calculate the two pieces separately. On [0,0.3] use the constant left-hand extension 1, and on [0.3,1] use the right-hand extension 3. Their integrals are 0.3 and 2.1. Using the value 3 as an endpoint of the left piece would artificially create a sloping final trapezoid. A value at a single point does not change the integral, but an endpoint-weighted numerical formula can respond to it.

The last distinction matters whenever we split a piecewise model. State which formula describes each piece and use its one-sided endpoint value for that piece. You already used splitting to handle signs and physical changes in Unit 10. Here it also improves the representation that the numerical rule integrates.

The denominator is zero inside the interval. The function is unbounded near 0.3, so the finite, piecewise-continuous setting used for this unit's rules does not apply. 11.11 What kind of error are we reducing?

There are several stages between a question and a numerical total. First choose a model or a data interpretation. Then choose a quadrature rule and grid. Finally perform arithmetic with finite-precision numbers. Changing the panel count mainly addresses the second stage. It cannot automatically improve the other two.

For a known smooth function, quadrature error is the difference between the exact integral and the rule evaluated in exact arithmetic. Refinement is designed to reduce it. For measured data, errors in the readings and the assumed behaviour between them also affect the result. Adding interpolated values does not provide more evidence about that behaviour. In floating-point arithmetic, rounding and cancellation add a further discrepancy, just as they did in Unit 7.

Key idea 11.3 · State what supports the claim

An actual error compares with a known exact value. An error estimate infers a likely error from evidence such as a refinement pattern. A justified bound follows from stated mathematical conditions and limits the possible error. Record which of these you have, together with the model and arithmetic limitations relevant to the task.

Absolute error is |Q−I|, with the same units as the integral. When I≠0, relative error is |Q−I|/|I|. A relative error becomes a poor description when the exact total is zero or very small because large positive and negative contributions nearly cancel. In such a case choose an absolute tolerance appropriate to the question, and examine the separate contributions.

Example 11.7 · A common calibration offset

A one-hour rate record has a possible common offset of at most 0.2 mm/h in every reading. How large could the resulting offset in a trapezoidal rainfall total be?

The trapezoidal weights sum to the duration: this follows because the rule integrates the constant function 1 exactly. Adding the same offset δ to every reading therefore changes the total by δ×1 hour. The magnitude is at most 0.2 mm. Finer numerical interpolation of the same readings cannot reduce this common offset.

This is a bound for the specified offset model, not a general uncertainty analysis for every instrument. It illustrates why the source of an error matters when choosing a remedy.

Keep full numerical precision inside the calculation and round only the displayed result. If two answers both print as 0.693, their internal difference may still be useful. Conversely, printing twelve digits does not establish twelve-digit accuracy. Summing many terms can also accumulate rounding error; when positive and negative terms nearly cancel, the final small total is particularly sensitive. Python's math.fsum can improve summation accuracy, but it cannot detect a pulse that was never sampled.

11.12 Investigate a failure and explain the repair

The following two-hour investigation joins your mathematical and computational checks. Work in your existing notebook so that the methods retain the tests you have already written. Before running the pulse case, predict the output for at least two resolutions. A prediction gives you something definite to compare with the program.

def pulse(x):
    return max(0.0, 1.0 - abs(x - 0.37)/0.004)

for n in [8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512]:
    print(n, midpoint(pulse, 0.0, 1.0, n),
          trapezoid(pulse, 0.0, 1.0, n))

Discussion and criteria for Activity 11.11

Both rules return zero at 8,16,32 panels. At 64, midpoint gives approximately 0.004638671875 while trapezoidal still gives zero. Detection is not yet accuracy: the first nonzero midpoint estimate exceeds the exact area. Splitting at known corners gives 0.004 to rounding accuracy. Your report should show the actual nodes, the missed interval, both estimates and the geometric benchmark. A graph of the pulse alone does not establish which points the quadrature sampled.

Discussion and criteria for Activity 11.12

For the jump, trapezoidal values at 4,8,16,32 are 2.25,2.375,2.4375,2.40625. Midpoint values are 2.5,2.5,2.375,2.375. Equal midpoint values can occur on successive grids even though the error remains nonzero. The pulse supplies a stronger blind spot: both rules agree at zero on several grids. A credible report therefore describes the function and its features before interpreting a small difference. Reversal should negate midpoint/trapezoidal results for the same panel count; equal bounds should return zero under the routine's stated convention.
