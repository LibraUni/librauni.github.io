M101 has developed a connected way to describe quantities, calculate change and test conclusions. Revision brings those ideas back into use. The aim is to choose a method from the question, carry it through with its conditions, and explain what the result establishes. Recognising a worked answer is easier than producing one; this sequence therefore alternates retrieval, short explanations, independent attempts and correction.

Use this after the thirteen units. It is a module closing resource, not another unit or a replacement for the original teaching. Keep a notebook, calculator and the public examination reference nearby. Try each retrieval or practice task before consulting the reference. R1 · Representations: what does the calculation mean?

90 minutes: retrieval 10 · reading 12 · practice 28 · specimen audit 10 · feedback 20 · retry 10.

Begin with ten minutes of retrieval: Distinguish position from displacement. Write what a scalar product, signed projection and cross product measure, including a condition on the projection direction. Explain the order in BAu before calculating anything.

Retrieval self-check · R1

A position is relative to an origin; displacement is finish minus start. The scalar product is u·v=|u||v|cosθ; signed projection is u·v|v| with v≠0. The cross product is perpendicular to its two inputs and has magnitude equal to their parallelogram area, with orientation fixed by their order. In BAu, apply A first, then B.

Use the first three minutes of the reading window to check and correct your retrieval notes; use the remaining 9 minutes for the explanation.

A vector calculation begins with an interpretation. A position locates a point relative to an origin; a displacement connects two positions. Subtracting the starting position from the finishing position cancels the arbitrary origin. A magnitude then measures the length of that displacement. Keeping these meanings visible helps you choose the operation before carrying it out.

Products answer different questions. A scalar product measures how much two vectors point along one another. To resolve a vector along a specified direction, divide the scalar product by the magnitude of the direction vector. This division removes the arbitrary length used to represent that direction. A cross product instead produces a perpendicular vector whose magnitude measures a parallelogram area. Reversing its order reverses the orientation. A correct number attached to the wrong geometric object is not a complete answer.

Result R1 · Direction and scale

For a nonzero direction vector v, the signed scalar projection of u is u·v|v|. Multiplying v by a positive number leaves this projection unchanged; reversing v reverses its sign. A zero vector cannot specify a direction.

Matrices carry the same need for interpretation. In BAu, A acts first because its output becomes B's input. Calculate a short intermediate vector if you are uncertain about the order. For systems of equations, elimination preserves simultaneous requirements: a last row that says 0=5 is a contradiction, while 0=0 contributes no new restriction. A free variable appears only when the remaining rows leave something undetermined.

Example R1 · Two equations, two different conclusions

Compare x+2y=7 with either 2x+4y=14 or 2x+4y=11. Subtracting twice the first equation gives 0=0 in the first system and 0=−3 in the second. For the first, write y=t and x=7−2t: every real t supplies a solution. For the second, no pair can satisfy both equations. The calculation has succeeded in each case: it has described the solution set.

In your answers, put an interpretation after the calculation. One sentence saying what the vector, area or solution family represents usually does more than several unexplained algebraic lines. Use endpoint subtraction before opening the first hint. Figure R1 in the worked feedback then connects your calculation to its geometry.

Practice R1.1 · Position, resolution and area

Points A and B have positions (−2,1,0) m and (1,5,0) m. Find the displacement from A to B, its length, and its signed scalar projection along w=(0,2,0). Interpret that projection. With the dimensionless vectors p=(1,0,1), q=(0,2,1), find p×q and the parallelogram area; explain the effect of reversing the order.
Hint

Separate endpoint subtraction, signed resolution and oriented area. A direction vector need not have length one.

Worked feedback
Subtract start from finish to obtain d=(3,4,0) m, with length 32+42=5 m. Since |w|=2, the scalar projection is
(3,4,0)·(0,2,0)2=4 m,
in the positive second-axis direction. The factor 2 in w changes its length, not its direction. For the other calculation,
p×q=(0·1−1·2,1·0−1·1,1·2−0·0)=(−2,−1,2).
The area is 4+1+4=3. Interchanging p and q gives (2,1,−2), so orientation reverses while area stays 3. If you subtracted the positions in the opposite order, the norm would still be 5 but the displacement and signed projection would point the wrong way.

Practice R1.2 · Order and exceptional cases

Let A=(1201), B=(2001), and u=(1,2)ᵀ. Calculate BAu and ABu through intermediate vectors and explain the difference. Separately use elimination to classify the system x+y=4,x+(k+1)y=k+6 for real k. Give its solutions whenever they exist.
Hint

Apply the rightmost matrix first. In the system subtract the first equation from the second, then inspect k=0 before division.

Worked feedback
Au=(5,2)ᵀ, then B(Au)=(10,2)ᵀ. Bu=(2,2)ᵀ, then A(Bu)=(6,2)ᵀ. The shear adds twice the second component to the first; scaling the first component before or after that addition changes the result. Subtracting the first equation from the second gives ky=k+2. If k≠0,
y=k+2k,x=4−k+2k
give a unique solution. At k=0 the second equation says 0=2, so there is no solution. There is no k giving infinitely many solutions: the zero coefficient does not produce a zero right-hand side. A classification need not contain examples of all three possibilities.

R2 · Limits and derivatives: preserve the conditions

90 minutes: retrieval 10 · reading 15 · practice 25 · specimen audit 10 · feedback 20 · retry 10.

Begin with ten minutes of retrieval: Write the requirements for continuity at an input and the role of nonzero h in a difference quotient. List the rules needed for a product containing a composition; include one logarithm domain restriction.

Retrieval self-check · R2

Continuity at a requires a defined value f(a), a limit as x tends to a, and equality of that limit to f(a). Keep h≠0 while dividing in the difference quotient; take its limit afterwards. For u(x)v(g(x)), use product and chain rules: u′(x)v(g(x))+u(x)v′(g(x))g′(x). For real lng(x), require g(x)>0.

Use the first three minutes of the reading window to check and correct your retrieval notes; use the remaining 12 minutes for the explanation.

Revision of differentiation is most useful when you separate three decisions: where the function exists, what happens near an input, and which rule describes its change. A cancelled factor can simplify nearby values without filling a hole in the original function. A domain restriction remains part of the original problem even when the simplified expression happens to be defined more widely.

Example R2 · Equality with a restriction

For x≠−2, factorisation gives

x2−4x+2=x−2.
The simplified line shows that the limit at −2 is −4. The original quotient still has no value there. To make a continuous extension, define a new value −4 at that input. The extension changes the function's domain; algebraic cancellation alone does not do so.

The difference quotient makes a related distinction. The increment h must be nonzero while it sits in the denominator. After cancellation you may have an expression with a value at h=0; its limit is what supplies the derivative. This is why “put h=0 first” destroys the argument: it creates an undefined fraction before the simplification that justifies the limit.

For rule-based differentiation, mark the large-scale structure first. An expression may be a product whose second factor is a composition. The product rule then creates two terms, while the chain rule contributes an inner derivative inside one of them. Writing these as separate decisions makes a missing factor easier to find than trying to remember one long final formula.

Method R2 · Check a derivative in layers

Check the original domain, identify the sum/product/quotient/composition structure, and inspect each derivative factor. A numerical comparison at several allowed inputs can expose an error; agreement at a few inputs does not establish an identity. At a point where both correct and incorrect expressions vanish, a numerical test may reveal nothing.

All angles in the derivative questions are in radians. The logarithm needs a positive argument, while exponentials accept every real argument. Do not transfer a restriction from one family to another merely because their notation looks similar. The problems below deliberately ask for a reason alongside the result.

Practice R2.1 · A limit and a derivative from the definition

Consider
f(x)=x2−9x−3.
State its domain and its limit at 3; explain whether it is continuous there. Then use the difference quotient to derive the derivative of g(x)=3x²+1. Show when cancellation is valid.
Hint

Factor the numerator for the limit. In the quotient for g, expand g(x+h) before cancelling h.

Worked feedback
The quotient f is defined for all real x except 3. For x≠3 it equals x+3, so its limit at 3 is 6. It is not continuous at 3 because it has no value there; the extension with value 6 would be continuous. For g,
g(x+h)−g(x)h=6xh+3h2h=6x+3h(h≠0).
Taking h→0 after cancelling gives g′(x)=6x for every real x. Do not claim the unsimplified quotient itself is defined at h=0.

Practice R2.2 · Choose rules before simplifying

Differentiate
H(x)=xsin(3x)+ln(2−x).
Identify the rules and the largest real domain of H and its derivative. A proposed answer is sin(3x)+3x cos(3x)+1/(2−x). Find the precise error. Also differentiate J(x)=x/(1+x) for x≠−1, using the quotient rule or a justified simplification.
Hint

There is a product and two compositions. The logarithm argument has a negative derivative.

Worked feedback
The first term gives sin(3x)+3x cos(3x) by product and chain rules. The logarithmic composition gives −1/(2−x), so
H′(x)=sin(3x)+3xcos(3x)−12−x.
Both domains are x<2. The proposed answer differentiates the logarithm as though its inner argument had derivative +1. The other two terms are correct. At x=0 the correct derivative is −1/2 and the proposed derivative is +1/2, so that input exposes the sign error; the chain-rule derivation establishes why it is wrong throughout the domain. For J, the quotient rule gives
J′(x)=(1+x)−x(1+x)2=1(1+x)2.
The input x=−1 remains excluded. Rewriting J as 1−(1+x)−1 gives the same derivative by the chain rule.

R3 · Use derivatives to make a complete judgement

90 minutes: retrieval 10 · reading 15 · practice 25 · specimen audit 10 · feedback 20 · retry 10.

Begin with ten minutes of retrieval: List the candidates for global extrema on a closed interval and the assumptions behind that list. Write the tangent approximation with an input increment and explain the slope units.

Retrieval self-check · R3

For a continuous function on a closed bounded interval, compare endpoint values and all interior stationary or nondifferentiable candidates. On each smooth interior part, a local extremum must have zero derivative. The tangent estimate is f(a+Δx)≈f(a)+f′(a)Δx; slope units are output-units per input-unit, so the product has output-units.

Use the first three minutes of the reading window to check and correct your retrieval notes; use the remaining 12 minutes for the explanation and linked figure comparison.

Finding a derivative is an intermediate step in an application. The question may ask where a quantity turns, how large it can become, or what a small input change does. These require different conclusions. A stationary point is an input where a derivative is zero. A local extremum compares nearby values; a global extremum compares every allowed input. The domain determines which comparison is required.

For a continuous function on a closed bounded interval, a global maximum and minimum are attained. If the function is differentiable in the interior, candidates are the interior stationary points and the endpoints. If an interior point is not differentiable, it also needs attention. This qualification matters for absolute-value functions; it is not needed for the polynomial below. A complete answer names both the extreme value and every place where it occurs.

A tangent approximation answers a local question instead. At a base input a,

f(a+Δx)≈f(a)+f′(a)Δx.
The slope multiplies the input change, not the final input. Its units therefore matter: a slope measured in output-units per input-unit converts an input increment into an estimated output increment. Staying in the domain is necessary, but it does not by itself make a distant tangent estimate accurate.

Example R3 · A local change in a square root

For f(x)=x at a=16, f(a)=4 and f′(a)=1/8. An increment Δx=0.08 gives an estimated output change 0.01, so 16.08≈4.01. The line is

L(x)=4+x−168.
The square of 4.01 is 16.0801, already suggesting the estimate is slightly high. This check gives useful direction; a numerical calculator comparison would estimate the actual discrepancy. It does not create a general error theorem.

Then inspect Figure 7.2, the thermal sensor photograph, with its caption: the instrument needs a calibrated response, while the nearby tangent model uses supplied local data. Return here using your browser's Back control. These two views connect the mathematical approximation to the measurements needed to use it.

When explaining a conclusion, name the evidence that supports it. “The second derivative is negative at the stationary input” justifies a local maximum. “The endpoint and stationary values have all been compared” supports a global conclusion on the stated interval. “The input increment is small and the calculator discrepancy is…” supports a particular approximation. These reasons are short because they answer a specific question.

Practice R3.1 · Local and global behaviour

Find and classify the stationary point of f(x)=−x²+2x+3 on [−1,4]. Find the global maximum and minimum and justify completeness. Explain why evaluating only the stationary point would miss part of the answer.
Hint

Differentiate, then list endpoint values alongside the interior stationary value.

Worked feedback
f′=2−2x, so x=1 is the only stationary input and lies inside the interval. Since f″=−2, it is a local maximum. The values are f(−1)=0, f(1)=4 and f(4)=−5. Thus the global maximum is 4 at x=1 and the global minimum is −5 at x=4. The polynomial is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable inside; all interior stationary points and endpoints have been checked. A stationary-point-only search would miss the minimum because it occurs at an endpoint.

Practice R3.2 · A tangent estimate with an honest check

Use the tangent to x at x=9 to estimate 9.12. A calculator value is 3.019933774. Give the signed discrepancy estimate minus calculator value. Explain why the same line is not automatically a good estimate of 1.
Hint

The slope at 9 is 1/6; distinguish x from Δx. For the distant input you can calculate both values exactly.

Worked feedback
The line is L(x)=3+(x−9)/6. At 9.12 it gives 3.02. The signed discrepancy is 0.000066226, about 6.62×10⁻⁵; it is an overestimate. At x=1 the line gives 5/3, whereas 1=1: discrepancy 2/3. Although 1 is in the square root's domain, it is far from the base point. A line that matches value and slope at one point need not track the function over a long interval.

R4 · Integration: method, meaning and verification

90 minutes: retrieval 10 · reading 15 · practice 25 · specimen audit 10 · feedback 20 · retry 10.

Begin with ten minutes of retrieval: Distinguish an antiderivative family, a definite integral and an accumulated quantity with an initial value. Give a structural clue for substitution and one for parts, then explain when a signed integral needs splitting.

Retrieval self-check · R4

An antiderivative family includes an arbitrary constant. A definite integral gives signed accumulation; add an initial amount to obtain the final amount. A substitution is promising when an inner expression and a multiple of its derivative occur together. Parts can help when differentiating one factor simplifies the remaining integral. For total throughput, split at sign changes and add magnitudes; retain signs for net change.

Use the first three minutes of the reading window to check and correct your retrieval notes; use the remaining 12 minutes for the explanation.

An integral question can ask for a family of antiderivatives or for a single accumulated quantity. The notation tells you which. An indefinite answer contains an arbitrary constant because differentiation cannot recover a constant offset. In a definite integral, evaluating an antiderivative at the two endpoints cancels that offset. A variable upper endpoint turns an accumulated quantity into a function, whose derivative recovers the continuous integrand.

Method selection starts with structure. If you can see an inner expression together with a constant multiple of its derivative, substitution may simplify the integral. Change every relevant part: integrand, differential and, for a definite integral kept in the new variable, both bounds. If instead a product becomes easier when one factor is differentiated, integration by parts may help. Check the remaining integral before committing to the choice.

Example R4 · Why the differential matters

In ∫01x1+x2dx, choose u=1+x². Then du=2x dx, so x dx=du/2; the factor 1/2 is essential. The bounds become 1 and 2. Therefore

∫01x1+x2dx=12∫12u1/2du=22−13.
The integrand is positive, so a negative result would signal a mistake before any detailed check. Differentiating (1+x2)3/2/3 returns x1+x2, checking the antiderivative.

Physical accumulation retains the sign of the stated rate. If a quantity flows out during part of an interval, the signed integral subtracts that outflow. For a single reversible connection, total throughput irrespective of direction uses the absolute rate, with a split wherever its sign changes. Initial storage is a separate term: accumulated change alone does not give the final amount unless the initial amount is zero.

A useful solution therefore has two checks. Differentiate an indefinite result to test its local rate. Then check the domain, initial value, sign, dimensions or a bound appropriate to the accumulated quantity. An exact derivative match will not detect a missing initial amount. The final task below brings that distinction into a short model.

Practice R4.1 · Choose and complete a substitution

Practice R4.2 · Parts and a derivative check

Find ∫xsinxdx by parts and verify the answer by differentiation.
Hint

Differentiate x and integrate sin x. Keep the negative sign in the antiderivative of sin x.

Worked feedback
Choose u=x and dv=sin x dx. Then du=dx and v=−cos x. Hence
∫xsinxdx=−xcosx+∫cosxdx=−xcosx+sinx+C.
Differentiation gives −cos x+x sin x+cos x=x sin x. The extra cosine terms cancel. A missing C does not change the derivative check, so it needs a separate completeness check for the indefinite family.

Practice R4.3 · Net change, total throughput and initial amount

A reservoir starts with 10 L. One reversible connection carries water at signed rate q(t)=2−t L/min for 0≤t≤3 min; positive means inflow. There are no other flows. Find the net change, final amount and total throughput irrespective of direction. State the rate of change of V(t)=10+∫0tq(s)ds.
Hint

Locate the sign change before calculating total throughput. Keep the initial 10 L separate from the integral.

Worked feedback
The rate vanishes at t=2, is positive before 2 and negative afterwards. The antiderivative is 2t−t²/2, so net change is 6−9/2=3/2 L and final amount is 23/2=11.5 L. The positive contribution on [0,2] is 2 L; the magnitude of the negative contribution on [2,3] is 1/2 L. Total throughput is therefore 5/2 L. Continuity of q permits the fundamental theorem, giving V′(t)=q(t)=2−t. Notice that the final amount and total throughput answer different questions, although both have litre units.

R5 · Numerical evidence and short Python reasoning

90 minutes: retrieval 10 · reading 15 · practice 25 · specimen audit 10 · feedback 20 · retry 10.

Begin with ten minutes of retrieval: Write a forward difference and a two-panel trapezoidal rule from memory. Name one approximation failure and one implementation failure. Explain which evidence could distinguish them.

Retrieval self-check · R5

A forward difference is f(a+h)−f(a)h, with h≠0. For two equal panels on [a,b], set h=(b−a)/2 and use

T2=h(f(a)2+f(a+h)+f(b)2).
A coarse grid can miss a narrow feature even when this formula is implemented correctly. Missing h in code is an implementation failure. Compare code updates with the formula, then use an analytic result or justified bound to check the approximation.

Use the first three minutes of the reading window to check and correct your retrieval notes; use the remaining 12 minutes for the explanation and linked figure comparison.

A numerical result depends on both the mathematical approximation and its implementation. First write the mathematical expression you intend to compute. Then trace which values the code actually uses and how it combines them. This separation lets you distinguish a discretisation error from a programming error. Refining a wrong implementation may simply repeat the same mistake more often.

For a forward difference, f(a+h)−f(a)h, smaller nonzero h often reduces approximation error in exact arithmetic. In floating-point arithmetic, very close function values can lose significant information when subtracted; dividing by a tiny h magnifies the effect. There is no universal instruction to use the smallest representable step. Compare several sensible steps and, where available, an analytic derivative.

For quadrature, the location of samples matters as much as their number. A narrow feature can lie between all the sampled inputs. Agreement between two estimates may then reflect shared missing information. In the Unit 11 missed-feature diagram, both the pulse width and the grid positions matter; spend two minutes of this reading window on Figure 11.5 and its caption: locate the pulse between the sampled inputs and notice that zero samples coexist with positive area. Return here using your browser's Back control. A monotonicity bound, analytic integral or exact special case can check a different vulnerable step.

Method R5 · Predict, trace, compare

Before running a short program, record the inputs, each update and the predicted output. Compare that prediction with the intended formula. Execution then tests your reading of the code; an independent mathematical check tests whether the formula answers the question.

The next task uses the public specimen's trapezoidal example as a bridge. In its code, adding whole contributions at the outer endpoints disagrees with the half weights in the mathematical rule. Here you will check the weights directly, then examine a different short implementation. During revision you may run the supplied code after hand tracing it; during the final-examination format, code execution is not permitted. Practice R5.1 · Approximation and exact comparison

For f(x)=x² at x=2, derive the forward difference in terms of h. Calculate it for h=0.1 and 0.05 and compare with the analytic derivative. Then compute the two-panel trapezoidal estimate of ∫02x2dx from values 0,1,4 and compare with the exact integral. Explain what these checks establish and what they do not.
Hint

Expand (2+h)². The trapezoidal endpoint weights are one half, and the panel width is 1.

Worked feedback
The forward difference is
(2+h)2−4h=4+h(h≠0).
It gives 4.1 and 4.05; the derivative is 4, so the errors are 0.1 and 0.05 in exact arithmetic. This pattern alone does not guarantee improvement for arbitrarily tiny floating-point h. The panel width is 1, so the trapezoidal estimate is
T2=1(02+1+42)=3.
The exact integral is
∫02x2dx=[x33]02=83,
giving signed error 3−8/3=1/3. The analytic results establish these particular errors. Agreement or improvement of numerical values alone would not prove a general identity or exactness for other functions.

Practice R5.2 · Trace what is written

This code is intended to form a right-endpoint sum for ∫01x2dx with two equal panels. Give x and total after each iteration and the printed value. Identify the mismatch between code and intended formula, and correct it. If you execute it during feedback, explain both outputs.

total = 0.0

Hint

Each rectangle contributes height times width. Check which of those appears in the update.

Worked feedback
The first iteration uses x=0.5 and total=0.25. The second uses x=1.0 and total=1.25, which is printed. The code sums heights but omits the common panel width. Replace the update by total += h * x**2. The corrected totals are 0.125 and 0.625, so it prints 0.625. That is the right-endpoint sum, not the exact integral 1/3: with an increasing function, each right-endpoint rectangle overestimates its panel area. Correcting the code does not remove the approximation error. Two iterations cannot establish convergence, but the exact integral supplies a useful independent comparison here.

R6 · Connect methods without confusing the model

90 minutes: retrieval 10 · reading 15 · practice 25 · specimen audit 10 · feedback 20 · retry 10.

Begin with ten minutes of retrieval: Give examples of a spatial vector and a vector of stored amounts. State how you would find a distance, a total amount, a changing rate and an accumulation. Explain why a parameter adjustment differs from grid refinement.

Retrieval self-check · R6

For spatial components, a Euclidean norm gives distance. For amounts stored in separate containers, add components to obtain the total. Differentiate a rate to find its change per unit time; integrate it to obtain accumulated quantity. A parameter change alters the model prediction. Grid refinement changes the approximation to the fixed model. These require different checks.

Use the first three minutes of the reading window to check and correct your retrieval notes; use the remaining 12 minutes for the explanation.

A mixed problem often becomes manageable when you identify what kind of object each symbol represents. Spatial coordinates can have a Euclidean distance. A vector of amounts in separate containers has a meaningful sum, but its Euclidean norm need not represent a physical total. A matrix may transform coordinates or encode transfers; its entries acquire meaning from the stated model.

Likewise, a rate and its accumulated quantity are different functions. If q is already a rate, q′ describes how that rate changes. An integral of q describes accumulation. Read the requested output and its units before selecting the operation. This reduces the temptation to differentiate merely because the prompt contains the words “rate of change” somewhere.

Example R6 · One model, two checks

Suppose a model supplies a positive constant rate r over a duration T. The accumulated quantity is rT. Halving a quadrature panel width cannot change that exact rectangle area if the constant is represented correctly. Changing r changes the model input and therefore the prediction. If a measurement disagrees, more panels cannot repair an inappropriate rate parameter. On the other hand, agreement with one measurement supports this prediction without establishing that the constant-rate assumption will hold for every duration.

The public specimen's final question separates these issues explicitly. Its spatial transformation, rate expression, numerical estimates and measurement comparison form a compact argument. Use the supplied information rather than inventing an additional physical law. A successful answer states which comparison is being made and how far the evidence reaches.

In the practice below, write a short chain of reasoning: representation, operation, calculation, check, conclusion. Then compress the answer without removing a necessary reason. This is a useful revision of communication as well as mathematics. A shorter answer is successful when another reader can reconstruct why the conclusion follows.

Practice R6.1 · A rotation and a rate are different objects

A spatial position is r=(2,−3)ᵀ cm and R=(0−110). Find Rr and show directly that its distance from the origin is unchanged. A separate counting model has rate q(t)=4+2t counts/s for 0≤t≤2 s. Find q′(1) with units and the accumulated count. Would doubling a trapezoidal panel count change the exact-arithmetic estimate for this linear rate? Explain.
Hint

A rotation preserves distance; verify this case by norms. A trapezoid exactly represents a straight-line rate graph.

Worked feedback
Rr=(3,2)ᵀ cm, and each position has distance 13 cm. The transformation rotates the spatial vector; it does not operate on the counting rate. The rate changes at q′(1)=2 counts/s². Accumulation is
∫02(4+2t)dt=[4t+t2]02=12 counts.
Any equal-panel trapezoidal sum is exactly 12 in exact arithmetic because each chord coincides with the linear graph. This special-case exactness is not a promise for nonlinear rates or arbitrary code.

R8 · Retain and connect · 45 minutes

After a gap, allow eighteen minutes for the six prompts below with notes closed. They check transfer of the idea rather than memory of a long solution. Use twelve minutes for the feedback, then fifteen minutes to compare with your diagnostic and make a short next-step plan.

R8.1

A displacement is (0,−5,12) m. Find its length and interpret the second component.

Feedback

Length 25+144=13 m; second component means 5 m in the negative second-axis direction.

R8.2

A reduced system says 2x+y=1 and 0y=0. Give its family.

Feedback

Let y=t, then x=(1−t)/2, for any real t.

R8.3

Differentiate ln(1+3x) and state its real domain.

Feedback

Derivative 3/(1+3x), domain x>−1/3; the factor 3 is the inner derivative.

R8.4

For B(x)=∫2xcostdt, give B(2) and B′(x), naming the relevant condition.

Feedback

B(2)=0 and B′(x)=cosx. Cosine is continuous on every relevant finite interval, so FTC applies.

R8.5

A program gives the same quadrature value on three grids. Give a reason this might still be wrong and a check that could expose it.

Feedback

All grids may miss a local feature, or share an implementation defect. An analytic integral, bound or independently justified special-case test can expose a discrepancy. Choose a check whose purpose you can state.

R8.6

A measured result disagrees with a model. Explain why changing a parameter and refining a numerical grid are different actions.

Feedback

Changing a parameter changes the mathematical prediction; refining a grid tries to approximate the fixed model more accurately. A numerically accurate prediction can still disagree with observations because the model is unsuitable.
