Accumulation functions and the fundamental theorem

Move the endpoint of an integral, and the total becomes a function. Its rate of change reveals the original integrand.

A fixed integral gives one accumulated total. But we often want to know the total so far, at every possible stopping point. The difference between two nearby stopping points will let us connect integration with the derivative definition from Unit 5.

Four hours: 90 minutes reading and examples, 90 minutes activities, 40 minutes feedback, and 20 minutes reserved for the unit exercises.

Use the practice allocation actively: after studying each worked example, cover its solution and reconstruct the method on paper, then attempt the numbered activities. Use the feedback time to locate and correct the first difference in your reasoning.

8.9 Letting the endpoint move

Fix a starting input a, and let the stopping input x vary. Define

A(x)=∫axf(t)dt.

For each chosen x, carry out the accumulation from a to that point. This produces one value A(x). The letter t labels positions inside the interval; x specifies where the interval ends. Using different letters makes their roles visible. Once the integral is evaluated, no free t remains.

At the starting point, A(a)=0. At an endpoint to its left, orientation matters: A(x)=−∫xaf(t)dt. An accumulation function can therefore be negative even if the integrand is positive, when its endpoint lies before the chosen starting point.

Example 8.5 · A moving endpoint with constant rate

Let A(x)=∫2x3dt. Find a formula and interpret its values at 2, 5 and 1.

The constant-integrand rule gives A(x)=3(x−2). Thus A(2)=0, A(5)=9, and A(1)=−3. The last value compares the endpoint 1 with the starting point 2 in the reverse direction. The derivative is A′(x)=3, equal to the original rate.

For a nonconstant rate, the accumulation graph will generally curve. Consider f(t)=t−1 and start at zero. The signed trapezium calculation gives

A(x)=∫0x(t−1)dt=x22−x(0≤x≤3).

For 0≤x≤1, the graph lies below the axis, and the contribution is the negative of a trapezium with parallel sides 1 and 1−x, width x. This gives −x(2−x)/2, the stated expression. For x≥1, add the negative triangle −1/2 and the positive triangle (x−1)2/2; simplifying gives the same expression. Its derivative is x−1. The rate was negative first, so the accumulated total initially decreases.

Activity 8.9 · Different starting points

For the same rate f(t)=t−1, define B(x)=∫1x(t−1)dt. Use additivity to express B(x) in terms of A(x). Find B(1) and compare their derivatives.

Discussion of Activity 8.9

Since A(1)=−1/2, additivity gives A(x)=A(1)+B(x). Therefore B(x)=A(x)+1/2, and B(1)=0. The vertical shift does not change the derivative: both have derivative x−1. The starting point changes the baseline, not the local rate of accumulation.

8.10 Why the derivative recovers the integrand

The agreement in the examples is a general consequence of continuity. Let f be continuous on [a,b], and consider A(x)=∫axf(t)dt at an interior point. For a small positive h, additivity cancels everything already accumulated before x:

A(x+h)−A(x)=∫xx+hf(t)dt.

The new contribution comes only from the narrow strip between x and x+h. If f varies little there, the strip contributes approximately f(x)h. Dividing by h suggests that the change in accumulation per unit input approaches f(x).

We can make that reasoning precise using the bounds from Lesson 2. Let mh and Mh be the smallest and largest values of f on the short interval. Continuity guarantees these extrema on the closed interval. Then

mh≤A(x+h)−A(x)h≤Mh(h>0).

As h tends to zero, continuity at x forces both bounds towards f(x). The difference quotient is squeezed towards the same value. For negative h, reverse the short integral and divide by the positive length −h; the quotient again lies between the smallest and largest nearby values. Both directions therefore give the same derivative.

Theorem 8.4 · Differentiating accumulation

If f is continuous on [a,b] and A(x)=∫axf(t)dt, then A is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable at every interior point, with

A′(x)=f(x),a<x<b.

This is the accumulation part of the fundamental theorem of calculus. At endpoints, the corresponding one-sided rate is available.

Continuity of A itself also has a direct explanation. Because continuous f is bounded on this interval, choose a finite K with |f(t)|≤K. A strip of width |h| can change A by at most K|h|, which tends to zero. The stronger derivative result depends on the rate approaching a definite local value.

Example 8.6 · Differentiate without evaluating

Find the derivative of H(x)=∫0x1+t4dt, for real x.

The integrand is continuous for every real input because 1+t4 is positive. The theorem gives

H′(x)=1+x4.

We do not need a formula for the integral first. In particular, H′(0)=1 and H′(1)=2. The accumulation function is increasing because its derivative is everywhere positive.

Activity 8.10 · Keep the roles separate

Let J(x)=∫2x(1+t2)dt. Find J(2), J′(2) and J″(x). Explain why the first two answers need not agree.

Discussion of Activity 8.10

J(2)=0 because its interval has zero width. The theorem gives J′(x)=1+x2, so J′(2)=5. Differentiating again gives J″(x)=2x. A zero accumulated value at the base point says nothing about the instantaneous rate there.

8.11 Reading accumulation from a rate graph

The theorem lets us use Unit 6's derivative tests without first calculating an integral. Wherever f is positive, A increases. Wherever f is negative, A decreases. A change from positive to negative gives a local maximum of A; a change from negative to positive gives a local minimum. A zero of f makes A stationary but does not by itself settle the kind of point.

Return to Figure 8.4. The rate x−1 is negative before 1 and positive after 1, so A has a minimum at 1. Yet A remains negative until 2: the positive contributions after 1 take time to cancel what was lost before 1. The sign of an accumulated value and the sign of its current rate answer different questions.

When f is differentiable, A″=f′. An increasing rate therefore makes the accumulation concave up, even when the rate itself is still negative. In that case A is decreasing less steeply as the input increases. Keep three levels distinct: height of the total, slope of the total, and change in that slope.

Activity 8.11 · Sketch a total from its rate

Let C(x)=∫0x(4−2t)dt on [0,4]. Before doing any algebra, locate where C increases, decreases and has an extremum. Use triangular areas to find C(2) and C(4). Sketch a curve consistent with those facts.

Discussion of Activity 8.11

The rate is positive before 2, zero at 2 and negative after 2. Hence C increases on [0,2], decreases on [2,4], and has a maximum at 2. The first triangle contributes 4; the second contributes −4. Thus C(0)=0, C(2)=4, C(4)=0. Since the rate decreases with slope −2, the accumulation is concave down throughout. A downward arch through these three points has the required shape.

For a rate that touches zero and stays positive on either side, the total keeps increasing through a horizontal tangent. The sign-change reasoning is the same as for any derivative; integration has supplied a new way of constructing the function whose shape we study.

8.12 Variable endpoints and continuity conditions

Sometimes the stopping point is itself a function. Let G(x)=∫ag(x)f(t)dt. Define the simpler accumulation A(u)=∫auf(t)dt. Then G(x)=A(g(x)), so the chain rule gives

G′(x)=f(g(x))g′(x).

This requires continuity of f on the relevant interval and differentiability of g. The factor g′(x) accounts for how quickly the endpoint moves. Omitting it confuses change per unit endpoint with change per unit x.

Example 8.7 · A moving lower limit

Differentiate K(x)=∫x3(1+t2)dt.

Reverse the limits to write K(x)=−∫3x(1+t2)dt. Hence K′(x)=−(1+x2). Increasing the lower limit removes a positive contribution, so the negative sign has a direct interpretation.

Activity 8.12 · Endpoint speed

Find the derivative of P(x)=∫0x21+t4dt. Find its value at x=−1 and explain the sign using the motion of the upper endpoint.

Discussion of Activity 8.12

The chain rule gives P′(x)=2x1+x8. At −1 it equals −22. As x increases near −1, x2 decreases, so the positive-integrand interval shrinks and the accumulated value falls.

Finally consider why the continuity hypothesis matters. Let a rate be 1 for t<1 and 3 for t≥1, on [0,2]. Its integral exists: split at the jump and add the two constant-rate contributions. The accumulation from zero is A(x)=x up to 1 and A(x)=1+3(x−1) after 1. These expressions meet continuously, but the left slope at 1 is 1 and the right slope is 3. There is no derivative at the jump.

Changing the rate's value at that single point does not change its integral: in the sums its effect is bounded by a fixed height difference times the width of a shrinking interval. It cannot remove the two different one-sided slopes. We may still use the theorem away from the jump, where the rate is continuous. At the jump itself, its hypotheses do not support a derivative conclusion.

We have established one direction of the connection: continuous rates generate differentiable accumulations. Next we will use this to calculate a definite integral from any function whose derivative is the required rate.
