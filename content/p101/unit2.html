Unit 2
Measurement, variation
and justified precision

Two people measure the same book and write down different lengths. One includes the cover; the other measures the pages. Their rulers might both be working perfectly. Before asking which number is better, we need to ask whether the two numbers answer the same question.

In Unit 1, we gave our models quantities with units, chose origins and directions, and checked what our programs calculated. Most of the numerical inputs were supplied. We now turn towards the observations that might supply those inputs. A measurement is an encounter between a question, an object, an instrument and a procedure. Its numerical result is only part of that encounter.

Consider the decisions hidden in the phrase “measure the length”. Which endpoints count? Is the object straight, curved or compressed? Is the ruler aligned with the desired direction? Does the observer read the centre of a thick mark or one of its edges? Writing these choices down makes it possible to compare observations made on different occasions. It can also reveal that an apparent disagreement arose before anyone read an instrument.

Once the question and method are clear, repeated measurements introduce another difficulty: the readings need not agree exactly. We will learn to describe their variation and to distinguish it from effects that shift a whole set of readings in one direction. Repetition gives us information, but it does not repair every weakness in a method. A ruler with the wrong spacing between its marks does not become a reliable ruler because we use it more often.

A record we can examine

The central object of this unit is a small, well-documented measurement record. You will begin with individual readings, then learn how to summarise them without losing their connection to the original observations. Later lessons ask how many digits a reported result can justify, what to do with an awkward or missing reading, and how to explain the limits of a conclusion.

Python will help preserve and process the record. Lists, loops and functions from Unit 1 are enough to begin. The scientific work is in choosing what a value means and checking that the processing respects it. Changing centimetres to metres changes the numerical representation; it does not improve the observation. An impressive number of digits on a screen cannot supply information that was never measured.

By the end of the unit

You should be able to specify a repeatable measurement, explain relevant instrument and procedure limitations, interpret a small set of repeated readings, and report a result with defensible precision. Your notebook should preserve enough of the original evidence for someone else to understand and check your account.

The route through Unit 2

  1. What exactly are we measuring? Define the quantity, read instruments and build a usable record.
  2. Variation, bias and a fair comparison. Investigate why readings differ and why some effects persist across repeats.
  3. Summarising a set of readings. Use a mean and descriptive spread, keeping their physical meaning in view.
  4. Precision, provenance and awkward data. Choose justified digits and document missing or questionable observations.
  5. A small investigation, fully explained. Bring question, method, data and limitations together, then work through the cumulative exercises and concise reference.

The unit has a planned workload of 22 hours, including 6 hours of Python. The opening lesson takes 4 hours, including 1 hour of Python; reading this introduction is included. These estimates allow for attempts and corrections as well as reading. You can divide the work into several sittings.

For a hands-on route, use an ordinary ruler and a suitable small object if these are available. The lessons also provide clearly labelled data-based alternatives. Analysing a supplied record develops reasoning about a method; it does not establish that you have handled the apparatus. No purchase is required. We will use simple algebra and arithmetic, so you can continue alongside M101 without waiting for calculus or new vector methods.

We begin with the decision that comes before every reading: what, precisely, is the quantity we want to know?
