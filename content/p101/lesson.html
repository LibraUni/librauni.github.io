Lesson 1
Quantities, units and physical scale

A number becomes physically useful when we know what it describes. Before asking whether a calculation is correct, we need to ask whether its inputs and its result mean what we think they mean.

This lesson develops several ways to make that judgement. We will follow a quantity through a change of units, use powers of ten to see its scale, and ask whether the quantities in an equation could be combined as proposed. Finally, we will construct an estimate whose assumptions are visible enough to challenge.

Keep paper beside you and reconstruct the worked calculations as you read. The three-hour estimate includes the Unit 1 introduction, seven activities, drawing and checking your work. No programming is needed yet. The discussions are hidden so that you can make an attempt before comparing approaches.

1.1 What does the number describe?

Imagine two people measuring the length of the same small instrument case. One writes 0.24; the other writes 24. The numbers differ by a factor of a hundred. It would be premature to conclude that one person has made a large mistake. If the first used metres and the second used centimetres, the two statements may describe exactly the same length.

The quantity is the length being described. A unit is the chosen reference amount of that kind of quantity. The numerical value tells us how many of those units the length contains. Here the complete statements are 0.24 m and 24 cm. The object does not expand when we change the unit; only the number used to represent its length changes.

We often represent a quantity by a letter. Writing ℓ = 0.24 m names the length with the symbol ℓ. In this equation ℓ represents the whole quantity, including its physical meaning; it is not merely an alternative name for the bare number 0.24. The same quantity can therefore also satisfy ℓ = 24 cm.

Even a number and a unit may leave the description incomplete. “The length of the case” could mean its outside length, its usable internal length or the distance between mounting holes. Before comparing two readings, make sure they refer to the same quantity. Agreement about the unit cannot repair disagreement about the endpoints.

Note 1.1 · Choosing the quantity
A practical definition tells someone what to measure. “Outside length between the two end faces” is more reproducible than “size”. Unit 2 develops this idea further.

Key idea 1.1 · A physical value needs its meaning

State the quantity, its numerical value and its unit. When the procedure or reference choice affects the meaning, state that too. Changing units changes the numerical value, not the physical quantity.

Example 1.1 · Comparing two descriptions

A report gives a rail length as 1.8 m. Another gives 1800 mm. Do these values disagree?

Compare them in one unit before comparing the numbers.

Solution

A metre contains 1000 millimetres. Thus 1.8 m contains 1.8 × 1000 = 1800 millimetres. The stated values agree as quantities. Whether the measurements themselves agree within their precision is a further question; the statements alone do not tell us about the instruments or procedures.

Activity 1.1 · What is missing?

A notebook says “length = 36; time = 12 s; result = 3”. Write two different physically meaningful interpretations of the length and result. Then name one detail, besides units, needed to make the timing repeatable.

Hint for Activity 1.1

Try centimetres for the first interpretation and metres for the second. Suppose the result is length divided by duration.

Discussion of Activity 1.1

One interpretation is 36 cm divided by 12 s, giving 3 cm/s. Another is 36 m divided by 12 s, giving 3 m/s. The arithmetic is the same, but the second rate is a hundred times larger. To repeat the timing, specify its start and end events—for example, the front of a trolley passing two fixed markers. You might also ask whether 12 s is the duration between those events or a clock reading. Other clear interpretations are possible; the aim is to expose the missing information.

We now have a reason for carrying units through a calculation: they preserve part of the meaning that the numbers alone would lose. To exchange results reliably, we also need agreed reference units.

1.2 A shared language of units

In this module our default system is the International System of Units, abbreviated SI. We begin mainly with length, time and mass. Their SI base units are the metre, second and kilogram, written m, s and kg. Unit symbols are case-sensitive: m is not M. They do not take a plural ending, so we write 5 kg rather than 5 kgs, with a space between the number and the symbol.

SI has seven base units. The remaining four are the ampere for electric current, kelvin for thermodynamic temperature, mole for amount of substance and candela for luminous intensity. You do not need to learn the physics of all seven quantities now. They are part of the wider reference system; we will develop the relevant quantities when we meet electricity, thermal ideas and matter.

Other units can be built from these. An area calculated by multiplying two lengths has a unit such as m × m = m². A volume has a unit such as m³. A distance divided by an elapsed time has a unit m/s, read “metres per second”. These are derived units. Their structure keeps a record of the calculation used to form the quantity.

1.2.1 Choosing a convenient size of unit

A metre is convenient for the dimensions of a room, but awkward for a very thin sheet or a long route. Prefixes let us express multiples or fractions of the same unit. The prefix kilo means a thousand, so one kilometre is 1000 metres. Milli means one thousandth, so one millimetre is 0.001 metre. Using a smaller unit requires a larger numerical value for the same length.

The same prefix can be attached to different unit symbols: ms means millisecond and mm means millimetre. The m on its own means metre; the first m in mm supplies the prefix. Context and typography matter. Write m s, with a space, for a product of metre and second; write ms, without a space, for a millisecond.

Mass has a small naming peculiarity: the SI base unit is already called the kilogram. For smaller masses, prefixes are attached to gram: 1 g = 0.001 kg and 1 mg = 0.001 g = 0.000001 kg. We do not stack prefixes onto kilogram. For now, concentrate on recording the unit explicitly and converting it carefully rather than memorising an extensive list.

A wider view · optional reading

A standard that can be shared

A ruler is a measuring tool, but a particular ruler is not the definition of a metre. In the present SI, the units are defined through fixed numerical values of physical constants. For example, the speed of light in vacuum is exactly 299 792 458 m/s in this system. Together with the definition of the second, this establishes the metre.

An exact definition does not make every measurement exact. An actual instrument still has limitations, and using it well still matters. The distinction between a shared standard and a particular measurement will become central in Unit 2. See the BIPM’s explanation of the SI.

Activity 1.2 · Predict the direction of the change

  1. Express 4.6 mm in metres. Before calculating, decide whether the numerical value should increase or decrease.
  2. Express 0.032 s in milliseconds.
  3. A sample has mass 250 mg. Express it in grams and then kilograms.
Discussion of Activity 1.2

(a) Metres are larger units, so fewer are needed: 4.6 mm = 4.6 × 0.001 m = 0.0046 m. (b) Milliseconds are smaller units, so the numerical value increases: 0.032 s = 32 ms. (c) Since 1000 mg = 1 g, 250 mg = 0.250 g. Dividing by another thousand gives 0.000250 kg. The mass has not changed at either step. Reversing the conversion returns 250 mg.

1.3 Changing units without changing the quantity

It is easy to remember that metres and centimetres differ by a factor of a hundred, yet still multiply when you should divide. A reliable approach is to write the conversion as a ratio of equal quantities. Because 100 cm and 1 m are the same length, the ratio (100 cm)/(1 m) is equal to one. Multiplying a quantity by that ratio changes its written form without changing its value.

For example, to convert 0.24 m into centimetres, arrange the ratio so that metres cancel:

0.24 m × (100 cm / 1 m) = 24 cm.

To convert back, use the reciprocal ratio: 24 cm × (1 m / 100 cm) = 0.24 m. Unit cancellation does not replace reasoning, but it makes the choice of factor visible. If you accidentally used the first ratio for the return conversion, you would be left with cm²/m rather than the intended metres.

Example 1.2 · Converting a rate

A conveyor is specified to advance at 0.90 m/min. Express the rate in cm/s.

Change the length unit and the time unit separately, keeping their positions in the quotient.

Solution
0.90 m/min × (100 cm / 1 m)
× (1 min / 60 s) = 1.5 cm/s.

The factor for time is 1 min / 60 s because minutes began in the denominator. As a check, 1.5 cm every second would give 90 cm in 60 seconds, which is 0.90 m in a minute. Dividing 90 by 60 is appropriate; multiplying would claim a much larger distance in a second than in a minute.

1.3.1 Why square units need extra care

Suppose a square has sides of length 1 cm. In millimetres, each side is 10 mm. Its area can be calculated either as 1 cm × 1 cm = 1 cm² or as 10 mm × 10 mm = 100 mm². Both lengths changed by a factor of ten, so the numerical area changed by a factor of a hundred.

In metres, 1 cm² = (0.01 m)² = 0.0001 m². Notice the brackets: the prefix factor is squared along with the unit. Similarly, 1 cm³ = (0.01 m)³ = 0.000001 m³. An error in a length conversion can therefore become a much larger error in a volume calculation.

Key idea 1.2 · Convert the whole unit

For squared or cubed units, square or cube the conversion factor. For a quotient, keep track of which unit is in the numerator and which is in the denominator. Finish by checking the size of the answer as well as its unit.

Activity 1.3 · A misleading label

A rectangular sensor face measures 25 mm by 40 mm. Its label gives the area as “1000 mm² = 1 m²”. Calculate the area in cm² and m², identify the mistake and give a simple physical reason why 1 m² is implausible.

Hint for Activity 1.3

Convert each side first. You can then check your answer by converting the area directly.

Discussion of Activity 1.3

The sides are 2.5 cm and 4.0 cm, giving 10 cm². In metres they are 0.025 m and 0.040 m, giving 0.001 m². Equivalently, 1000 mm² × (1 m / 1000 mm)² = 0.001 m². The label divided by a thousand rather than a million. A face only a few centimetres across cannot cover a square a metre on each side.

That final size check did something the arithmetic alone could not do: it connected the result back to the object. The next section develops a compact way to compare sizes that are far apart.

1.4 Making scale visible

A measurement expressed as 0.0000048 m is correct but easy to misread. Writing it as 4.8 × 10−6 m separates a manageable number from a scale factor. This is scientific notation. For a positive nonzero value, we write a coefficient at least 1 and less than 10, multiplied by an integer power of ten. A negative value has a negative coefficient whose magnitude lies in the same interval. Zero is simply written as zero.

The exponent counts factors of ten, not a physical sign. A positive exponent makes the scale factor larger than or equal to ten: 10³ = 1000. A negative exponent gives a reciprocal: 10−3 = 1/1000 = 0.001. Thus 4.8 × 10−6 m is a small positive length, not a negative length. And 10⁰ = 1, so 4.8 × 10⁰ m is just 4.8 m.

Example 1.3 · Seeing a ratio

A stipulated route length is 2.4 km and a stipulated spacing is 0.80 mm. How many such spacings would fit along the route, neglecting any gaps or bends?

Solution

First express both lengths in metres: 2.4 km = 2.4 × 10³ m and 0.80 mm = 8.0 × 10−4 m. Dividing gives

(2.4 × 10³ m) / (8.0 × 10−4 m)
= 0.30 × 10⁷ = 3.0 × 10⁶.

The metre units cancel. Dividing the powers subtracts their exponents: 3 − (−4) = 7. The result is three million spacings. To check it another way, 2.4 km is 2 400 000 mm; dividing by 0.80 mm again gives 3 000 000. Comparing 2.4 with 0.80 before converting would have hidden a factor of a million.

The difference between addition and multiplication matters when we talk about physical scale. A centimetre added to a millimetre is a large relative change; a centimetre added to a kilometre is tiny by comparison. A ratio asks how many times larger one value is than another, so it is often the useful comparison across very different sizes.

“Order of magnitude” usually refers to a rough power-of-ten scale. Conventions for choosing a single power differ near the boundary between scales. Here, when precision matters, we will state the scientific notation or an explicit ratio instead of relying on that phrase alone. An estimate of 6 × 10³ and one of 9 × 10³ differ substantially less than an estimate of 6 × 10³ and one of 6 × 10⁶.

Activity 1.4 · Numbers on different scales

  1. Write 0.000072 s in scientific notation and in microseconds.
  2. Write 5.6 × 10⁴ m in kilometres.
  3. One duration is 3.0 ms and another is 6.0 µs. How many times longer is the first?
  4. Explain why 10−5 m is positive and is smaller than 10−4 m.
Discussion of Activity 1.4

(a) 7.2 × 10−5 s = 72 µs. Multiplying 7.2 by 0.00001 recovers the original value. (b) 56 000 m = 56 km. (c) The durations are 3.0 × 10−3 s and 6.0 × 10−6 s. Their ratio is 0.50 × 10³ = 500. (d) A negative exponent means a reciprocal power: 10−5 = 1/100 000, which is positive and one tenth of 1/10 000.

Seeing detail is not yet measuring it

Magnification can reveal a world of structure that we cannot see directly. But making an image larger on a screen does not make the object larger. To turn a feature in a picture into a physical length, we need calibration information, such as a scale bar whose length represents a known distance. 1.5 What dimensions can tell us

A length can be expressed in metres or centimetres; a duration in seconds or minutes. In each case the unit changes while the kind of quantity stays the same. Dimensions describe that underlying kind of dependence. We use L for length, T for time and M for mass. These upright capital letters are dimension symbols, not SI unit symbols. In particular, dimension T does not mean a unit called “T”.

Square brackets around a quantity symbol will denote its dimensions in this section. If d is a distance, [d] = L. If τ is an elapsed time, [τ] = T. A rate formed as d/τ therefore has dimensions L/T, which can also be written LT−1. The same statement applies whether the rate was reported in m/s or km/h.

Products and quotients combine dimensions in the same way that they combine units. For a rectangular area A = ab, both lengths contribute a factor L, giving [A] = L². A ratio of two lengths has dimension L/L = 1. We call such a ratio dimensionless. It can still have a useful physical meaning—for example, the ratio of a model’s length to the real object’s length.

1.5.1 Checking an equation before calculating

Adding quantities imposes a different requirement. We can add 2 m and 30 cm after expressing them in compatible units, obtaining 2.30 m. We cannot form a physical length by adding 2 m and 30 s. Even though the bare numbers can be added, they count unlike quantities. Terms added or subtracted in a physical equation must have matching dimensions, and the two sides of the equation must match.

Example 1.4 · A missing or misplaced time

A model uses a distance d, a constant rate r with dimensions L/T, and an elapsed time τ with dimensions T. Which proposed expressions for d are dimensionally possible: rτ, r/τ, or r + τ?

Solution

The product has dimensions (L/T) × T = L, so rτ could represent a distance. The quotient has dimensions (L/T)/T = L/T², so it cannot represent a distance. The sum tries to add a rate to a time; its terms do not match, so it is not a meaningful physical sum.

This checks the structure, not the truth of the model. The expression 2rτ also has dimensions L. Dimensions alone cannot decide whether the factor 2 belongs there, or whether the rate really remains constant.

Key idea 1.3 · A necessary check, not a proof

A physically meaningful equation must be dimensionally consistent. Passing that check does not establish its numerical factors, signs, assumptions or agreement with observations. Dimensions can reject some wrong expressions; they cannot certify a model.

Matching dimensions also does not guarantee that two quantities play the same role. Position and distance both have dimensions L, yet they answer different questions. Nor does a dimension check notice that one input was typed in centimetres into a calculation expecting metres. Both are lengths. We need both a dimensional check of the relationship and a unit check of the particular values.

Activity 1.5 · What can you rule out?

A rectangular panel has positive side lengths a and b. Someone proposes three expressions for its area: a + b, ab and 2ab.

  1. Use dimensions to decide which expression must be wrong.
  2. Can dimensions distinguish the other two?
  3. Use a 3 m by 2 m rectangle to decide which expression gives its area, and explain what this extra check adds.
Discussion of Activity 1.5

(a) The sum has dimensions L, while area has dimensions L², so a + b cannot give the area. (b) Both ab and 2ab have dimensions L². (c) A rectangle three metres across and two metres high contains six one-square-metre squares. Its area is 6 m², given by ab; 2ab gives 12 m². The geometric interpretation distinguishes expressions that passed the same dimension check. One example is not a proof for every shape; here it exposes the erroneous factor for the proposed rectangle rule.

Activity 1.6 · Right dimensions, wrong input

A calculation expects a length in metres and a duration in seconds. For a 75 cm journey lasting 5 s, someone types 75/5 and labels the answer 15 m/s. Explain why a dimension check can pass while the result is wrong. Give the corrected rate and a reverse check.

Discussion of Activity 1.6

Length divided by time has the right dimensions regardless of the length unit. But the input should be 0.75 m, so the corrected rate is 0.75 m / 5 s = 0.15 m/s. At that rate, five seconds corresponds to 0.75 m, as required. The raw calculation 75/5 could correctly give 15 cm/s if centimetres were the declared length convention. The mistake is attaching metres to a value calculated in centimetres.

This distinction will matter when we program. A machine manipulates the values it is given; it does not automatically know that the label on a result has changed its physical meaning.

1.6 An estimate with reasons

Not every useful physical question requires a precise measurement at the outset. Before choosing a container, for example, we might want to know whether it holds roughly half a litre or several litres. An estimate can answer that question by combining plausible dimensions with a simple shape. The purpose is to make a reasoned judgement at an appropriate scale, not to produce many decimal places.

Consider an imagined cylindrical container. Suppose its internal diameter is about 8 cm and its usable height about 12 cm. We will neglect wall curvature and treat the interior as a perfect cylinder. This is already a model: the real object need not have an exactly circular cross-section or perfectly vertical sides.

Example 1.5 · How much would it hold?

Estimate the volume of the stipulated cylindrical interior. Use the circular-area formula A = πr² and cylinder volume V = Ah, where r is radius and h is height. Take π ≈ 3 for a first rough estimate. One litre is 1000 cm³.

Turn the stated diameter into a radius before calculating the cross-sectional area.

Solution

The radius is about 4 cm, half of the 8 cm diameter. The approximate area is 3 × (4 cm)² = 48 cm². Multiplying this cross-sectional area by the height gives

V ≈ 48 cm² × 12 cm = 576 cm³ ≈ 0.6 L.

Using π from a calculator gives about 603 cm³, still roughly 0.6 L. Reporting 0.603185789 L would conceal the roughness of the inputs and idealised shape. Our conclusion is a capacity of around six tenths of a litre under the stated assumptions, not an accurately measured capacity.

As a size check, a box 8 cm by 8 cm by 12 cm has volume 768 cm³. The cylinder fits inside that box, so its volume should be smaller. This catches some errors, including accidentally using the diameter as the radius, which would multiply the cylinder result by four.

The estimate becomes more informative if we ask which assumption matters. Radius appears squared, whereas height appears only once. A factor of two in radius gives a factor of four in volume for a fixed height; a factor of two in height gives a factor of two in volume for a fixed radius. This comparison is about how the formula responds to changes, not a formal theory of measurement uncertainty.

Activity 1.7 · A useful range, not a decorative decimal

Suppose the internal radius is plausibly between 3.5 cm and 4.5 cm, and the usable fill height between 10 cm and 12 cm. For this rough exercise, use π ≈ 3.

  1. Estimate a lower and an upper volume using these assumed ranges.
  2. Would a claim that the container holds 5 L be credible under these assumptions?
  3. Give one physical measurement that would help narrow the estimate. Explain why your interval is not a statistically established confidence interval.
Hint for Activity 1.7

For positive radius and height, the volume grows as either grows. Combine the two smallest allowed values for the lower calculation and the two largest for the upper calculation.

Discussion of Activity 1.7

The lower calculation is 3 × 3.5² × 10 = 367.5 cm³, and the upper is 3 × 4.5² × 12 = 729 cm³. A useful rough statement is about 0.4 L to somewhat over 0.7 L. These endpoints use π ≈ 3 and assumed size ranges: they are not exact physical bounds. Using π ≈ 3.14 would give about 0.38–0.76 L.

Five litres is far outside this scale. It would require a major failure of the dimensions, units or shape assumptions. Measuring the internal diameter at representative heights would help test both the size and cylindrical approximation; measuring the actual fill height would also help. This range reflects choices about plausible inputs. No probability model or repeated-measurement analysis has established a specified chance that the true volume lies inside it.

1.6.1 Returning to the physical claim

We began with numbers that needed interpretation. We can now ask a sequence of more precise questions. What quantity is being described? What unit is attached to its numerical value? Does the conversion preserve the quantity? Are the dimensions compatible with the proposed calculation? Is the resulting scale plausible, and which assumptions could change it?

These checks complement one another. The right unit cannot rescue a wrong reference choice. Matching dimensions cannot detect every missing numerical factor. A plausible size cannot prove that the model is appropriate. Taken together, however, they give us several independent opportunities to notice that an answer does not mean what it appears to mean.

In the next lesson we will make a prediction about position. We will state a starting point, distinguish elapsed time from a clock reading, and assume a constant rate of change. Only after the quantities and assumptions are clear will we turn the calculation into a program. That order keeps the physical meaning visible when the arithmetic moves onto a computer.
