Thermal energy balances

A temperature rise tells us that a state has changed. To connect that rise to joules, we need a model of the body’s response and an account of every energy transfer across its boundary.

Allow four hours: 60 minutes for explanation and examples, 90 minutes for written activities, 60 minutes for the Python investigation and 30 minutes for feedback. All heat capacities supplied here are model values for the stated temperature interval.

7.5 From a temperature rise to an energy change

Suppose we heat a solid sample gently, keeping it in one phase and allowing its temperature to become approximately uniform. A larger sample of the same substance needs more energy for the same rise. For a fixed sample, a larger rise generally needs more energy. Over a sufficiently modest range these observations motivate an approximately linear relation between internal-energy change and temperature change.

The specific heat capacity, c, describes the energy per unit mass per unit temperature change under specified conditions. Its SI units are Jkg−1K−1. For the condensed samples used here, expansion work is negligible and c is treated as constant, giving

ΔEint≈mc(Tf−Ti)=CΔT,C=mc.

The heat capacity C, in J/K, belongs to the whole sample. Doubling its mass doubles C while leaving the material’s c approximately unchanged. A value C=500J/K means a 1 K rise requires a 500 J increase in internal energy within this model. It does not specify the starting temperature or the route by which the energy enters.

Key idea 7.3 · A fixed-phase temperature model

Use ΔEint≈mcΔT for a fixed mass, fixed phase, approximately uniform temperature and an appropriate constant specific heat, with expansion effects negligible. When heat is the only energy input, Q≈mcΔT. A phase change or appreciable variation of c requires a different model.

Example 7.4 · A sample cools

A 0.40 kg solid sample has c=500Jkg−1K−1. It cools uniformly from 65 °C to 35 °C without changing phase. Find its internal-energy change and, if no work occurs, its heat transfer.

Working. Its heat capacity is C=(0.40)(500)=200J/K, and ΔT=−30K. Thus

ΔEint=(200)(−30)J=−6000J.

With no work and no other change in stored energy, Q=−6.0kJ. The sample loses internal energy and 6.0 kJ enters its surroundings. Taking the absolute temperature 308.15 K instead of the change −30 K would answer neither question.

7.6 Heat and work in one account

Choose a fixed amount of matter and compare an initial state with a final state. If its bulk kinetic and gravitational potential energies are unchanged, energy conservation says that its internal-energy change equals the total energy transferred into it. With heat Q positive inwards and work Won positive when done on the system,

ΔEint=Q+Won.

This is the first law of thermodynamics for the conditions just specified. A book that uses work done by the system instead writes ΔEint=Q−Wby, because Wby=−Won. The physical balance agrees once we translate the convention. Reading the subscript is more useful than memorising a sign without its definition.

If the chosen system also changes its bulk motion or height, those changes belong on the stored-energy side too. For a system whose relevant stores are kinetic, gravitational and internal energy, write ΔK+ΔUg+ΔEint=Q+Won, counting each interaction once. Whether gravity appears as Ug or as external work depends on whether the Earth–object interaction is included in the chosen system, just as in Unit 6.

Example 7.5 · A stirred sample with a loss

A paddle does 1800 J of work on a stationary liquid sample. During the same interval 300 J is transferred by heat to the room. The sample has heat capacity 600 J/K and begins at 21 °C. Find its final temperature, neglecting expansion and any remaining bulk fluid motion.

Method. Use the liquid as the system. Work enters, so Won=+1800J. Heat leaves, so Q=−300J. First find the energy change, then translate it into a temperature change:

ΔEint=−300+1800=1500J,
ΔT=1500J600J/K=2.5K,Tf=23.5∘C.

The liquid warms despite losing energy by heat. Adding the 300 J as another positive input would predict too large a rise; calling all 1800 J “heat” would misidentify the mechanism.

When a brake slows a moving body, we can often connect a mechanical-energy decrease to an internal-energy increase. The receiving system matters. Some energy may enter the wheel, brake, road or air. We must either include those recipients or state what fraction reaches the part whose temperature we calculate. Unit 6’s positive dissipated amount can supply that increase; it must not then be counted again as a second input.

The first change is 900+(−250)=650J, equivalently 900−250. The second is 120−200=−80J, so it cools by 1 K. Insulation makes Q small; work may still cross the boundary. An approximately constant internal energy additionally requires the net input and other relevant changes to balance.

7.7 Two bodies reaching a common temperature

Place a warm body H and a cooler body C in thermal contact inside an insulated enclosure. Let their constant heat capacities be CH and CC. Assume they remain in their original phases, perform negligible work, and eventually reach one common temperature Tf. The enclosure’s own energy change is neglected for this first model.

For the pair, the exchange between H and C is internal. There is no significant energy input from outside, so their internal-energy changes must add to zero:

CH(Tf−TH)+CC(Tf−TC)=0.

Expand the brackets and collect the terms containing the unknown final temperature. This gives (CH+CC)Tf=CHTH+CCTC, hence

Tf=CHTH+CCTCCH+CC.

This is a weighted average, with heat capacities as weights. The body with the larger capacity changes its temperature less for a given exchanged energy, so the final temperature lies closer to its starting value. With positive capacities and no other input, the result lies between the initial temperatures. A value outside that interval is a useful warning about the arithmetic or assumptions.

Example 7.6 · Unequal capacities

Let CH=300J/K, TH=80∘C, CC=900J/K and TC=20∘C. Find the final temperature and check the energy changes separately.

Tf=300(80)+900(20)300+900∘C=35∘C.

The warm body changes by −45 K and loses 300(45)=13500J. The cooler body changes by +15 K and gains 900(15)=13500J. Their changes sum to zero. The final value is closer to 20 °C because that body has three times the capacity.

Celsius values work in this weighted average because the weights sum to one. If both input temperatures are shifted by 273.15, the final result shifts by the same amount. You can therefore check the result in kelvin without changing the predicted energy exchange.

7.8 Checking a thermal calculation in Python

A short function lets us compare cases without rebuilding the algebra each time. Give the arguments one clear unit convention: capacities in J/K and both temperatures in °C. Positive capacities and finite input values are required; Celsius values below −273.15 are outside this model. A numerical guard checks those inputs, while the physical assumptions about phase, uniformity and insulation remain our responsibility.

from math import isfinite

def equilibrium(c_hot, t_hot, c_cold, t_cold):
    values = [c_hot, t_hot, c_cold, t_cold]
    if not all(isfinite(x) for x in values):
        raise ValueError("Inputs must be finite")
    if c_hot <= 0 or c_cold <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Capacities must be positive")
    if min(t_hot, t_cold) < -273.15:
        raise ValueError("Celsius input below absolute zero")
    final = (c_hot*t_hot + c_cold*t_cold) / (c_hot+c_cold)
    change_hot = c_hot * (final-t_hot)
    change_cold = c_cold * (final-t_cold)
    return final, change_hot, change_cold

print(equilibrium(300, 80, 900, 20))

The returned values are (35.0,−13500.0,13500.0): temperature, warm-body change, cooler-body change. The names identify the starting roles; the function also works if the temperatures are equal or the arguments are exchanged. Python’s all requires every finite-value check to be true. A ValueError stops an invalid calculation and explains which input condition failed.

The four results match the static table. Equal starting temperatures give that common temperature and zero changes. Exchanging the bodies leaves the final temperature unchanged while exchanging the two changes. Zero capacity raises the stated error. The hand benchmark checks a known result; exchange symmetry checks argument handling; the zero case checks a guard. A near-zero sum of energy changes checks internal consistency, but the same balance generated the prediction. It does not independently establish that a real apparatus is insulated or has the assumed capacities.

7.9 What a simple thermal model leaves out

In an experiment, the vessel and probe may also warm. If a vessel of capacity CV begins at the cooler temperature, its contribution is CV(Tf−TC). Add that term to the same balance rather than silently assigning its energy to the water. Heat lost to the room is another term with a sign. The usefulness of a simplified calculation depends on whether the omitted contributions are small enough for the question.

Example 7.7 · Include the vessel

Add a 300 J/K vessel initially at 20 °C to Example 7.6. Find the new final temperature.

Working. The initially cool part now has total capacity 900+300=1200J/K. The final temperature is [300(80)+1200(20)]/1500=32∘C. The warm body loses 14400 J; the cooler body gains 10800 J and the vessel gains 3600 J. The three contributions balance. Ignoring the vessel overestimated the final temperature by 3 K.

Heat capacities alone predict an endpoint, not how long equilibrium takes. A transfer rate needs information about contact, geometry and the surroundings. Nor can an endpoint outside the fixed-phase range be accepted merely because the code returns a number. If an input predicts melting or boiling, revisit the energy model.

Discussion of Activity 7.9

A cooler vessel taking energy and heat lost to a cooler room could both lower the endpoint. Measure or estimate vessel capacity and compare better-insulated trials. A probe offset could shift the reading: compare against a calibrated reference. Wrong units or a sign error could corrupt the calculation: reproduce the 35 °C hand benchmark and print the signed changes. Extra numerical digits improve neither insulation nor calibration. The evidence must distinguish these explanations.
