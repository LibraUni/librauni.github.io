P101 · Unit 3 · Lesson 1

Position, displacement
and average rates

The inspection carriage’s return journey presents a useful puzzle: how can its overall change be zero while its journey is not? To resolve it, we need to be precise about the quantity being added, subtracted or divided. This lesson develops that precision, then turns the reasoning into short Python functions you can check by hand.

Allow 4 hours: about 60 minutes for the unit introduction, explanation and examples; 90 minutes for written attempts; 60 minutes for Python; and 30 minutes for checking feedback and correcting your work. The activity times are first-attempt guides. Split the lesson across sittings if useful. Download the notebook or plain Python companion. The written discussions remain closed until you choose to open them.

3.1 A position needs a reference

Imagine a small carriage that carries a sensor along a straight inspection rail. Which part of the carriage supplies its position? A front wheel, the centre of the sensor or a painted mark would give different coordinates. Following Unit 2’s approach, specify the quantity first: here we track the centre of a painted mark fixed to the carriage. This lets us describe the motion with one point without pretending that the whole carriage has no size.

Choose a mark fixed to the rail as the origin, call its coordinate zero, and take rightwards along the rail as positive. Measure positions in metres. These three choices give a meaning to a signed coordinate x. A coordinate of −2 m locates our tracked point two metres left of the origin; it does not describe a negative physical length. The rail and reference marks are treated as fixed relative to the laboratory throughout this lesson.

The subscript “i” will identify an initial value and “f” a final value. These labels refer to the interval we choose, not necessarily the beginning and end of an entire experiment. If the initial coordinate is −2 m and the final coordinate is 4 m, the coordinate has increased by 6 m. We call this signed change the displacement. The capital Greek letter delta, Δ, means “change in”, so Δx is read “change in x”. It is not a number multiplying x.

Δx=xf−xi=4m−(−2m)=+6m

The order is essential: final minus initial. One way to check it is to ask what must be added to the initial position to obtain the final position. Adding +6 m to −2 m gives 4 m. If the carriage travels the other way, from 4 m to −2 m, the displacement is −6 m. The same endpoints have been visited in the reverse order, so the sign changes.

Key idea 3.1 · A signed change

In this one-dimensional description, displacement is final coordinate minus initial coordinate. Its sign gives the direction of the net change relative to the chosen positive direction. Both coordinates must use the same reference and unit.

Example 3.1 · Negative positions, positive change

A tracked point moves from −7 m to −3 m on our right-positive axis. Find its displacement and interpret the result.

Working. Subtract the complete initial value, keeping its sign:

Δx=−3m−(−7m)=+4m

The displacement is +4 m, towards the right. Both positions are left of the origin, but the final one is farther right. A negative position tells us which side of the origin the point occupies; it does not tell us its direction of travel. For a check, −7 m + 4 m = −3 m.

Discussion of Activity 3.1

The first displacement is −1 m − 3 m = −4 m, a net change leftwards. The second final coordinate is −6 m + 2 m = −4 m; its positive displacement is rightwards although it remains left of the origin. A final position has no earlier position to compare with. The same final coordinate can be reached from either direction.

3.2 The route and the change

Now give the carriage a journey with a turn. It starts at −2 m, travels directly to 4 m, then returns directly to 1 m. “Directly” means without reversing within either stage. The first stage covers 6 m of rail, the second 3 m. The distance travelled is the length of the route actually followed, counting each part each time it is traversed. It is therefore 9 m.

The displacement answers a different question: what is the final position relative to the initial one? Comparing 1 m with −2 m gives +3 m. The carriage finishes three metres to the right of where it started. The route includes a return over rail it had already crossed; that retracing adds distance but cancels part of the earlier signed change.

We can reach the displacement in two ways. Subtracting endpoints gives +3 m. Adding the stage displacements gives +6 m + (−3 m), also +3 m. To find distance, add the positive lengths: 6 m + 3 m = 9 m. The decision about signs expresses the physical question; it is not a choice made after seeing whether an answer looks convenient.

The magnitude of a signed quantity is its size without its directional sign. Vertical bars denote magnitude: |−3 m| = 3 m and |+3 m| = 3 m. For a single straight stage with no reversal, its length equals the magnitude of its displacement. If there are several such stages, add their lengths. Taking the magnitude only after adding signed changes would discard the extra travel caused by reversals.

Key idea 3.2 · Endpoints and route

Displacement depends on the initial and final positions. Distance travelled depends on the whole route. On a straight track, distance is at least the magnitude of displacement; they are equal for travel with no reversal, including any pauses. Here “distance” always means distance travelled unless a separation between positions is explicitly named.

Displacement is a vector quantity: it includes direction. Distance travelled is a scalar quantity: it has a size and unit, without a direction. We need only one signed component to describe displacement along our fixed line. More general vector calculations can wait; you do not need arrows in several dimensions to use the distinction here.

Example 3.2 · A complete return

The carriage starts at −2 m, reaches 4 m, and returns to −2 m, with no other reversals. Find distance and displacement.

Working. Each stage has length 6 m, so distance is 12 m. The displacement is −2 m − (−2 m) = 0 m. Equivalently, the stage changes +6 m and −6 m cancel. Zero displacement means the endpoints coincide. It says nothing by itself about whether the carriage moved in between.

Discussion of Activity 3.2

The stage changes are −1 − 2 = −3 m and 5 − (−1) = +6 m. Overall displacement is +3 m, matching 5 − 2; distance is 3 + 6 = 9 m. The added return contributes −3 m displacement and 3 m distance, giving zero net displacement and 12 m distance. The magnitude of the signed sum is 3 m: it describes endpoint separation. The sum of the magnitudes is 9 m: it counts travel in both directions before the added return.

3.3 An average belongs to an interval

A displacement of 6 m might occur over a few seconds or over an afternoon. To describe a rate, we must attach a time interval. Suppose the carriage is at −2 m when a clock reads 10 s and at 4 m when it reads 14 s. The elapsed time is 4 s, not 14 s. The clock had already been running when our chosen interval began.

Δt=tf−ti=14s−10s=4s

Dividing the change +6 m by the elapsed 4 s gives +1.5 metres per second. This is the average velocity over that interval. It tells us the signed displacement per unit elapsed time. The bar over v in the formula below denotes an average over the chosen interval:

v¯=ΔxΔt=xf−xitf−ti

Our notation uses seconds for clock readings and metres for positions, so the result has unit m/s, also written m s−1. The sign is inherited from the displacement because elapsed time is positive. We always take the final event later than the initial event. If both time readings are equal, the quotient is undefined; it is not an infinitely large or zero average that we can simply enter in a report.

The same average could describe smooth motion, a pause followed by faster movement, or a journey with reversals. It does not assert that the carriage moved 1.5 m in every second. An average is a summary of a specified interval. A statement about the rate at a particular instant would require a different investigation, developed later in the programme.

For average speed, use distance travelled as the numerator:

average speed=distance travelledelapsed time

The denominator must cover the same journey as the numerator. If the question concerns the whole trip, include stops between departure and arrival. You may define a separate average over moving periods only, but it answers a different question and must be labelled accordingly. Since distance and elapsed time are nonnegative and positive respectively, average speed cannot be negative.

Example 3.3 · One trip, two averages

Extend the carriage’s trip: at 10 s it is at −2 m, at 14 s it reaches 4 m, it stays there until 16 s, and at 19 s it reaches 1 m. Stipulate no reversals within the two travelling stages. Find the whole-trip average velocity and speed.

Working. The full elapsed time is 19 − 10 = 9 s. The displacement is 1 − (−2) = +3 m. The two path lengths are 6 m and 3 m; the pause adds time but no distance, giving 9 m travelled.

v¯=+3m9s=13 m/s≈+0.33m/s
average speed=9m9s=1m/s

The speed average is larger than the magnitude of the velocity average because part of the travel is back towards the start. The exact fraction one-third is the working value for the idealised velocity average; 0.33 m/s is a rounded display. These invented quantities carry no claim of real measurement precision.

If we instead exclude the two-second pause, the distance is still 9 m but the moving time is 7 s, giving about 1.29 m/s. That is a moving-time average speed, not the requested nine-second trip average.

3.4 Combining stages without losing time

Unit 2 used the arithmetic mean to summarise repeated readings. It is tempting to use the same operation on two stage speeds. But a stage rate already contains a division by time. Treating two rates as equally important can misrepresent a journey when one stage lasts longer.

Example 3.4 · Equal distances, unequal times

A carriage travels rightwards for 12 m in 3 s, then a further 12 m in 9 s. Neither stage contains a reversal. Find its average speed for the combined journey.

Working. Combine distance and time before dividing: the distance is 24 m and elapsed time 12 s, giving 2 m/s. The stage average speeds are 4 m/s and four-thirds of a metre per second. Their arithmetic mean would be eight-thirds of a metre per second, about 2.67 m/s, which is too large.

The slower stage occupied three times as long as the faster stage. Counting each stage once overlooks this. Equivalently, multiply each stage’s average speed by its duration to recover its distance, add the distances, and divide by total duration:

(4m/s)(3s)+(43 m/s)(9s)3s+9s=2m/s

No assumption of constant speed within a stage was needed: each stage average multiplied by its duration recovers that stage’s distance. Because both stages are rightwards with no reversal, the combined average velocity is also +2 m/s.

An arithmetic mean of two stage rates does work when the stages occupy equal durations. For example, 2 s at an average speed of 1 m/s and 2 s at 3 m/s cover 2 m and 6 m: 8 m in 4 s gives 2 m/s. The equal-time condition explains the agreement. It is safer to return to total distance and total time than to memorise an averaging rule without its conditions. For signed average velocities, combine signed displacements over total elapsed time in the same way.

3.4.1 Changing the description

Changing a fixed origin cannot change how far the carriage travelled or how long it took. In Example 3.3, adding 5 m to every coordinate changes the initial and final coordinates from −2 and 1 m to 3 and 6 m. Their difference is still +3 m. Similarly, adding 100 s to both clock readings leaves elapsed time unchanged. These are useful checks of a calculation: a shared shift cancels in a difference.

Reversing the positive direction has a different effect. With the same origin, all position signs reverse, so displacement and average velocity change sign. Distances and average speeds stay the same. This is a new description of the same physical trip. We are changing fixed coordinate conventions, not observing from a moving vehicle; a moving reference would raise additional questions.

3.4.2 Returning to the constant-rate model

Unit 1’s model predicted position from an initial value and a constant signed rate. If that model applies throughout an interval, dividing its predicted displacement by elapsed time recovers that same rate. The reasoning also runs partway in reverse: an interval average defines a constant-rate model that reproduces the two endpoints. But matching two endpoints does not show that the model matches every intermediate position. Our outward-and-return journey supplies a counterexample: its whole-trip average cannot describe both stages faithfully.

3.5 What the record leaves open

So far, the route descriptions have supplied information such as “directly” and “remains there”. A real table may not. Before calculating distance from successive positions, examine what you know about the gaps between them.

The table reproduces the positions and times used in Example 3.3, but deliberately omits its route conditions. If these four pairs were all we knew, B and C would establish zero displacement during that interval. They would not establish rest. The carriage could remain at 4 m, or travel to 5 m and back to 4 m before the next reading. Both possibilities fit the two entries. The latter contributes 2 m to distance travelled.

Under the straight-track description, the magnitudes of successive coordinate differences are 6, 0 and 3 m. Their sum, 9 m, is the minimum route length compatible with these exact sample positions. It becomes the actual distance only if there are no unrecorded reversals between samples and the equal-position interval involves no travel. Extra excursions can add distance while leaving the entries unchanged.

The whole-interval average velocity calculated from the entries is still +3 m / 9 s. It requires only the two endpoint positions and times. A distance-based average speed needs more information. If an otherwise identical trip includes the 2 m hidden excursion, the full distance is 11 m and average speed is eleven-ninths of a metre per second, about 1.22 m/s. The endpoint-based average velocity remains one-third of a metre per second.

Note 3.1 · Exact examples and measurements

The minimum-length statement concerns the exact positions stipulated in this invented record. Noisy measurements can zigzag even when an object moves smoothly. Adding every measured fluctuation as travel is not automatically a reliable estimate of the real route length. Keep Unit 2’s instrument and procedure limitations in the interpretation.

3.6 Calculate, check and explain in Python

Our first function should do exactly what its name promises: calculate signed average velocity from two positions and their corresponding clock readings. The inputs are finite real numbers, positions in metres and times in seconds, using one unchanged coordinate convention and clock. We assume the record has already been inspected; this small function is not a validator for arbitrary files or missing values.

def average_velocity_m_per_s(x_i_m, x_f_m, t_i_s, t_f_s):
    elapsed_s = t_f_s - t_i_s
    if elapsed_s <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Final time must be later than initial time.")
    displacement_m = x_f_m - x_i_m
    return displacement_m / elapsed_s

The names make the argument order explicit. The subtraction creates an elapsed time before any division occurs. The condition <= 0 rejects equal or reversed time readings with the ValueError pattern used in Unit 2. Returning a value leaves printing and report wording to the calling code. Notice that a negative position or displacement is not rejected: its sign is meaningful.

mean_velocity = average_velocity_m_per_s(-2.0, 1.0, 10.0, 19.0)
print("Average velocity / m/s:", mean_velocity)

The result is approximately 0.3333333333333333. It is a numerical approximation to the fraction we found by hand, expressed in m/s under the input convention. Keep the calculated value; choose a suitable shorter display only when preparing the report. The function’s arithmetic cannot establish instrument precision or prove that a physical model is correct.

Average speed deserves a separate function because it requires an independently specified path length. Passing the magnitude of the endpoint displacement would quietly substitute a different quantity when the route reverses.

def average_speed_m_per_s(distance_m, t_i_s, t_f_s):
    elapsed_s = t_f_s - t_i_s
    if elapsed_s <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Final time must be later than initial time.")
    if distance_m < 0:
        raise ValueError("Travelled distance cannot be negative.")
    return distance_m / elapsed_s

mean_speed = average_speed_m_per_s(9.0, 10.0, 19.0)
print("Average speed / m/s:", mean_speed)

The distance input must already be in metres and nonnegative. The elapsed-time condition is the same as before. A zero distance over a positive time gives zero average speed; a zero time is refused. The function cannot decide whether your distance includes a hidden reversal or whether you accidentally supplied centimetres. Those checks belong to the record and your interpretation.

Looking back, and preparing a graph

Use the remaining 20 minutes of written practice to explain the carriage’s journey without beginning with a formula. Name the reference, the endpoints, the route and the interval; then choose the quantities that answer “where did it end relative to the start?” and “how much travel occurred per second?” Draw a new journey of your own that has zero displacement but nonzero distance, label times, and check both averages. The discussions above provide the criteria for checking it.

Displacement preserves net change, distance preserves accumulated travel, and the two averages divide those different quantities by elapsed time. None of these summaries retains the whole time history. That is the reason for the next step: in Lesson 2 we will place the recorded positions and times on a graph, keeping the distinction between data and the motion we imagine between them.
