Describing a repeating motion

A vibrating object passes the same position many times. Position alone therefore cannot tell us whether one complete repetition has occurred. We begin by following a motion through a cycle, keeping track of direction as carefully as location.

Allow three hours: 60 minutes for reading and graph work, 80 minutes for the activities and 40 minutes for feedback and correction. Include the block and unit introductions in the reading time.

8.1 A motion about equilibrium

Imagine moving one tip of the fork slightly sideways and releasing it. The bent metal exerts a restoring force: a force directed back towards its equilibrium position. At equilibrium, the forces on the part being described balance. But balance at one instant does not mean that a moving part stops there. Its velocity carries it through, after which the restoring force acts in the opposite direction. This repeated movement about equilibrium is an oscillation.

A trolley attached to a spring gives a simpler model of the same kind of behaviour. We choose a horizontal coordinate and set its zero at the trolley’s equilibrium position. To the right, displacement x is positive; to the left, it is negative. A stretched spring pulls the trolley left, whereas a compressed spring pushes it right. The restoring force changes sign with the displacement.

Inertia and the restoring interaction have different roles. The force accelerates the trolley towards equilibrium; inertia allows an already moving trolley to pass through it. Near the farthest point on either side, the trolley slows, stops momentarily and reverses. In an ideal undamped model, the same sequence continues with unchanged size. Friction and other energy transfers usually make a real free oscillation shrink. We will return to that change in Lesson 3.

Key idea 8.1 · Equilibrium and motion

Equilibrium identifies a position at which the resultant force is zero. An oscillating body can pass through that position with nonzero velocity. A complete cycle returns to the same state of the repeating motion, including its direction of travel.

Start a cycle at the rightmost point, just as the trolley begins moving left. Follow it through equilibrium to the leftmost point, then through equilibrium again and back to the rightmost point. It has passed zero twice, in opposite directions. Those two crossings cannot be treated as identical starting events: one belongs to the outward part of this cycle and the other to the return.

Activity 8.1 · Following a cycle (15 minutes)

Draw a horizontal track and mark the two extreme positions and equilibrium. Starting at the rightmost position, draw five successive snapshots covering one cycle. For each, mark the direction of velocity when it is nonzero and the direction of the restoring force when it is nonzero. Explain why the trolley does not remain at equilibrium when it first reaches it.

Discussion of Activity 8.1

The positions are right extreme, equilibrium, left extreme, equilibrium, right extreme. Velocities at the three intermediate snapshots point left, vanish, and point right respectively; velocity also vanishes at both right-extreme snapshots. The restoring forces point left, vanish, point right, vanish, and point left. At the first equilibrium crossing the trolley already has leftward velocity. Zero force gives zero instantaneous acceleration there, not zero velocity.

8.2 How much and how often?

Two oscillations can take the same time per cycle while moving through different distances. Conversely, they can cover the same displacement range at different rates. We therefore need separate quantities for size and timing.

For a symmetric oscillation, the amplitude A is the largest distance from equilibrium. If the extreme displacements are +A and −A, the distance between them is 2A, called the peak-to-peak displacement. Amplitude is nonnegative and carries the unit of the oscillating quantity. A displacement amplitude might be in metres; a pressure signal would have an amplitude in pascals.

The period T is the time for one full repetition. The frequency f counts cycles per unit time. If one cycle takes T seconds, then an interval of NT seconds contains N cycles. Dividing the count by the interval gives

f=NNT=1T,T=1f.

Frequency is measured in hertz, Hz, equivalent to cycles per second. A frequency of 4 Hz means four complete cycles each second and a period of 0.25 s. Increasing frequency decreases period. The reciprocal relation is a useful check against the common mistake of treating them as two names for the same number.

Example 8.1 · Separate the centre, size and timescale

A position record varies symmetrically between 14 mm and 26 mm. Adjacent maxima occur at 0.30 s and 1.10 s. Find its equilibrium position, amplitude, peak-to-peak displacement, period and frequency.

The centre of the range is (14+26)/2=20mm. Displacement measured from that centre runs from −6 mm to +6 mm, so the amplitude is 6 mm and the peak-to-peak displacement is 12 mm. The time between adjacent maxima is

T=1.10−0.30=0.80s,f=10.80s=1.25Hz.

The 20 mm value locates equilibrium relative to the instrument’s origin. It is not the amplitude. If the instrument’s zero were moved, both extreme positions would shift together but their separation, period and frequency would be unchanged.

Activity 8.2 · Two independent changes (20 minutes)

Signal A has extremes −3 cm and +3 cm about zero and completes 12 cycles in 6 s. Signal B has extremes −6 cm and +6 cm and completes 6 cycles in 6 s. Find each amplitude, period and frequency. Describe separately what changed in size and timing. A third instrument reports A’s positions with 10 cm added to every value; what quantities change?

Discussion of Activity 8.2

A has amplitude 3 cm, frequency 2 Hz and period 0.5 s. B has amplitude 6 cm, frequency 1 Hz and period 1 s. B has twice the amplitude and twice the period, hence half the frequency. The shifted record of A has equilibrium at 10 cm and extremes 7 cm and 13 cm. Its amplitude, period and frequency remain unchanged.

8.3 Reading a motion graph

A displacement–time graph records one chosen point’s displacement at successive times. Read horizontally to follow its history. The vertical axis tells you how far and on which side of equilibrium the point lies. The slope tells you its velocity, as in Units 3 and 7. A rising curve means positive velocity; a falling curve means negative velocity.

In Figure 8.2, the trolley starts at +4 cm and initially moves towards negative displacement. At 0.5 s it crosses equilibrium while still moving left. At 1 s it reaches −4 cm and reverses. Only at 2 s has it returned to the starting extreme and is ready to repeat the same motion. The zero crossings at 0.5 s and 1.5 s are separated by half a period; their slopes have opposite signs.

The curve is flat at a turning point, where instantaneous velocity is zero. That does not mean acceleration is zero. To change from moving left to moving right, the trolley must accelerate rightwards around the left extreme. Near equilibrium the displacement changes most rapidly and speed is greatest for this smooth sinusoidal pattern. We will quantify these observations in Lesson 2.

The horizontal axis is time, not a second spatial direction. Although the curve looks wavy, the trolley itself travels along a straight track. The graph combines many instants into one picture. A wave travelling through space will require a separate spatial description in Unit 9.

Example 8.2 · Which events measure a period?

A smooth symmetric record crosses zero at 0.20 s while rising, 0.65 s while falling and 1.10 s while rising. What period do these events indicate?

The two rising crossings identify the same stage of successive cycles. Their separation is 1.10−0.20=0.90s, giving f≈1.11Hz. The 0.45 s separation between a rising and a falling crossing is half of that period. Choosing events by displacement alone would have doubled the inferred frequency.

Activity 8.3 · Read before calculating (25 minutes)

For Figure 8.2, make a table at 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 s showing displacement and the sign of velocity. Mark one interval of positive displacement with negative velocity, and one interval of negative displacement with positive velocity. Sketch a position record in which 6 cm has been added to every plotted displacement. Explain which graph features change and which remain.

Discussion of Activity 8.3

Displacements are +4, 0, −4, 0, +4 cm; velocity signs are zero, negative, zero, positive, zero. Between 0 and 0.5 s the trolley is right of equilibrium while travelling left. Between 1 and 1.5 s it is left of equilibrium while travelling right. Adding 6 cm moves the graph’s centre to 6 cm and extremes to 2 and 10 cm. It preserves slopes, amplitude and the 2 s period. Location relative to equilibrium and direction of travel answer different questions.

8.4 Timing several cycles

Timing one quick oscillation by hand is difficult. A modest start or stop delay can be a substantial fraction of the period. A useful method is to time several complete cycles between matching events, then divide the total interval by the number of cycles. Choose an event that is easy to recognise, such as passage through a mark in one specified direction.

Count the starting event as zero. The first return is one completed cycle, the next is two, and so on. Eleven successive matching events enclose ten cycles. This distinction between a count of events and a count of intervals is the same one that appears when working with recorded times in Python.

Example 8.3 · More cycles, a smaller timing contribution

Ten complete oscillations take 16.4 s. Suppose the start and stop timestamps each have a possible timing contribution bounded by ±0.10 s. Find the period estimate and a bound due to those endpoint contributions alone.

The central estimate is 16.4/10=1.64s. If one timestamp is early and the other late, their difference can be displaced by 0.20 s. Dividing by ten gives

T=1.64s,timing bound: ±0.02s.

The corresponding period interval is 1.62–1.66 s. Its frequency interval is obtained by taking reciprocals in reverse order: approximately 0.602–0.617 Hz. The central estimate is approximately 0.610 Hz. This calculation bounds the specified timing contributions; it does not establish a statistical confidence interval or remove clock calibration error.

Repeating the multi-cycle measurement helps reveal variation. Retain each raw duration and its cycle count, as in Unit 2. A systematic clock-rate error affects every interval, and a changing oscillation may have no single fixed period over a long observation. The method is useful because it reduces the relative influence of endpoint timing, provided the motion remains sufficiently repeatable.

Activity 8.4 · Plan and audit a timing (20 minutes)

An invented trial records 13 successive rightward crossings. The first is at 2.4 s and the last at 20.4 s. Find the number of cycles, period and frequency. Each endpoint has a stated possible timing contribution of ±0.15 s. Bound its contribution to the period. Explain one reason to repeat the trial and one limitation repetition would not remove.

Discussion of Activity 8.4

Thirteen events enclose twelve cycles. The interval is 18.0 s, so T=18/12=1.50s and f=2/3Hz≈0.667Hz. The endpoint contribution to the interval is at most 0.30 s, and to the period at most 0.30/12=0.025s. Repeated trials can expose variable timing or changing periods. They cannot by themselves remove a consistently miscalibrated clock or a repeated counting mistake. State the event and direction explicitly in a method another person could follow.

We now have a physical cycle, a graph and a way to estimate its timescale. The next lesson constructs a formula that keeps track of the stage within that cycle, so that a starting state becomes a prediction at later times.
