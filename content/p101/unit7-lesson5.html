Block synthesis and formative checkpoint

The same physical event can invite several accounts. A force diagram describes interactions at an instant; momentum and energy balances connect states; a derivative describes local change; observations test the resulting predictions. Choosing the useful account is part of the reasoning.

Allow four hours: 40 minutes for synthesis, reference and conclusion; 10 minutes for Activity 7.19; 90 minutes for the cumulative exercises; 40 minutes for their feedback and correction; 30 minutes for iCMA 02; and 30 minutes to review and retry it. These are the unit’s closing activities. TMA 02 has its own separate six-hour allowance.

7.19 Connecting the block

Start with the question. If you need a force or acceleration, draw the system and its external interactions. If you need the outcome of a brief encounter with an unknown force history, consider an impulse or momentum balance. If a displacement or temperature change is central, build an energy account with the right stores and transfers. These choices are connected: the stopping model in Lesson 4 could be checked through both derivatives and work–energy reasoning.

System and interval remain essential. Momentum conservation for a pair of colliding bodies requires negligible external impulse over the collision. A later thermal calculation may need a larger system containing the parts that receive the dissipated mechanical energy. Counting a loss of mechanical energy and then treating that same amount as an additional external heat input would count it twice.

The motion comparison adds a second discipline: separate a mathematical model, the code that evaluates it and the observations used to test it. A hand benchmark checks a calculation; a fresh run checks reproducibility; an independently recorded outcome tests a prediction under the experiment’s conditions. Each check answers a different question.

Activity 7.19 · Choose an account (10 minutes)

Choose a first method for each task and name one condition: find tension in a stationary suspended load; find the shared speed after sticking; predict a fixed-phase sample’s temperature rise; check velocity from a position function; compare a stopping prediction with observations.

Discussion of Activity 7.19

Use force equilibrium for the stationary load; momentum for the brief sticking collision with negligible external impulse; an energy balance and valid heat capacity for the sample; differentiate the position function within its domain; and compute signed residuals against documented observations using consistent units. Method selection should identify which information is needed and what might invalidate the account.

Exercise 7.1 · Temperature and interval

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 5 minutes

A sensor warms from −8 °C to 17 °C. Express both temperatures in kelvin and give the signed change. Explain the error in reporting the change as 298.15 K.

Hint for Exercise 7.1

Convert levels separately, then subtract.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.1

The levels are 265.15 K and 290.15 K; the rise is 25 K, equivalently 25 °C. The fixed offset cancels in a difference. Adding 273.15 to 25 treats an interval as a Celsius level.

Exercise 7.2 · One temperature, two amounts

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 5 minutes

Two stationary containers hold the same dilute monatomic gas at equal temperature. One contains three times as many particles. Compare mean particle translational kinetic energy and total translational internal energy. Explain why this argument is not a universal formula for every material.

Hint for Exercise 7.2

Distinguish the mean per particle from the sum.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.2

The mean translational kinetic energies are equal; the larger sample has three times the total in the ideal monatomic model. Other materials have additional microscopic motions and interaction energies. Equal temperature alone does not specify their total internal energies.

Return to Exercise 7.2

Exercise 7.3 · Name the transfer

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 5 minutes

A thermally insulated liquid is stirred, then allowed to settle. Its temperature rises. Identify the energy transfer across the liquid boundary, the stored change and the approximate value of heat transfer. What changes if the boundary instead includes the motor and its energy supply?

Hint for Exercise 7.3

Follow the shaft, then move the system boundary.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.3

Work enters through the shaft; internal energy increases. Insulation makes heat transfer approximately zero. For a larger boundary containing the motor and supply, the shaft transfer is internal and energy is redistributed among stores within the larger system; any exchanges crossing its new outer boundary must still be counted.

Return to Exercise 7.3

Exercise 7.4 · Signed first law

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 7 minutes

A stationary fixed-mass sample receives 500 J by heat and does 200 J of work on its surroundings. Its heat capacity is 150 J/K and it starts at 18 °C. Find its final temperature. Give the answer using both work sign conventions and state the temperature-model assumptions.

Hint for Exercise 7.4

Work done by the sample is negative work on it.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.4

With inward transfers positive, ΔEint=500−200=300J. Equivalently Q−Wby=500−200. Thus ΔT=300/150=2K and Tf=20∘C. Use a fixed phase, appropriate constant capacity, approximately uniform temperature and negligible other stored-energy changes/expansion effects.

Return to Exercise 7.4

Exercise 7.5 · Three-body equilibrium

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 8 minutes

A hot body has capacity 400 J/K and begins at 80 °C. A cooler body of capacity 600 J/K and a vessel of capacity 200 J/K both start at 20 °C. The isolated assembly reaches equilibrium without phase change or work. Find the final temperature and each energy change.

Hint for Exercise 7.5

Add the two initially cool capacities; check all three signed changes.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.5

The total initially cool capacity is 800 J/K. The final temperature is

Tf=400(80)+800(20)1200=40∘C.

The hot body changes by 400(40−80)=−16000J; the cool body gains 12000 J and the vessel gains 4000 J. Their sum is zero.

Return to Exercise 7.5

Exercise 7.6 · Infer a missing transfer

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 7 minutes

Repeat Exercise 7.5, but the measured common final temperature is 35 °C. Assuming the stated capacities, initial temperatures and negligible work are correct, infer net heat transfer for the whole assembly. Why does this calculation not prove that heat loss is the only possible explanation?

Hint for Exercise 7.6

Sum the three internal-energy changes at the measured endpoint.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.6

The hot change is 400(35−80)=−18000J; the initially cool parts gain 800(35−20)=12000J. The net change is −6000 J, so the assumed model implies Q=−6000J. A calibration error, incorrect capacity or nonuniform endpoint could invalidate an input assumption. Independent checks are needed to identify the cause.

Return to Exercise 7.6

Exercise 7.7 · Reject an unsuitable model

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 6 minutes

A program uses constant mcΔT to predict the temperature of a sample while it melts. Another program accepts a negative heat capacity for an ordinary solid and reports a final equilibrium temperature outside the two starting temperatures. Diagnose both problems and propose a repair.

Hint for Exercise 7.7

One failure concerns the physical model; the other includes an invalid input.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.7

The fixed-phase temperature-rise law omits energy associated with melting. Separate the phase-change interval and use an appropriate model before predicting beyond it. For these ordinary-body examples, capacities must be positive: reject the negative input. With positive capacities and no external transfer, the weighted-average endpoint lies between the starting levels. Merely clamping an impossible result would hide the problem.

Return to Exercise 7.7

Exercise 7.8 · A complete return

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 7 minutes

For numerical time in seconds and position in metres, x(t)=−1+4t−t2 on 0–4 s. Find velocity, acceleration, the turning time and position, total displacement, distance and average speed. Explain how acceleration can stay negative while speed increases.

Hint for Exercise 7.8

Find the zero of velocity, then inspect its sign on both sides.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.8

v=4−2t m/s and a=−2 m/s². The turn is at 2 s, with position 3 m. Both endpoints are −1 m, so displacement is zero. Distance is 4 m out plus 4 m back, giving 8 m and average speed 2 m/s. After the turn both velocity and acceleration are negative, so the magnitude of velocity increases.

Return to Exercise 7.8

Exercise 7.9 · A changing acceleration

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 6 minutes

In numerical SI units, x(t)=2+t+0.2t3. Find position, velocity and acceleration at 2 s. Explain why x(2)/2 is not instantaneous velocity and state the physical units of the cubic coefficient.

Hint for Exercise 7.9

Differentiate the function before evaluating.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.9

v(t)=1+0.6t2 and a(t)=1.2t. At 2 s, position is 5.6 m, velocity 3.4 m/s and acceleration 2.4 m/s². Position divided by elapsed time is neither the local slope nor, with this nonzero origin value, the interval displacement divided by time. The cubic coefficient has units m/s³.

Return to Exercise 7.9

Exercise 7.10 · A finite-step check

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 6 minutes

For the cubic in Exercise 7.9, the centred velocity estimate at 1 s is 1.6+0.2h2 in numerical SI units. Calculate it for steps 0.5 s and 0.1 s. If each position has rounding contribution bounded by ±0.002 m, bound the resulting velocity contribution for each step. What trade-off is visible?

Hint for Exercise 7.10

The two position errors can differ by 0.004 m; divide by 2h.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.10

The estimates are 1.65 m/s and 1.602 m/s; the exact derivative is 1.6 m/s. The rounding contributions are bounded by 0.004 m/s and 0.020 m/s respectively. A smaller step reduces this finite-step model error but amplifies the effect of limited input resolution. These bounds concern the stated rounding alone.

Exercise 7.11 · Stopping and residuals

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 7 minutes

Use Models A and B from Lesson 4 at initial speed 9 m/s, braking magnitude 3 m/s² and delay 0.50 s. Find stopping distances and total times. If an observed distance is 17 m, calculate both residuals. Which is closer on this one observation?

Hint for Exercise 7.11

Separate delay and braking; use observed minus predicted.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.11

Braking time is 3 s and distance is 81/6=13.5 m. Model B adds 0.5 s and 4.5 m, giving 3.5 s and 18 m. Residuals are 17−13.5=+3.5 m and 17−18=−1 m. B is closer on this observation; that does not decide a whole dataset or establish the cause of the discrepancy.

Exercise 7.12 · A fair summary

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 6 minutes

For residuals (−3,+1,+2) m, calculate signed mean and mean absolute residual. A second model has residuals (−1,−1,−1) m. Compare the two summaries and explain why neither identifies the physical cause. State why repeated initial speeds should remain in a stopping dataset.

Hint for Exercise 7.12

Absolute values measure discrepancy without sign cancellation.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.12

The first signed mean is zero and MAR is 2 m. The second signed mean is −1 m and MAR is 1 m, so its typical absolute discrepancy is smaller despite its nonzero signed mean. Neither summary supplies calibration or physical mechanism. Repeated speeds expose variation at the same recorded input; discarding them hides evidence.

Exercise 7.13 · Audit the code and the claim

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 8 minutes

A student calculates prediction = speed_mph**2 / 6 and labels it metres, then computes prediction - observed_m while calling this the lesson’s residual. They also claim the smaller residual mean proves a universal braking law. Identify and repair all three errors. Use 20 mph as an arithmetic check.

Hint for Exercise 7.13

Translate units, then restore the sign convention, then restrict the conclusion.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.13

First set v = speed_mph * 0.44704, then use prediction = v**2 / 6 for b=3m/s2. At 20 mph this is about 13.323 m, not 66.667 m. Use observed_m - prediction. A smaller chosen summary supports a specified numerical description on the examined record; it does not establish a universal law, identify a cause or eliminate missing metadata. Check the complete residual plot and parameter sensitivity.

Exercise 7.14 · From collision to warming

P101 · Unit 7 · Core · 7 minutes

A 0.40 kg cart at +2.0 m/s sticks to a stationary 0.60 kg cart. External horizontal impulse is negligible during impact. Calculate their common speed and the decrease in translational kinetic energy. Suppose all of that decrease becomes internal energy in a part of the assembly with heat capacity 12 J/K. Find its temperature rise and explain the additional assumption needed for that final step.

Hint for Exercise 7.14

Use momentum first, kinetic energies second and the thermal capacity last.

Worked solution to Exercise 7.14

Total momentum is +0.80 kg m/s, so the combined 1.00 kg moves at +0.80 m/s. Initial kinetic energy is 0.80 J and final kinetic energy is 12(1.00)(0.80)2=0.32 J. The decrease is 0.48 J. If the specified part receives all of it, ΔT=0.48/12=0.040K. This requires the stated energy partition, negligible heat loss and a valid fixed-phase capacity; momentum alone does not determine where the lost translational energy goes.

7.21 Formative iCMA 02

Use this ten-question checkpoint to revisit Block 2. Allow 30 minutes to choose answers and write brief reasons, then 30 minutes to examine feedback and retry difficult points. It gives local practice feedback; it does not submit an assessment or save a mark. Without JavaScript, choose on paper and open each explanation when ready.

Question 1

A stationary load hangs from one vertical cord. Which identifies the force pair on the load?

Explanation for Question 1

Answer A. Tension and weight act on the load and balance. The load’s force on the cord acts on a different body; it is the partner of the cord’s tension force.

Question 2

A satellite moves in a uniform circular orbit. Which statement is consistent?

Explanation for Question 2

Answer B. Direction changes, so velocity changes. The acceleration and resultant force point inwards; constant speed does not require zero resultant force.

Question 3

At twice the distance from the centre of a spherical gravitating body, outside it, gravitational force on the same small test mass is:

Explanation for Question 3

Answer C. The inverse-square dependence gives a factor (1/2)2=1/4, with the source mass and test mass fixed.

Question 4

Two bodies stick in a brief collision. Which condition justifies conserved horizontal momentum of the pair?

Explanation for Question 4

Answer B. Internal impulses cancel in the pair; negligible external horizontal impulse is the needed condition. Sticking generally reduces translational kinetic energy.

Question 5

A constant force is perpendicular to a particle’s displacement. Its work is:

Explanation for Question 5

Answer A. Work is the dot product F·s, which is zero for perpendicular vectors. This does not say every other force does zero work.

Question 6

An insulated stationary sample receives paddle work. What can increase?

Explanation for Question 6

Answer C. Work can increase internal energy even when heat transfer is negligible. Heat labels a transfer, not a stored quantity.

Question 7

Two isolated bodies of positive heat capacity reach equilibrium with no work or phase change. Their final temperature is:

Explanation for Question 7

Answer B. The capacity-weighted average lies between the starting values. It is the arithmetic midpoint only for equal capacities (or equal starting temperatures).

Question 8

At an instant a body has negative velocity and negative acceleration. Away from a zero of velocity, its speed is:

Explanation for Question 8

Answer A. The velocity becomes more negative, increasing its magnitude. The signs must be interpreted together.

Question 9

A recorded distance is smaller than a prediction. Observed-minus-predicted residual is:

Explanation for Question 9

Answer C. Subtracting the larger prediction from the smaller observation gives a negative residual. Reversing the subtraction would use a different convention.

Question 10

A fresh notebook run matches its hand benchmark. What does this establish most directly?

Explanation for Question 10

Answer B. This supports the calculation and its reproducibility. Testing physical assumptions and measurement limitations needs additional evidence.

UseTopicResult and conditions
RecallTemperatureT/K=θ/∘C+273.15. A change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K. Do not add the offset to a temperature interval.
UnderstandState and transferInternal energy belongs to the system’s microscopic state. Heat is transfer caused by a temperature difference; work is another transfer mechanism. Neither is stored “heat”. Choose the system boundary.
RecallFirst lawΔEint=Q+Won=Q−Wby when other stored-energy changes are negligible. Inward heat and work on the system are positive.
UnderstandHeat capacityΔEint=CΔT=mcΔT, with C in J/K and c in J/(kg K). Use an appropriate approximately constant capacity, fixed phase and sufficiently uniform temperature; include other energy changes when relevant.
RecallIsolated equilibriumTf=C1T1+C2T2C1+C2. Positive capacities; common final temperature; no external heat, work or phase change. Include a vessel as another term if needed. The endpoint lies between the starting levels.
UnderstandMicroscopic modelFor a dilute ideal monatomic gas, temperature tracks mean translational kinetic energy per particle. Total internal energy also depends on particle number. This is not a universal microscopic formula for all materials.
RecallRates of motionv=dxdt, a=dvdt. For x=x0+v0t+12at2, constant a gives v=v0+at. A zero of velocity is a turn only if velocity changes sign.
UnderstandSpeed and signsSpeed is |v|. Away from v=0, equal signs of v and a mean increasing speed; opposite signs mean decreasing speed. Displacement and distance are different.
ConsultCentred estimatev(t)≈x(t+h)−x(t−h)2h, with h>0 and both sample times inside the domain. Reducing h can reduce approximation error while amplifying rounded-input effects.

Consult · Python comparison pattern

Here speed_mph and distance_ft are NumPy arrays, b is positive and tau is nonnegative. Check inputs and retain the full residual plot.

v = speed_mph * 0.44704
observed = distance_ft * 0.3048
predicted = v**2 / (2*b) + v*tau

What the unit has connected

We began with a moving object that came to rest and asked where its mechanical energy could go. Internal energy gave us a more detailed description of the receiving system. Temperature supplied a measurable thermal state, while heat and work described transfers across a chosen boundary. A heat capacity then connected a specified internal-energy change to a temperature change within a clear model.

Returning to motion, we used derivatives to connect position, velocity and acceleration. The stopping investigation showed why an exact formula and a correct program are only part of a physical account. Observations can distinguish predictions, expose variation and reveal missing information. A useful conclusion retains that information alongside the numerical result.
