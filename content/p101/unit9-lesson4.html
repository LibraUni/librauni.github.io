Waves at boundaries and obstacles

A wave’s behaviour depends on what it meets. A fixed end can send a crest back as a trough; a junction can divide a disturbance between two regions; an opening can let waves spread into a sheltered area. We will connect these changes to boundaries and physical scale.

Allow three hours: 65 minutes reading, 70 minutes written activities and 45 minutes feedback and correction. No additional Python work is required.

9.13 Reflection at an end

Call a wave approaching a boundary the incident wave. A disturbance returning from the boundary is the reflected wave. Direction reversal does not by itself determine whether the displacement also changes sign. For that, we must specify what the end is allowed to do.

At an ideal fixed end of a string, the total transverse displacement is zero at every instant. During overlap, incident and reflected displacements must therefore add to zero at the end. An upward incident contribution requires an equally large downward reflected contribution. The reflected pulse is inverted: an incident crest returns as a trough. For a sinusoid, the displacement inversion corresponds to a phase change of π.

For an ideal free end, imagine attaching the string to a negligibly light ring that can slide vertically without friction on a pole. The end may move transversely, and the pole supplies no transverse constraint force in this idealisation. The reflected pulse has the same displacement sign as the incident pulse. During overlap their end displacements reinforce. Thus a free end and a fixed end impose different conditions and produce different reflections.

The result refers specifically to transverse displacement of the string. For other wave variables, such as sound pressure and particle displacement, the sign behaviour at a boundary must be established for the variable being measured. We should not transfer “a rigid boundary inverts the wave” from a string picture to every possible wave quantity.

Example 9.8 · Use the endpoint condition

At a fixed endpoint, the incident sinusoidal displacement contribution at one instant is +1.5 mm. Find the reflected contribution and the actual endpoint displacement. Explain why an observer measuring the endpoint does not see it rise by 1.5 mm first.

The reflected contribution is −1.5 mm, so the actual displacement is zero. The two components describe contributions to the same state at that instant. Their sum satisfies the endpoint constraint continuously; they are not two successive movements of the endpoint. This is the same pointwise addition used in Lesson 2.

Repeated reflection at two fixed ends supplies oppositely travelling waves. Their superposition can form the standing patterns of Lesson 3, provided the wavelength fits both endpoints. Losses and imperfect supports affect real amplitudes and response widths, but the ideal boundary conditions explain the basic mode shapes.

Activity 9.13 · Direction and inversion (20 minutes)

Draw an upward pulse approaching an end and the reflected pulse after it has separated from the end, first for a fixed end and then for an ideal free end. Mark direction arrows. If a pulse travels 1.8 m to an end at 3.0 m/s, when does its reflection return to the starting point? Explain whether inversion changes this travel time.

Discussion of Activity 9.13

Both reflected pulses travel back towards the start. The fixed-end pulse is downward, while the free-end pulse remains upward. The round-trip distance is 3.6 m, so the return time is 1.2 s. Displacement inversion does not change the assumed propagation speed or travel distance. Negligible loss and an unchanged uniform string are implicit in this simple timing model.

9.14 Transmission into a different medium

At a junction between two strings, an incoming disturbance can partly return and partly continue into the second string. The continuing part is the transmitted wave. Its speed may differ because the second medium has different mechanical properties. We will use supplied speeds rather than derive them from tension and mass density.

For a stationary junction driven by a steady sinusoid in a linear system, the boundary moves at the driving frequency. The transmitted and reflected sinusoidal responses therefore retain that frequency. The spatial spacing can change. Since λ=vw/f, a smaller speed at unchanged frequency gives a smaller wavelength.

Example 9.9 · What stays continuous?

A 6 Hz wave travels at 12 m/s in the first region and 8 m/s in the second. Find the incident and transmitted wavelengths. What is the reflected wavelength back in the first region?

λ1=12ms−16Hz=2.0m,λ2=8ms−16Hz=43m.

The reflected frequency is also 6 Hz. It travels in the first medium at 12 m/s, so its wavelength is 2.0 m. The transmitted wave has a shorter spacing because successive cycles advance less far during the same one-sixth of a second.

The wavelengths do not tell us how energy is divided between reflection and transmission. That depends on the junction and both media. Nor can we obtain transmitted amplitude by subtracting the reflected amplitude from the incident amplitude without a correctly signed boundary model. In this unit the useful predictions are the frequency continuity and the corresponding spatial scales.

Activity 9.14 · Repair a boundary explanation (20 minutes)

A student writes, “The wave slows down by a factor of two, so its frequency and wavelength both halve.” Correct the statement for a stationary boundary and a steady source. Then calculate the wavelength beyond the boundary if the incident wavelength is 0.90 m and the transmitted speed is two-thirds of the incident speed.

Discussion of Activity 9.14

The source and stationary boundary maintain the frequency. If speed halves, wavelength halves while frequency remains fixed. If both frequency and wavelength halved, their product would fall to one-quarter of the original speed. For the stated two-thirds speed ratio, wavelength becomes (2/3)(0.90)=0.60m.

When a wave crosses a boundary obliquely, a change of speed can also change propagation direction. Unit 10 will develop that geometry for light. Here we turn to another reason that a wave can enter a region beyond a simple straight path: spreading around an edge or through an opening.

9.15 Diffraction and physical scale

If water passing through an opening behaved only like independent particles travelling straight ahead, we would expect a sharply bounded corridor beyond the gap. The curved ripple bands in Figure 9.7 reveal a broader spatial response. Diffraction is the spreading of a wave past an edge or opening into regions that a simple straight-line construction would leave in shadow.

To describe this view, a wavefront joins points at the same phase, for example a line of crests. It is a line in a two-dimensional surface picture and a surface in three dimensions. In a uniform medium whose properties are the same in every direction (an isotropic medium), local propagation is perpendicular to the wavefront. A straight family of fronts can therefore indicate a common direction, while curved fronts indicate a spreading pattern.

A useful qualitative construction treats points across an opening as contributions to the transmitted wave. Those contributions spread and superpose beyond the opening. Their relative phases depend on direction. The resulting reinforcement is concentrated more narrowly when the opening is very wide compared with the wavelength; when its width is comparable with the wavelength, spreading is substantial. This is the scale comparison we need, without a quantitative diffraction formula.

Key idea 9.4 · Compare a size with a wavelength

For an opening of width a, use the dimensionless ratio a/λ. When a≫λ, the main transmitted region is relatively narrow; when a is comparable with or smaller than λ, spreading is broad. The comparison describes angular spread, not the total energy transmitted.

There is no universal absolute size called a “small gap”. A doorway is large compared with a visible-light wavelength but can be comparable with an audible-sound wavelength. In a common medium with approximately constant speed, reducing frequency increases wavelength, so the same opening becomes smaller in wavelength units. This helps explain why low-frequency sound can spread broadly around obstacles.

Example 9.10 · One opening, two wavelengths

A 0.80 m opening is exposed to two sound frequencies, 170 Hz and 3400 Hz. Use a supplied speed of 340 m/s to compare diffraction scales.

At 170 Hz, λ=340/170=2.0m, giving a/λ=0.40. At 3400 Hz, λ=0.10m, giving a/λ=8.0. The lower-frequency wave should spread much more broadly in this simple comparison. These ratios do not predict an exact loudness at a listener: source strength, reflections and absorption also matter.

Narrowing an opening can increase angular spread while reducing transmitted power. “More diffraction” must therefore not be read as “more energy gets through”. Likewise, broad spreading does not itself imply a new frequency. With a steady source, a stationary opening and the same medium on both sides, the frequency and wavelength retain their values while the distribution of propagation directions changes.

Activity 9.15 · Design a scale comparison (30 minutes)

In an ideal ripple tank of uniform depth, use a fixed wavelength of 2 cm. Compare openings of 2 cm and 12 cm. Predict which gives broader angular spreading, then describe a fair observation that would test that prediction. State two quantities to hold fixed, one to record, and why the brightest or highest ripple alone would be an inadequate measure. Explain what changing the water depth would add to the comparison.

Discussion of Activity 9.15

The ratios are 1 and 6, so the 2 cm opening should give broader spreading. Keep the driving frequency and water depth fixed, as well as the source arrangement where possible. Record the directions over which a clear transmitted pattern appears at a fixed distance, using the same detection criterion. A single peak height measures amplitude at one place, not angular spread, and the transmitted power can differ. Changing depth can change wave speed and therefore wavelength; it would confound the opening-width comparison unless measured and accounted for.

We can now describe the role of both phase and physical scale. Superposition determines how overlapping contributions combine; boundary conditions select reflected or standing behaviour; the wavelength-to-opening comparison tells us when spreading is prominent. The final lesson asks you to choose which of these descriptions a particular claim needs and to support it with evidence.
