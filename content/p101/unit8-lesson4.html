Sampling a signal

A formula supplies a displacement at any allowed time. A recorded signal supplies a finite set of values. We now examine the gap between those descriptions, using a known model so that misleading conclusions can be exposed by an independent benchmark.

Allow four hours: 45 minutes reading, 40 minutes written exploration, two hours Python and 35 minutes feedback and correction. Use the functions, arrays and labelled plots established in Units 1–3.

8.12 A continuous model and a list of samples

Suppose an instrument records position every Δt seconds. Its sampling frequency is fs=1/Δt, in samples per second. The subscript distinguishes it from the signal frequency f, in cycles per second. A 2 Hz oscillation sampled at 20 samples/s has ten samples per cycle; sampled at 3 samples/s it has only 1.5 samples per cycle on average.

For uniform sampling beginning at time zero, sample number n occurs at

tn=nΔt=nfs,n=0,1,2,….

A supplied sinusoid then gives xn=Acos(2πftn+ϕ). This is a generated sample, not a measurement. It lets us control the exact signal and isolate what the sampling does. Real observations also involve timing errors, noise, sensor response and finite measurement duration.

Keep the sample times with their values. A list of displacements alone loses the timing needed to calculate a period. When drawing the record, use visible markers for samples. A line joining adjacent markers can guide the eye but does not reveal the path between them. A separate finely evaluated model curve is useful here because the model is known.

Example 8.11 · Count samples and intervals

A record runs from 0 to 2 s inclusive at 8 samples/s. How many timestamps are present, and how many samples per cycle does it supply for a 2 Hz motion?

The spacing is 1/8=0.125s. There are 2×8=16 intervals and therefore 17 timestamps, including both endpoints. The samples-per-cycle ratio is fs/f=8/2=4. The extra endpoint closes the record; it does not change the spacing or create an extra sample per cycle.

Duration and spacing solve different problems. Finer spacing can reveal changes between neighbouring values. A longer duration captures more cycles and makes a repeated event easier to distinguish from an isolated feature. Extending a badly spaced record does not necessarily remove ambiguity: the same missed stages may recur forever.

8.13 Estimate a period transparently

Lesson 1 measured a period between matching events. For a sampled displacement record, use successive upward crossings of equilibrium. Subtract any known equilibrium offset first, then look for a pair in which displacement is nonpositive at the first time and positive at the next. Restricting the direction avoids mistaking half a cycle for a full one.

A crossing usually falls between timestamps. We can estimate its time by joining the two values with a straight segment. Let the pair be (ti,xi) and (ti+1,xi+1), with xi≤0<xi+1. The fraction of the vertical rise needed to reach zero is −xi/(xi+1−xi). Apply the same fraction to the time interval:

tc=ti+−xixi+1−xi(ti+1−ti).

This is linear interpolation: an estimate based on the straight segment between the two samples. A sine curve is not exactly straight over a finite interval. The estimate becomes useful when the local curve is adequately resolved, and its accuracy should be checked against the known model before using it to interpret another record.

Example 8.12 · Locate a crossing between readings

Displacements −0.8 mm and +1.2 mm occur at 0.40 s and 0.50 s. Estimate the upward zero-crossing time.

The rise from −0.8 to zero is 0.8 mm out of a total rise of 2.0 mm: a fraction 0.4. The estimated time is 0.40+0.4(0.10)=0.44s. It lies between the two timestamps, closer to the smaller-magnitude displacement. This interval check would catch a sign error that placed it outside the pair.

With crossing estimates c0,c1,…,cq−1, inspect the individual intervals cj+1−cj. If they represent successive cycles, an average period estimate is

T^=cq−1−c0q−1,q≥2.

The hat marks an estimate. There are q−1 intervals between q crossings. Dividing the whole time span by that count is equivalent to averaging the individual intervals. Keep those intervals visible: a single average can conceal an unusual gap, changing period or missed crossing.

The intervals are 0.79 s and 0.83 s. The average is (2.06−0.44)/2=0.81s, giving approximately 1.23 Hz. One crossing supplies no separation. In a sinusoid, a downward crossing occurs halfway between upward crossings, so mixing directions introduces half-cycle intervals. 8.14 When distinct signals give identical samples

Consider two candidate displacement models, both with amplitude 1 cm and zero initial phase:

xa(t)=(1cm)cos[2π(2Hz)t],
xb(t)=(1cm)cos[2π(1Hz)t].

One completes two cycles each second and the other only one. Now sample both at fs=3s−1. At time tn=n/3s, their phases differ in a revealing way:

cos(4πn3)=cos(2πn−2πn3)=cos(2πn3).

The first equality rewrites the phase; the second uses whole-turn periodicity and cosine’s symmetry, cos(−θ)=cosθ. The samples are exactly equal for every integer n. No amount of extra observation at those same instants can distinguish these two particular candidates.

This ambiguity is aliasing: different continuous signals become indistinguishable after sampling. The sparse record repeats every second and our crossing estimator will report approximately 1 s, although the generated 2 Hz signal has a true period of 0.5 s. A stable, reproducible estimate can therefore be wrong about the underlying motion.

Another revealing case occurs at exactly two samples per cycle. Sample the 2 Hz model at 4 samples/s. With phase zero, values alternate between +1 and −1 cm. With phase π/2, every sample falls at a zero crossing and all values are zero. Two samples per cycle can miss the oscillation completely for a particular starting phase.

Key result 8.4 · Sampling requires a frequency bound

For an ideal signal known to contain no frequencies at or above a chosen bound fmax, uniform sampling faster than 2fmax avoids this class of aliasing under ideal reconstruction assumptions. Equality is not a safe design rule. Our simple graph and crossing method usually needs substantially more samples per cycle, sufficient duration and a benchmark check.

The bound is prior information about what the instrument can receive; it cannot generally be inferred from a smooth-looking sampled plot. Real instruments often limit high-frequency input before sampling. In this unit, the known single-frequency simulation supplies the bound. We are not deriving a general reconstruction theorem or using a spectral library.

8.15 Build a checked sampling notebook

The notebook separates three operations: generate the known model, estimate a period from samples alone, and compare the estimate with the known period. Keeping those roles separate prevents the estimator from quietly using the answer it is meant to recover.

Start with an integer number of intervals. The code below makes times from 0 to 4 s inclusive at 20 samples/s. Dividing integer indices by the rate avoids accumulating a repeatedly added floating-point step. NumPy’s cosine uses radians.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

f = 2.0                 # cycles per second
fs = 20.0               # samples per second
A = 0.01                # metres
phi = 0.0               # radians
n_intervals = 80
t = np.arange(n_intervals + 1) / fs
x = A * np.cos(2*np.pi*f*t + phi)

plt.plot(t, x, "o-", label="sampled model")
plt.xlabel("Time / s")
plt.ylabel("Displacement / m")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

The next function implements the crossing calculation. Its inputs are only timestamps, displacements and the known equilibrium level. It rejects mismatched, nonfinite or non-increasing inputs. A small absolute tolerance of 10−12m treats near-zero values caused by floating-point cosine evaluations as zero; this is a numerical convention for the model, not a measurement uncertainty.

import numpy as np

def crossing_period(times, values, level=0.0, atol=1e-12):
    """Seconds and metres in; return period (s), upward crossings (s).

    atol is an absolute zero tolerance in metres for this simulation.
    It is not a measurement uncertainty.
    """
    t = np.asarray(times, dtype=float)
    y = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    if t.ndim != 1 or y.ndim != 1 or len(t) != len(y) or len(t) < 2:
        raise ValueError("Use equally long one-dimensional records")
    if not np.all(np.isfinite(t)) or not np.all(np.isfinite(y)):
        raise ValueError("All times and values must be finite")
    if not np.isfinite(level) or not np.isfinite(atol) or atol < 0:
        raise ValueError("Use a finite level and nonnegative tolerance")
    if not np.all(np.diff(t) > 0):
        raise ValueError("Times must increase strictly")
    z = y - level
    z[np.abs(z) <= atol] = 0.0
    crossings = []
    for i in range(len(t) - 1):
        if z[i] <= 0 < z[i+1]:
            fraction = -z[i] / (z[i+1] - z[i])
            crossing = t[i] + fraction * (t[i+1] - t[i])
            crossings.append(float(crossing))
    if len(crossings) < 2:
        raise ValueError("Fewer than two resolved upward crossings")
    period = (crossings[-1] - crossings[0]) / (len(crossings) - 1)
    return period, crossings

Subtracting level gives displacement from equilibrium. The test z[i] <= 0 < z[i+1] selects upward crossings, including one exactly at the left sample. A crossing at the final timestamp has no later sample to establish its direction and is not counted. If fewer than two crossings are found, the function raises an error instead of inventing a period. The returned crossing times let you inspect the individual intervals.

Discussion of Activity 8.14

At 20 samples/s, the first five displacements in metres are approximately 0.010000, 0.008090, 0.003090, −0.003090, −0.008090. The symmetric crossing brackets give an estimated period very close to 0.5 s. The phase-shifted 4 samples/s record and zero-amplitude record have no resolved oscillation after the stated absolute numerical zero convention, so an estimate is refused. Repeated timestamps violate the input contract. A clean rerun and hand benchmark test the code; the alias construction tests the information retained by the sampling. None of these synthetic trials establishes a real instrument’s calibration.
