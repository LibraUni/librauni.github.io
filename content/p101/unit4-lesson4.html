P101 · Block 2 · Unit 4 · Lesson 4

Constant-acceleration
motion

Suppose a force calculation gives an acceleration of 2 m/s² to the right. That tells us how velocity changes, but it does not yet tell us where the object will be three seconds later. The object might already be moving left, moving right or starting from rest. To predict its motion, we must combine the acceleration with a starting state and an interval over which the model applies.

We will develop the required relations from a straight velocity–time graph. The geometry matters: it explains the factor of one-half in the position formula, the difference between displacement and distance, and why an average acceleration cannot always replace a changing one. No differentiation or integration is required. Keep the force model from Lesson 3 alongside the motion calculation; a correct formula can still be used beyond the interval for which its assumptions hold.

Allow three hours: 60 minutes for reading and examples, 90 minutes for the six activities, and 30 minutes for feedback and correction. Use g=9.8ms−2 unless another value is given. Worked values keep enough digits to make checks visible; they do not imply that the stipulated models describe measured motion to that precision.

4.20 Give the prediction a starting state

Choose a fixed axis, an origin and a positive direction. Set the clock to t=0 at the start of the interval being studied. Let the initial position be x0 and the initial signed velocity be u. Later position and velocity are x and v. The signed displacement is s=x−x0. Here s is a displacement symbol, while upright s denotes the second. Neither the initial position nor the initial velocity has to be zero merely because the clock starts at zero.

For constant acceleration a, every equal time interval produces the same velocity change. The average acceleration over any nonzero interval is therefore also a. Starting from a=(v−u)/t and rearranging gives

v=u+at.

The velocity–time graph is a straight line with intercept u and gradient a. The graph is not a picture of the track: a sloping velocity graph can describe motion on a level bench. Its gradient has units (ms−1)/s=ms−2. Its height has velocity units. Reading a graph’s height as acceleration would confuse two different pieces of information.

Example 4.19 · A clock that starts partway through a journey

A trolley passes a marker at laboratory clock time 8.0 s with velocity 1.5 m/s right. For the next 3.0 s its acceleration is 0.80 m/s² right. Taking right as positive, find its velocity at laboratory time 11.0 s.

The elapsed time in our motion model is t=11.0−8.0=3.0s, not 11.0 s. Thus

v=1.5ms−1+(0.80ms−2)(3.0s)=3.9ms−1.

The trolley gains 0.80 m/s each second, a total gain of 2.4 m/s. Its initial velocity remains part of the answer. Using 11 s would silently extend the acceleration model to a time before the stated interval.

Constancy is a physical assumption, not a consequence of having two readings. Suppose velocity is 2 m/s at the start and 6 m/s after 4 s. The average acceleration is 1 m/s². Velocity might rise steadily throughout, or remain at 2 m/s for two seconds and then rise more rapidly. Both histories have the same endpoint change. Only the first has constant acceleration over the whole interval. They need not cover the same displacement.

Activity 4.20 · What do two readings establish?

Allow 10 minutes. A velocity changes from −3.0 m/s to +1.0 m/s in 2.0 s. Find the average acceleration. If acceleration is constant, find the velocity after 0.50 s and the time of the turn. What can you still claim if only the two endpoint velocities are known?

Hint for Activity 4.20

A turn occurs at zero signed velocity. Keep the negative initial velocity in the equation.

Discussion of Activity 4.20

The average acceleration is [1−(−3)]/2=2.0ms−2. Under the constant-acceleration assumption, v=−3+2(0.50)=−2.0ms−1, so motion is still in the negative direction. Setting 0=−3+2t gives a turn at 1.5 s. Without that assumption, the average acceleration is still known, but neither this intermediate velocity nor this turning time is determined by the endpoints alone.

4.21 Read displacement from a velocity–time graph

At constant velocity, displacement is velocity multiplied by elapsed time. On a velocity–time graph this product is the area of a rectangle. A positive velocity gives positive displacement; a negative velocity gives negative displacement. That sign convention carries over when velocity changes: area above the time axis counts positively, and area below it negatively.

For a straight velocity graph there is a direct geometric argument. Pair an instant near the beginning with the equally distant instant near the end. Because velocity changes uniformly, the two velocities always add to u+v. Their mean is therefore (u+v)/2, regardless of which symmetric pair we choose. The time-average velocity for the full interval is this same value. Multiplying by the duration gives

s=u+v2t.

When both velocities are positive, the region under the line is a trapezium with parallel sides u and v and width t. Its area gives the same result. If the line crosses zero, split the region into two triangles, counting one negatively. The symmetric-pair reasoning still holds. What does not generally hold is taking the mean of the endpoints of a curved velocity graph.

Substituting v=u+at into the mean-velocity expression yields

s=u+(u+at)2t=ut+12at2.

The first term is the displacement the object would have made if it had kept its initial velocity. The second is the additional signed contribution from the changing velocity. In the positive-acceleration sketch, it is the triangular area with base t and height at. This explains the one-half: the extra velocity does not act at its final value for the entire interval.

To recover position, add the initial position: x=x0+ut+12at2. Each added term must be a length. In particular, acceleration multiplied by time squared has units ms−2s2=m. An expression with at in place of at2 would add a velocity to a length and cannot be correct.

Example 4.20 · Displacement, then position

A trolley is at x0=−2.0m when the clock starts. Its velocity is u=1.0ms−1 and its acceleration is a=0.50ms−2. Find its position after 4.0 s.

First calculate the displacement:

s=(1.0ms−1)(4.0s)+12(0.50ms−2)(4.0s)2=8.0m.

Therefore x=−2.0+8.0=6.0m. As a separate check, the final velocity is 3.0 m/s, so the mean velocity is 2.0 m/s and its product with 4.0 s is again 8.0 m. The object travels 8.0 m but ends at coordinate 6.0 m; an origin is not the start of every journey.

Activity 4.21 · Compare the areas

Allow 15 minutes. Use the two histories in Figure 4.19. In A, velocity rises steadily from 2 to 6 m/s over 4 s. In B, it stays at 2 m/s for the first 2 s, then rises steadily to 6 m/s during the next 2 s. Find each displacement using rectangles and triangles or trapezia. Explain why using the whole-interval average acceleration in s=ut+12at2 works for A but gives the wrong result for B.

Hint for Activity 4.21

In B, the triangular extra area exists only over the last two seconds.

Discussion of Activity 4.21

For A, sA=[(2+6)/2](4)=16m. For B, the first rectangle contributes 2(2)=4m and the second trapezium contributes [(2+6)/2](2)=8m, totalling 12 m. Equivalently, B has an 8 m baseline rectangle and a 4 m extra triangle.

Both histories have average acceleration 1 m/s² over 4 s. Substituting it as though it were constant predicts 16 m for both. B spends more time at low velocity; the endpoint change cannot capture that timing. The position formula needs a constant acceleration throughout its chosen interval.

4.22 Choose a relation by what is known

Sometimes time is the missing information rather than the quantity we want. We can remove it algebraically without first dividing by acceleration. From v−u=at and 2s=(u+v)t, multiply the second equation by a and substitute the first:

2as=(u+v)at=(u+v)(v−u)=v2−u2.

Thus

v2=u2+2as.

The factorisation is the difference-of-squares identity. This derivation never divides by a, so it remains an identity when a=0. In that case, however, it reduces to v2=u2 and contains no information about displacement or elapsed time. Return to v=u and s=ut to describe constant-velocity motion. A relation can be true and still be insufficient for a particular question.

Key idea 4.6 · One model, connected relations

For one fixed axis and constant acceleration over elapsed time t, with initial velocity u and signed displacement s=x−x0:

v=u+at,s=u+v2t,
s=ut+12at2,v2=u2+2as.

State the initial conditions and interval. These are related consequences of the same model; they are not four independent physical laws. Squared velocities do not retain direction.

Choose a relation that includes the required unknown and the quantities you know. If time is given and final velocity is wanted, the first relation is usually direct. If displacement is known and time is irrelevant, the squared-velocity relation can avoid an unnecessary quadratic equation. After solving, restore the physical meaning: which sign of velocity is possible, and is the computed time inside the modelled interval?

Example 4.21 · Will the stop fit?

A carriage moves right at 6.0 m/s. A controller produces constant acceleration 1.5 m/s² left until the carriage stops. There are 10 m of clear track ahead. Is that enough in this idealised model?

Choose right positive, so u=6.0ms−1, a=−1.5ms−2 and the stopping condition is v=0. Time is not needed initially:

s=v2−u22a=0−(6.0ms−1)22(−1.5ms−2)=12m.

The carriage needs 12 m, so 10 m is insufficient. At 10 m, v2=36−30=6m2s−2, giving v=+6ms−1≈2.45ms−1. The positive sign follows because this point is reached before the stop. The negative mathematical candidate would describe a later return if the same leftward acceleration continued through a reversal, contrary to the stated controller operation.

The stopping time is (0−6)/(−1.5)=4.0s. The mean velocity is 3.0 m/s over that interval, giving the independent check s=12m. This calculation is a specified model, not a safety margin for real equipment.

Activity 4.22 · Signs survive a change of axis

Allow 20 minutes. A bead moves left at 4.0 m/s and has constant acceleration 2.0 m/s² right. Find its displacement and elapsed time when it first stops. Then find its velocity after a displacement of 3.0 m left, specifying the first arrival there. Repeat the stopping calculation with left chosen positive. Finally, explain why setting a=0 in s=(v2−u2)/(2a) is not allowed even though uniform motion is a valid physical case.

Hint for Activity 4.22

With right positive, both the initial velocity and the stated leftward displacement are negative. At the first arrival the bead is still travelling left.

Discussion of Activity 4.22

Right positive gives u=−4, a=+2 in SI units. At rest, s=[0−(−4)2]/(2×2)=−4.0m and t=[0−(−4)]/2=2.0s. At s=−3.0m, v2=16+2(2)(−3)=4m2s−2. The first arrival has v=−2.0ms−1; v=+2.0ms−1 would be a later return if the acceleration continued.

Left positive gives u=+4, a=−2, so stopping displacement is +4.0m and time remains 2.0 s. The physical stop is unchanged. The rearranged expression divides by a and therefore excludes zero acceleration. Use s=ut for that case; do not interpret division by zero as a failure of the physical model.

4.23 Free fall includes the upward part

For an object moving vertically after release, with gravity the only significant force and approximately uniform, the force calculation gives a constant acceleration. Choose upward positive. Then ay=−g, where g itself is a positive magnitude. Replace x by vertical position y and use the initial vertical velocity uy:

vy=uy−gt,y=y0+uyt−12gt2.

The acceleration remains downwards while the object rises, at its highest point, and while it descends. At the highest point its instantaneous velocity is zero, but it does not remain there for an interval. During a short interval straddling the top, velocity changes from positive to negative. Zero velocity at one instant is therefore fully consistent with nonzero acceleration.

Example 4.22 · A vertical launch

A small ball is released vertically upwards at 9.8 m/s from y0=1.0m. Neglect air effects. Find the time and height of its highest point, then its state 1.5 s after release.

At the top, vy=0, so 0=9.8−9.8t gives t=1.0s. Substitution into the position model gives

ymax=1.0m+(9.8ms−1)(1.0s)−12(9.8ms−2)(1.0s)2=5.9m.

At 1.5 s, vy=9.8−9.8(1.5)=−4.9ms−1 and y=1.0+9.8(1.5)−4.9(1.5)2=4.675m, about 4.7 m. The ball is above its release point but moving downwards. Position’s sign and velocity’s sign answer different questions. At both the top and this later instant, ay=−9.8ms−2.

Free fall is also an experimental technique. In NASA Glenn’s drop facility, experiment hardware and its surrounding vehicle fall together inside a chamber evacuated to reduce air resistance. A sample can then experience very small support forces even while gravity produces a substantial downward acceleration. This joins the zero contact reading in Lesson 3 to the motion model here.

Activity 4.23 · Check the scale of a drop facility

Allow 15 minutes. Model a vehicle released from rest in uniform gravity, with negligible drag. Calculate its fall distance and downward speed after 5.18 s. Compare the distance with the 132 m reported in Figure 4.22. Explain why this agreement does not validate a calculation that continues ay=−g through the facility’s stopping stage.

Hint for Activity 4.23

Choose downward positive for this calculation, or retain upward positive and interpret a negative displacement.

Discussion of Activity 4.23

Downward positive gives s=12(9.8)(5.18)2=131.479m, about 131 m, and v=9.8(5.18)=50.764ms−1, about 50.8 m/s down. This is consistent with the reported scale using rounded inputs and an idealised model; it is not a precision reconstruction of the facility.

During stopping, the vehicle encounters a substantial support force from the decelerator. The gravity-only force inventory no longer applies. Extending the free-fall equation through that stage predicts continued downward speeding rather than stopping. Agreement during one interval does not establish the model for another.

4.24 A return journey has distance but may have no displacement

The position equation describes an endpoint relative to an origin. It does not record how much path was travelled between the endpoints. A ball that rises and returns to its release point has zero displacement, even though it has travelled up and down. To find total distance when direction changes, divide the motion at each turning point and add the positive lengths of the pieces.

For Example 4.22, return to the release height means y−y0=0, so 0=9.8t−4.9t2=t(9.8−4.9t). There are two solutions: t=0 is release, and t=2.0s is the later return. Both are valid times for that height; the wording identifies the one requested. The ball rises 4.9 m and falls 4.9 m, travelling 9.8 m while its displacement is zero.

More generally, solving for an event can produce two times, one time or no real time. Those possibilities have physical interpretations: a height may be passed on both ascent and descent, touched only at the top, or never reached. Do not discard a second solution merely because a question seems to invite one answer. First test its sign, the direction of motion and the interval over which the model is valid.

Example 4.23 · Signed areas across a turn

A bead has initial velocity u=−2.0ms−1 and constant acceleration a=1.0ms−2 along a sufficiently long straight track. Find its displacement and distance travelled over 6.0 s.

Its final velocity is v=−2+1(6)=4.0ms−1, and the position relation gives

s=(−2.0ms−1)(6.0s)+12(1.0ms−2)(6.0s)2=6.0m.

The bead turns when 0=−2+t, at 2.0 s. Before that instant, the velocity graph has a negative triangular area −12(2)(2)=−2.0m. After it, the positive triangular area is 12(4)(4)=8.0m. The signed sum is −2+8=6.0m, agreeing with the formula. The distance is instead 2+8=10m.

The acceleration really does continue through the turn in this stipulated model. That differs from a sliding-friction-only stop, where the force regime changes at zero speed.

Activity 4.24 · Two visits to one height

Allow 15 minutes. For the ball in Example 4.22, test whether it passes y=4.675m at both 0.50 s and 1.50 s. Find its velocity at each visit. Find its total distance travelled by 1.50 s, and compare that with its displacement from release. Explain why one signed position formula remains valid across the top even though the distance calculation is split there.

Hint for Activity 4.24

The top is at 5.9 m after 1.0 s. Add the ascent from 1.0 m and the descent from the top.

Discussion of Activity 4.24

Substituting 0.50 s gives y=1+4.9−1.225=4.675m and vy=+4.9ms−1. At 1.50 s the same height is reached with vy=−4.9ms−1. The two visits have the same position and opposite velocities.

Distance by 1.50 s is (5.9−1.0)+(5.9−4.675)=6.125m, whereas displacement is 4.675−1.0=3.675m. The acceleration remains −g throughout the rise and fall, so the position model does not need to change at the top. Splitting is needed to add path lengths without cancellation, not because gravity changes direction there.

4.25 End one interval before starting the next

Many useful predictions consist of several simple intervals. A cart may be pulled, then coast, then slow through friction. Each interval has its own force inventory and initial conditions. The final position and velocity from one become the initial position and velocity of the next. Resetting the elapsed-time variable for a new interval does not reset the physical position or erase the velocity.

Example 4.24 · From a force to a stopping point

A 2.0 kg block slides right on a level bench at 3.0 m/s. A horizontal cord pulls right with 6.0 N for 2.0 s and then goes slack. Sliding friction is modelled as a constant 2.0 N while the block moves right; after it stops, no horizontal force is required. Find the total displacement until rest.

During the pull, a1=(6−2)/2=2.0ms−2. Over the first 2.0 s,

v1=3+2(2)=7.0ms−1,s1=3(2)+12(2)(2)2=10m.

During the second interval, friction is the only horizontal force, so a2=−2/2=−1.0ms−2. The new initial velocity is 7.0 m/s. At the stop,

Δt2=0−7−1=7.0s,s2=02−722(−1)=24.5m.

The total displacement is s1+s2=34.5m, and total elapsed time is 2+7=9.0s. Motion stays rightward until rest, so distance equals displacement here. On a velocity–time graph, the first trapezium has area 10 m and the second triangle 24.5 m.

At the stop, replace sliding friction with the admissible static state: zero horizontal resultant and continued rest. Extending a2=−1.0ms−2 indefinitely would make the block reverse without an applied force capable of sustaining that motion. It would be an extrapolation of an expired model.

A useful final check runs backwards through the argument. Does the predicted motion agree with the sign of the acceleration? Does that acceleration agree with the force sum? Do the forces satisfy contact and motion conditions? Initial-time and zero-acceleration checks catch algebraic errors, while these physical checks catch a different class of mistakes. Agreement between two rearrangements of one formula is useful, but does not independently validate the force model.

Activity 4.25 · Repair a prediction beyond a stop

Allow 15 minutes. A 3.0 kg block slides right at 4.0 m/s on a level bench. There is no cord pull; the kinetic friction magnitude is 3.0 N. A student calculates s=4(6)−12(1)(6)2=6m after 6.0 s. Find where the reasoning first fails, then give the correct position relative to the start and velocity at 6.0 s. State the friction at that time if no other horizontal force is present.

Hint for Activity 4.25

Calculate the stopping time before deciding whether six seconds lies inside the sliding interval.

Discussion of Activity 4.25

While sliding right, a=−3/3=−1.0ms−2. It stops at t=(0−4)/(−1)=4.0s, after a displacement s=4(4)−12(1)(4)2=8.0m. The student’s formula is valid only up to this stopping event. Afterward, rest is possible with zero friction and zero acceleration.

At 6.0 s the block remains 8.0 m right of its start with velocity zero. The algebra in the student’s substitution is correct; the mistake is applying the rightward-sliding force model for two seconds after rightward sliding has ended.

Looking ahead

We can now connect a force inventory to acceleration, and acceleration to a trajectory along one axis. The initial state, signs and ending event are part of that prediction. A turning point does not necessarily end a constant-acceleration interval; a change of force regime does.

The final lesson uses these same relations in two perpendicular directions. Its new question is how two component descriptions share one time and one landing event.
