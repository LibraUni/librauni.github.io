P101 · Unit 2 · Lesson 5

A small investigation,
fully explained

A measurement investigation is an argument built from a question, a procedure and a record. The calculation matters, but it is only one link. If the question changes halfway through, or the record no longer identifies the procedure, an exact mean cannot repair the argument. This closing lesson asks you to hold all the links together.

You will analyse a supplied comparison of two ways of viewing a ruler, then write an account that another reader could check. The records are simulated: completing the analysis demonstrates reasoning with supplied evidence, not that you have handled an instrument or carried out the physical procedure. The unit exercises then let you choose methods across the whole unit, with less prompting than the lesson activities.

Allow 5 hours, including 1 hour of Python: 45 minutes for explanation and examples, 55 minutes for the investigation’s written activities, 95 minutes for the cumulative exercises, 60 minutes for the notebook and 45 minutes for feedback and corrections. Download the learner notebook, plain Python companion and Unit 2 reference PDF. The same reference is available on this page. No new software package is needed.

2.25 Turn a question into a comparison

“Which method is best?” sounds like a research question, but it leaves “best” undefined. Best for speed, repeatability, agreement with a reference or ease of use? A bounded question names both the comparison and the evidence that will answer it. Here the question is: How do the centre and spread of repeated recorded lengths differ between a view from above and an oblique view, for this specified card edge and procedure?

The measurand is the length of the straight A–B edge of one flat card. Method T views the endpoint and ruler marks from directly above. Method O views from a fixed oblique direction. The ruler, card, endpoint definition, observer, support and nearest-millimetre recording rule are stipulated to remain the same. Each attempt begins with lifting and replacing the ruler. In this teaching record, the viewing direction is the intended change; no particular angle is claimed.

Viewing direction can matter when the marks and the point being aligned are separated in depth. Their apparent alignment may change with viewpoint; this is the parallax issue introduced earlier. A comparison should nevertheless begin with the observed results, not with a predetermined answer. A difference between two small sets would be consistent with a viewpoint-related effect, but would not identify every possible cause or measure accuracy without an appropriate reference.

Chronological order also belongs to the method. If every T reading were taken first and every O reading later, a change with time could be confused with the method change. The supplied sequence interleaves them: T1, O1, O2, T2, T3, O3, O4, T4, T5, O5, O6, T6, T7, O7. Interleaving makes the two methods less separated in time. It does not magically remove drift or all other differences. Keep the sequence so that such questions remain inspectable.

Example 2.23 · A question that the record can answer

“Which view gives the true length?” cannot be answered from these records alone: no independently established reference length is supplied. “Which view has the smaller range in this record?” can be answered directly. “Does that smaller range establish better accuracy?” is a separate question, and the answer is no. A consistent method can share a systematic discrepancy across every attempt.

Keep the investigation’s conclusion aligned with its question: compare recorded centres and spreads under the stated conditions, then identify what further evidence would support an accuracy claim.

Example 2.24 · Two counts, one correction

Each method has seven attempts and six included readings. There are twelve included readings in total, but they should first be summarised in their two method groups because the question is a comparison. The processed lists, in each method’s recorded order, are T: 84, 85, 84, 83, 84, 84 mm; O: 86, 87, 85, 86, 88, 84 mm. O3 contributes one corrected value, not both 58 and 85.

Pooling all twelve would give a mean of 85 mm. That arithmetic is legitimate for the pooled list, but it hides the two method centres. It does not tell us that either method had a mean of 85 mm, and it changes the question being answered.

For T, the six values sum to 504 mm, so:

For O, the sum is 516 mm and the mean is 86 mm. The extremes are 84 and 88 mm, giving range 4 mm. Distances from 86 mm are 0, 1, 1, 0, 2 and 2 mm: total 6 mm, hence D = 1 mm. 2.27 Reproduce the analysis

The notebook includes the three summary functions from Lesson 3 and the record-preparation function from Lesson 4. Read their input contracts before reusing them. The calculation should be repeatable from the supplied source and decisions, without relying on variables left in memory from another notebook. This is why a clean restart is part of the activity rather than a final cosmetic step.

Each source dictionary uses the familiar keys trial, entered_mm and note, plus method containing "T" or "O". The trial key is now the text ID, such as "O3"; the preparation function uses membership and lookup, so it works with these unique string IDs as well as integer IDs. The supplied decisions are explicit. There is no rule that removes a value merely because it lies far from a mean.

# Run after the notebook's source records, decisions and prior functions.
processed = prepare_readings(source_records, decisions)
values_T_mm = []
values_O_mm = []
method_by_id = {}
for record in source_records:
    method_by_id[record["trial"]] = record["method"]

for record in processed:
    method = method_by_id[record["trial"]]
    if method == "T":
        values_T_mm.append(record["value_mm"])
    elif method == "O":
        values_O_mm.append(record["value_mm"])
    else:
        raise ValueError("Unknown method")

print("T values / mm:", values_T_mm)
print("O values / mm:", values_O_mm)

The first loop creates a dictionary connecting each trial ID to its method. The second loop groups the processed values using that connection. Nothing is sorted, so each group keeps the relative order in which its included observations occurred. The original ledger is still needed to recover the full interleaving and missing attempts. An unknown method is refused instead of silently being put into the other group.

def describe(label, values_mm):
    if len(values_mm) == 0:
        print(label, "has no eligible readings")
        return
    print(label, "included count:", len(values_mm))
    print(label, "mean / mm:", format(mean_value(values_mm), ".1f"))
    print(label, "range / mm:", range_value(values_mm))
    print(label, "D / mm:",
          format(mean_absolute_deviation(values_mm), ".1f"))

describe("T", values_T_mm)
describe("O", values_O_mm)

Here return without a value simply leaves the reporting function when the group is empty. The display should show T: count 6, mean 84.0, range 2, D 0.3; O: count 6, mean 86.0, range 4, D 1.0, with millimetre labels. The retained working D for T is approximately one-third, not exactly 0.3. Display formatting is not fed back into any calculation.

Use independent checks that could expose a wrong implementation. For the small list 2, 4, 6 mm, hand calculation gives mean 4 mm, range 4 mm and D four-thirds of a millimetre. A retained zero and a missing marker should behave differently, as in Lesson 4. Changing units should scale every length summary: converting the T readings to metres gives mean 0.084 m, range 0.002 m and D approximately 0.000333 m. These checks test different parts of the reasoning rather than merely repeating the main answer.

2.28 Explain the result and its limits

A useful report is selective without being evasive. The main paragraph should let a reader understand the question, the comparison and its outcome. The attached ledger and notebook carry the detail needed to inspect the calculation. Do not force a reader to infer the quantity or units from a variable name, and do not bury a material correction under the phrase “data cleaning”.

Example 2.26 · A conclusion with the right reach

A defensible conclusion is: “In this simulated comparison, the six included T readings have mean 84 mm, range 2 mm and mean absolute deviation about their mean approximately 0.3 mm. The six O readings have mean 86 mm, range 4 mm and D 1 mm. T is more tightly grouped and has a mean 2 mm lower in this record.”

The extra sentence “Therefore T is accurate and O is wrong by 2 mm ” goes beyond the evidence. The 2 mm is a difference between means, not an independently established error. A suitable reference and a fuller account of conditions would be needed to investigate accuracy. Further repeated comparisons could examine whether the observed difference persists, but repetition alone would not supply that reference.

When proposing the next check, name the uncertainty it addresses. A suitable known reference length would address agreement with a reference, subject to that reference’s own limitations. More fresh placements under both specified views would investigate stability of the observed comparison. Better documentation of viewpoint and alignment would help reproduce the method. These are different purposes, so “take more data” is not yet a complete proposal.

Exercise 2.1 · Define what is being measured

Allow 8 minutes.

A flexible strip has a curved long edge. Someone reports its “length” as 184 mm. Give two distinct operational definitions that could produce different legitimate lengths. Choose one and describe a repeat procedure, two controls and the metadata needed to interpret five readings.

Hint for Exercise 2.1

The straight distance between endpoints and the distance along an edge are different quantities.

Worked solution to Exercise 2.1

One quantity is the straight-line separation of two marked endpoints with the strip resting in a specified state. Another is the distance along its curved edge. Choose, for example, straight separation with the strip unstrained and fixed on a flat support. Mark endpoints, align a ruler, view from above and record in millimetres to a stated increment. Lift and replace the ruler between attempts while keeping the strip’s state and endpoints fixed. Record source ID, units, divisions/recording rule, procedure, trial order and notes. Five repeated screen views of one placement do not test the same procedure. The isolated 184 mm does not establish which quantity was measured or its accuracy.

Exercise 2.2 · Coordinates and a shared shift

Allow 8 minutes.

A card’s endpoints read 18 mm and 103 mm on a ruler. Calculate its length. Both coordinate indications then acquire the same+3 mm shift: calculate the new difference. Compare this with a direct length display that reads 3 mm high. Why must “the offset cancels” be qualified?

Hint for Exercise 2.2

An endpoint coordinate and a direct length indication enter different calculations.

Worked solution to Exercise 2.2

The original difference is 103−18=85 mm. With the same additive shift on both coordinates, the difference is 106−21=85 mm. The shared additive term cancels in this subtraction. A direct indication of the 85 mm length that reads 3 mm high would instead show 88 mm; there is no second shifted coordinate being subtracted. The cancellation requires the same additive effect on both coordinate readings. Different endpoint errors or an incorrect scale factor need not cancel.

Exercise 2.3 · Centre and spread from a small list

Allow 10 minutes.

For 72, 74, 73, 72, 74 mm, calculate count, sum, mean, observed interval, range and mean absolute deviation about the mean. Show the signed deviations and explain why their sum is not the spread.

Hint for Exercise 2.3

Count both appearances of 72 and 74. Distances are nonnegative.

Worked solution to Exercise 2.3

The count is 5 and sum 365 mm, so the mean is 73 mm. The observed interval is 72–74 mm and range 2 mm. Signed deviations are−1,+1, 0, −1,+1 mm; they sum to zero because positive and negative departures balance. Absolute distances are 1, 1, 0, 1, 1 mm, totalling 4 mm. Therefore:

D=4mm5=0.8mm

The zero signed sum does not mean the readings agree; D describes their average distance from the mean.

Worked solution to Exercise 2.4

Exercise 2.9 · Diagnose a program by hand

Allow 10 minutes.

A program processes values 0, 2, 4 mm by adding only values greater than zero to a total, but divides by the original list length. It reports 2 mm. Is this a sound missing-data rule? Test the same rule on−2, 0, 2 mm. Describe a repair and two boundary checks.

Hint for Exercise 2.9

The first answer may be correct for the wrong reason. Zero contributes nothing to a sum but still counts.

Worked solution to Exercise 2.9

For 0, 2, 4, omitting zero from the sum accidentally leaves the correct sum 6 and count 3, so the reported mean 2 is correct despite the flawed rule. For−2, 0, 2, the program totals only 2 and divides by 3, reporting two-thirds instead of the correct zero. Negative values can be valid for signed quantities; positivity is not a missing-data test. Use an explicit missing marker and reviewed inclusion decisions, then compute the count and sum from the same included list. Check a valid zero, signed values and an empty/all-excluded set. The empty set should yield no numerical mean, not a fabricated zero. A program needs appropriate stated input conditions as well as passing one example.

IdeaMethod and conditions
Arithmetic meanAdd all included values and divide by their count. The mean need not equal an observed value. Its extra arithmetic digits do not establish a new instrument capability.
RangeSubtract the minimum from the maximum. The observed interval gives both endpoints; the range is their difference. It does not describe bounds on future readings.

Conditions and limitsAn empty list has no mean, range or D. A singleton has range and D zero but cannot reveal repeatability. These descriptive spreads are not a guaranteed error bound, confidence interval or complete measurement uncertainty.
Shifts and unit changesAdding the same constant to all values shifts the mean equally but leaves range and D unchanged. Converting mm to m divides values, mean, range and D by 1000. Two endpoint coordinates cancel the same additive offset in their difference; a direct length indication does not.

From readings to evidence

Unit 2 began with a simple difficulty: a reading is not yet a result that another person can interpret. Defining the quantity and procedure gave the reading a meaning. Repetition exposed variation; comparisons and references helped separate different questions about performance. Summaries made records easier to compare, while provenance kept the evidence available for inspection. A careful report now connects these steps without claiming more than they establish.
