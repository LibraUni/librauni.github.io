Choosing a balance and unit review

A collision, a pull along a track and a descending load can all involve forces, momentum and energy. The question is not which law is “the right one” in isolation. It is which account uses the information we have, with assumptions we can defend, to find what we need.

Allow three hours: 35 minutes for the method review, reference and conclusion; 10 minutes for Activity 6.21; 90 minutes for the twelve cumulative exercises; and 45 minutes for worked solutions and correction. The exercises are core practice. There is no additional assessed submission in this lesson.

6.21 Selecting a useful balance

Start with a sketch, a system boundary and two clearly labelled states or an instant. A question about acceleration or a contact force usually needs Newton’s laws. A question about a short encounter with unknown contact history may be simplified by momentum. A question about speed after a displacement may be simplified by work and energy. These are starting choices, not exclusive categories.

Some questions need more than one account in sequence. During a brief sticking collision, use momentum if external impulse is negligible. During a later smooth climb, use mechanical energy if other forces do no work. Do not impose kinetic-energy conservation across the sticking collision just because it applies to the later climb. Changing the interval can change the valid approximation.

Example 6.16 · Collision first, climb second

A 0.20 kg cart at +3.0 m/s sticks to a stationary 0.40 kg cart. The joined carts then climb a smooth track. Neglect external horizontal impulse during impact and dissipative work during the climb. The shared post-collision speed is 0.20(3.0)/0.60=1.0 m/s. That is the climb's initial speed, so

h=(1.0)22(9.8)≈0.0510 m.

Using the original cart's 0.90 J as the joined carts' climbing energy would predict too large a height. After sticking, only 0.30 J remains as translational kinetic energy. This calculation concerns ideal translating particles on a suitable track; wheel rotation and loss of contact are outside the model.

Activity 6.21 · Name the missing condition (10 minutes)

For each statement, identify the missing condition or information: “Two trolleys collide, so their momentum is conserved”; “the object loses height, so its speed must increase”; “the work is zero, so there are no forces”; “my code balances energy, so it proves the experiment”. Write a corrected version before reading the discussion.

Discussion of Activity 6.21

Momentum conservation requires negligible external impulse for the chosen system and component. A gravitational decrease can be transferred elsewhere instead of increasing speed. Nonzero forces can do zero work through perpendicularity, zero displacement or cancellation in the net work. Code can satisfy a balance by construction; testing an experiment requires independent observations and their limitations. Good corrections name the system, interval and missing transfer, rather than merely adding “usually”.

Exercise 6.1 · Signed change

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 6 minutes

A 0.40 kg object changes velocity from−2.5 to+1.5 m/s. Find initial momentum, final momentum and change. Explain why subtracting speeds fails.

Hint for Exercise 6.1

Use final minus initial, keeping signs.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.1

Initial momentum is pi=(0.40)(−2.5)=−1.0 kg m/s and final momentum is pf=(0.40)(1.5)=+0.60 kg m/s. Thus Δp=0.60−(−1.0)=+1.60 kg m/s. Subtracting speeds would hide the reversal.

Return to Exercise 6.1

Exercise 6.2 · Time-weighted impulse

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 7 minutes

A horizontal resultant is+10 N for 0.08 s, then−2 N for 0.20 s. Find total impulse and the average over both intervals. A 0.50 kg object begins at−0.40 m/s; find its final velocity.

Hint for Exercise 6.2

Add signed force–time products before dividing by total time.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.2

The total impulse is 10(0.08)−2(0.20)=+0.40 N s. The average is 0.40/0.28≈+1.43 N. Initial momentum is −0.20 kg m/s; final momentum is +0.20 kg m/s, giving final velocity +0.40 m/s. Weighting the two forces equally would ignore their different durations.

Return to Exercise 6.2

Exercise 6.3 · Audit the system

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 6 minutes

A gives B+0.30 N s, B gives A−0.30 N s, and a hand gives A+0.04 N s. Find the changes for A, B and A+B. Can the pair be treated as momentum-conserving to a 0.01 kg m/s resolution?

Hint for Exercise 6.3

Separate internal exchange from the hand.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.3

A changes by −0.30+0.04=−0.26 kg m/s and B by +0.30 kg m/s. Their total changes by +0.04 kg m/s. This exceeds the target resolution; cancellation of internal impulses does not remove an outside impulse.

Return to Exercise 6.3

Exercise 6.4 · Sticking with signs

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 7 minutes

A 0.30 kg trolley at+2.0 m/s sticks to a 0.70 kg trolley at−0.50 m/s. Neglect external impulse. Find common velocity and kinetic energies before and after.

Hint for Exercise 6.4

The final velocities are equal; kinetic energies must be calculated separately before contact.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.4

Initial momentum is 0.30(2.0)+0.70(−0.50)=+0.25 kg m/s. Dividing by total mass 1.00 kg gives v=+0.25 m/s. Before contact, Ki=0.600+0.0875=0.6875 J; afterwards, Kf=12(1.00)(0.25)2=0.03125 J. The decrease is 0.65625 J. Momentum is conserved; translational kinetic energy is not.

Return to Exercise 6.4

Exercise 6.5 · One equation, two unknowns

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 6 minutes

Two equal 1.0 kg particles initially have velocities+2.0 m/s and 0. Give two different pairs of final velocities that conserve momentum. Compare their kinetic energies and explain what momentum alone leaves undecided.

Hint for Exercise 6.5

The final velocities must sum to 2.0 m/s.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.5

Possible velocity pairs are (1, 1) m/s and (0, 2) m/s. Both give total momentum +2 kg m/s. Their kinetic energies are 1 J and 2 J, compared with the initial 2 J. Sticking supplies the first pair; an ideal elastic exchange is consistent with the second. Momentum alone does not select the outcome.

Return to Exercise 6.5

Exercise 6.6 · Projected work

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 6 minutes

A constant force(3.0, 4.0)N acts through displacement(−2.0, 1.0)m. Find the work. Is the force necessarily opposing the motion in every coordinate?

Hint for Exercise 6.6

Multiply corresponding components and add.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.6

W=3(−2)+4(1)=−2 J. The horizontal contribution is −6 J and the vertical contribution is +4 J. Negative total work by this force does not require both component contributions to be negative.

Exercise 6.7 · A stopping distance

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 7 minutes

A 1.5 kg particle travels right at 4.0 m/s on a horizontal track. A constant 3.0 N leftward resultant stops it. Find stopping distance and time by suitable methods; check the two answers together.

Hint for Exercise 6.7

Use work for distance and impulse for time.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.7

Initial kinetic energy is 12 J. The work equation −3s=−12 gives s=4.0 m. Initial momentum is +6 kg m/s, so −3t=−6 gives t=2.0 s. Acceleration is −2 m/s² and average stopping velocity is +2 m/s, independently giving 4 m in 2 s. Stop these equations at the stated stopping instant.

Return to Exercise 6.7

Exercise 6.8 · Support affects friction

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 8 minutes

A 2.0 kg block slides right through 2.0 m from rest. A 10 N pull acts 30° above horizontal; the sliding coefficient is 0.20. Find normal force, friction, net work and final speed.

Hint for Exercise 6.8

Use vertical balance before the horizontal work account.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.8

Vertical balance gives N=19.6−5.0=14.6 N. Friction is 0.20(14.6)=2.92 N. Pull work is 20cos30∘≈17.321 J and friction work is −5.84 J. Net work is about 11.481 J, so v=2(11.481)2.0≈3.39 m/s. Positive support and a rightward resultant support the assumed contact and sliding direction.

Return to Exercise 6.8

Exercise 6.9 · Move the potential zero

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 7 minutes

A 0.80 kg object rises from−0.20 m to+0.55 m relative to a shelf. Find its potential-energy change and gravity work. Give the new heights if the zero is moved 1.0 m higher and verify the same change.

Hint for Exercise 6.9

Only the difference of heights enters the prediction.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.9

The rise is 0.55−(−0.20)=0.75 m. Potential energy increases by 0.80(9.8)(0.75)=5.88 J; gravity does −5.88 J. The new heights are −1.20 m and −0.45 m, still differing by 0.75 m. Negative absolute potential energy is allowed.

Return to Exercise 6.9

Exercise 6.10 · An energy claim

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 8 minutes

A 1.0 kg particle starts at 2.0 m/s and descends 0.50 m. With no driving input, it finishes at 3.0 m/s. Infer dissipated energy. Then test a claimed 4.0 m/s endpoint and state what additional input it would need if dissipation were zero.

Hint for Exercise 6.10

Compare available initial-plus-gravitational energy with final kinetic energy.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.10

The available energy is 2.0+4.9=6.9 J. At 3.0 m/s, final kinetic energy is 4.5 J, leaving 2.4 J dissipated. At 4.0 m/s, 8.0 J is required: an additional input of at least 1.1 J is needed, even with zero dissipation. The original no-input account is inconsistent.

Return to Exercise 6.10

Exercise 6.11 · A collision followed by a climb

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 12 minutes

A 0.50 kg cart at 2.4 m/s sticks to a stationary 0.70 kg cart, then the pair climbs a smooth fixed track. Neglect external impulse during collision and losses during climb. Find joined speed, kinetic energy lost in impact, and maximum rise. Identify a calculation that would count too much energy.

Hint for Exercise 6.11

Treat impact and climb as different intervals with different conservation conditions.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.11

Momentum gives the shared velocity v=1.2/1.2=1.0 m/s. Initial kinetic energy is 1.44 J; after sticking it is 0.60 J. Thus 0.84 J leaves the translational account. The maximum rise is h=0.601.2(9.8)≈0.0510 m. Using the original 1.44 J for the climb wrongly conserves kinetic energy across sticking. Contact and negligible wheel rotation remain assumptions.

Return to Exercise 6.11

Exercise 6.12 · Review a computational conclusion

P101 · Unit 6 · Core · 10 minutes

A program computes the sticking velocity from conserved momentum, then reports zero momentum residual and declares the model experimentally proved. An energy program replaces any negative final energy with zero before taking a square root. Explain both defects and propose one independent check for each.

Hint for Exercise 6.12

A balance used to generate a result is not independent evidence; a guard should expose inconsistent inputs.

Worked solution to Exercise 6.12

The momentum residual checks implementation consistency, not independently measured outcomes. Use a hand benchmark and, for a physical test, independent velocities with uncertainty and external-impulse estimates. Clamping negative kinetic energy hides an impossible endpoint: reject it and inspect the ledger. A case with 19.6 J available and 25 J claimed dissipation must raise an error. Height-zero invariance checks code but is not experimental evidence.

Return to Exercise 6.12

6.24 What the balances have taught us

We began with two trolleys whose brief contact was difficult to resolve. Momentum let us relate their states without knowing every detail of the contact force, provided we justified the external-impulse approximation. Work and energy answered a different question: how motion and position changed the energy account, and where a missing mechanical-energy contribution might have gone.

The common habit is to define what is being counted before invoking conservation. Internal momentum exchanges can cancel in a total while changing both objects' motions. Mechanical energy can become internal energy while a fuller total remains balanced. A numerical answer becomes useful when its system, signs, interval and limitations remain attached.

Unit 7 turns towards temperature, internal energy and thermal transfer. The dissipated-energy term that we have kept visible here becomes an invitation to describe the receiving system more carefully. Keep your corrected collision and energy comparisons: their distinctions between model, code and evidence will remain useful.
