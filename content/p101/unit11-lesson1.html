P101 · Unit 11 · Lesson 1

From interactions to fields

We begin with an interaction you can already calculate: gravity. Asking which part of its force law belongs to the source leads naturally to a field, and gives us a way to organise electric forces.

Allow three hours: 75 minutes reading and worked reasoning, 65 minutes for activities, and 40 minutes for feedback, correction and retrieval.

11.1 A field separates source from probe

Consider a small object of mass m outside an isolated, spherically symmetric body of mass M. Unit 5 gave the gravitational force magnitude GMm/r2, where r is the distance from the body’s centre and G≈6.67×10−11Nm2kg−2. The force points towards the source. A second probe of twice the mass experiences twice the force at the same location. The factor GM/r2, however, is unchanged.

We call the gravitational force per unit probe mass the gravitational field, written g. It is a vector: both its magnitude and its direction are needed. The force on a probe is then

Fg=mg.

The field’s unit is newtons per kilogram. Since one newton is one kilogram metre per second squared, this is also metres per second squared. That equality of units has a physical meaning: if gravity is the only force, ma=mg, and the probe’s acceleration is g. If the probe rests on a support, the gravitational field still exists, but the support’s force changes the resultant and hence the acceleration.

Choose the radial direction away from the centre as positive. The radial component of the field is

gr=−GMr2.

The minus sign expresses the inward direction; the field magnitude is GM/r2. Here r is always a positive distance, not a signed horizontal coordinate. For a spherical source of radius R, this exterior formula applies at r≥R. It does not describe the interior simply by substituting smaller distances. Treat the source as fixed and the probe as small enough that its presence does not appreciably change the source arrangement.

Example 11.1 · Same field, different forces

At a point near a planetary surface, the field is 8.0Nkg−1 downwards. What gravitational forces act on probes of 0.50 kg and 1.5 kg? What happens if each is released with no other forces?

Multiplying mass by the same field gives 4.0 N and 12 N downwards. Dividing each force by its own mass gives the same acceleration, 8.0ms−2 downwards. The force changes with the probe; the field at the specified point does not. Holding either probe stationary requires another force, rather than a zero gravitational field.

Activity 11.1 · Move the probe, then change it (15 minutes)

A spherical source produces a field magnitude 6.0Nkg−1 at radius R. Find the magnitude at 2R, and the force on a 2.0 kg probe there. Replace it by a 3.0 kg probe without moving the source. State what changes. Sketch the field arrows at opposite points on the sphere of radius 2R.

Discussion of Activity 11.1

The field falls by a factor of four to 1.5Nkg−1. The forces are 3.0 N and 4.5 N towards the source. The probe replacement changes force but not field. At opposite points the arrows have equal lengths and opposite directions, each pointing inwards. Equal field magnitudes need not mean equal field vectors.

11.2 Charge and the direction of electric force

Electric charge is another property through which matter participates in an interaction. It comes with two signs, conventionally positive and negative, and is measured in coulombs, symbol C. A proton carries charge +e and an electron charge −e, where e≈1.602×10−19C. Ordinary objects contain both kinds. An object with equal total positive and negative charge is electrically neutral; transferring electrons can leave it with a nonzero net charge.

Charge is conserved in an isolated system: transferring charge changes where it is located, while preserving the total algebraic charge. For example, transfer of negative electrons from object A to object B leaves A more positive and B more negative by equal amounts. The sign describes the charge, not a positive or negative amount of matter. We will use ideal charged particles or compact objects whose sizes are negligible compared with their separation.

Two stationary point charges in vacuum exert forces along their line of separation. Like signs repel and unlike signs attract. Coulomb’s law gives the magnitude:

F=ke|Qq|r2,ke≈8.99×109Nm2C−2.

We use Q for a source charge and q for a probe charge. The absolute value makes this a magnitude formula. Decide direction from attraction or repulsion and the geometry, then assign a component sign. This avoids confusing a negative charge with a force pointing left. A negative charge can experience a force in either direction, depending on the source and its position.

Example 11.2 · A small charge, a measurable force

A fixed source Q=+4.0nC lies at x=0, and a probe q=−2.0nC at x=0.30m. Find the force on the probe. A nanocoulomb is 10−9C.

F=(8.99×109)(4.0×10−9)(2.0×10−9)(0.30)2N=7.99×10−7N.

The charges attract, so the probe’s force points towards the origin: Fx=−7.99×10−7N, or approximately −0.80μN. The source experiences an equal and opposite force; a support must act if it is to remain fixed. If the separation doubles, the force magnitude becomes one quarter as large.

The inverse-square form resembles Newtonian gravity, but charge’s two signs introduce attraction and repulsion. A neutral object can also respond through redistribution of its positive and negative constituents. We will not model that redistribution here; “neutral” means zero net charge, rather than a universal absence of electrical effects.

Activity 11.2 · Separate three signs (15 minutes)

Keep the source in Example 11.2. Put a +2.0nC probe first at x=+0.30m, then at x=−0.30m. Find both force components. Repeat for a negative probe at the left-hand location. Explain why neither the charge sign nor the coordinate sign alone determines the force sign.

Discussion of Activity 11.2

All three magnitudes are 7.99×10−7N. The positive probe is repelled: its force component is positive on the right and negative on the left. The negative probe on the left is attracted towards the origin, so its component is positive. Charge sign determines attraction or repulsion together with the source sign; location then determines the direction along the chosen axis.

11.3 The electric field at a position

Now divide the electric force by the probe charge. The resulting electric field E describes the fixed source arrangement at the position under consideration. Its direction is defined by the force on a positive probe. We can then recover the force for either sign of probe:

E=Feq,Fe=qE.

The units are newtons per coulomb. The probe used to infer the field must be sufficiently small that it does not appreciably rearrange the source charges. With that condition, halving the probe charge halves its force and leaves their ratio unchanged. Removing the probe removes that probe’s force, but does not remove the field produced by the sources.

For an isolated point source, with outward radial direction positive, Coulomb’s law becomes

Er=keQr2,r>0.

A positive source gives an outward field, a negative source an inward field. The magnitude is ke|Q|/r2. The source position is excluded: the ideal point-source expression is undefined there. Near a real extended object, its actual size and charge distribution can become essential.

A field drawing can show arrows at selected positions. Arrow direction records the vector direction; arrow length may represent magnitude if the drawing specifies a length scale. Alternatively, continuous field lines can show the direction through their tangents. A line does not by itself give a numerical field magnitude: consult the stated model or scale.

A position identifies where to evaluate the field. An arrow attached there identifies a vector. The two play different roles.

Key idea 11.1 · Field and response

The sources determine g and E. A probe’s response is mg or qE. A positive mass responds along g; a negative electric charge responds opposite to E.

Example 11.3 · Recover the field from a force

A probe with charge −3.0μC experiences electric force +0.012N along an x-axis. Find the field component and the force on a +0.50μC replacement.

Ex=+0.012−3.0×10−6NC−1=−4.0×103NC−1.

The field points in the negative direction, opposite to the original negative probe’s force. The replacement force is (0.50×10−6)(−4.0×103)=−0.0020N. Using the magnitude of the first charge in the division would reverse the inferred field incorrectly.

Activity 11.3 · Read the source’s map (15 minutes)

At a position the electric field is 250 N/C to the left. Find the force on q=+8.0nC and on q=−8.0nC. Then remove the probe. Which quantities remain defined? Explain why a field arrow is not automatically the path of a moving particle.

Discussion of Activity 11.3

The forces are 2.0μN left and right respectively. The source field remains defined after removal; there is no longer a force on that absent probe. A field specifies force and hence an acceleration contribution. The particle’s velocity can initially point elsewhere, so a trajectory need not follow a field line.

11.4 Combining fields from several sources

A probe can respond to more than one source. In our electrostatic model, add the field vectors produced by the separate fixed charges, then multiply the total by the probe charge. This is superposition. Along one axis it means adding signed components, not adding every magnitude as a positive number. In a plane you would add corresponding components, using the vector methods of M101.

Make the geometry explicit before calculating. Mark every source and the evaluation point. For each source, draw the direction that its field has at that point: away from a positive charge, towards a negative one. Only then assign positive or negative components and insert the distances. Every distance belongs to its own source–point pair.

Example 11.4 · Cancellation is a vector statement

Two fixed charges, each +2.0nC, are at x=−0.20m and x=+0.20m. Find the field at the origin, then change the right-hand source to −2.0nC.

Each source contributes magnitude 8.99×109(2.0×10−9)/(0.20)2=449.5NC−1. Initially the left source’s field points right and the right source’s field points left. Their sum is zero. After changing the right charge’s sign, its field at the origin points towards it, also right. The total is then +899NC−1, approximately 9.0×102NC−1.

The zero in the first arrangement is a cancellation at one position. Each source still contributes, and a displaced probe generally finds a nonzero field. In the second arrangement, a negative probe experiences force to the left even though the total field points right.

Activity 11.4 · Where can the field vanish? (20 minutes)

A +1.0nC source is at x=0, and a +4.0nC source at x=0.60m. Predict which source the zero-field point lies nearer. Find its position between the sources by equating field magnitudes. Explain why the fields cannot cancel outside that interval.

Hint

For 0<x<0.60m, the distances are x and 0.60−x. Sketch directions before taking square roots.

Discussion of Activity 11.4

Between the sources the fields oppose. Cancellation requires 1/x2=4/(0.60−x)2, with both distances positive. Taking positive square roots gives 0.60−x=2x, so x=0.20m. The point is nearer the smaller charge, which needs a shorter distance to match the stronger source. To the left of both sources both fields point left; to the right both point right, so neither region permits cancellation.

We now have a method for predicting a force at a position. If the object moves through many positions, the force can vary along its journey. The next lesson asks how to turn that changing force into an energy transfer.
