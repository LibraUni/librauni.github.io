P101 · Unit 2 · Lesson 3

Summarising a set
of readings

A list of readings contains more information than a single number can carry. Yet a long list is awkward to compare with another one. We need summaries that make a pattern easier to see, while remembering which parts of the record they leave behind.

In Lesson 2 we distinguished variation within a set from an effect that shifts the set together. We can now give some of those descriptions numerical form. We will calculate a mean, a range and an average distance from the mean, then ask what each tells us about a small set of measurements. These calculations describe the values supplied to them. Their physical usefulness still depends on the quantity, procedure and conditions recorded with those values.

Allow 4 hours, including 1 hour of Python: about 60 minutes for reading and worked examples, 90 for written activities, 60 for the notebook, and 30 for feedback and corrections. All numerical datasets here are simulated; no apparatus or new software package is required. Download the learner notebook or its plain Python companion. You will use signed arithmetic, lists, loops and functions from Unit 1. New notation and the few new Python operations are explained as they arise.

2.13 A summary keeps some information

Imagine being handed two reports: “the length was about 126 mm” and “the length was about 126 mm”. They appear to agree, but one might summarise repeated measurements of a single card, while the other combines five different cards. One might come from a sound ruler and another from a scale that reads high. Before comparing the numbers, establish what has actually been brought together.

For the core calculation, we return to record P101-U02-L01-A. It contains five simulated readings of one specified edge of a flat card. The ruler is stipulated to have 1 mm divisions, with readings taken from above to the nearest millimetre and the ruler lifted and replaced between trials. The card and endpoints are held fixed. 2.14 The mean as a balance point

The arithmetic mean is obtained by adding all the values and dividing by the number of entries. Every entry has equal weight in the calculation we use here. Write the readings as x1, x2 and so on up to xn. The subscript identifies the entry; n is the number of entries. The symbol x¯, read “x bar”, denotes their mean.

x¯=x1+x2+⋯+xnn

Use consistent units before adding. Dividing a sum of lengths by a count leaves a length: the count has no physical unit. The mean is a calculated description of these entries; it need not coincide with a reading on the instrument, and it does not automatically equal the true value of the measured quantity.

Example 2.11 · The card record, entry by entry

For record A there are five entries, including all three occurrences of 126 mm. Their sum is 630 mm, so:

x¯=(126+127+126+125+126)mm5=126mm

A shorter way to check the sum is to start from five copies of 126 mm. One entry is 1 mm higher and one is 1 mm lower; the changes cancel. The sum is therefore five times 126 mm. This second calculation checks the arithmetic without simply retyping the same addition.

Do not replace the five entries by the three distinct values 125, 126 and 127. That would describe a different list. It happens to give the same mean in this symmetric example, but the apparent success conceals an incorrect method.

The mean has a useful balance property. For record A, subtracting the mean from each reading gives signed deviations of 0, +1, 0, −1 and 0 mm. The upward and downward contributions cancel. More generally, the sum of the deviations from the arithmetic mean is zero: the original sum is exactly the number of entries multiplied by their mean. This is a check on the calculation, not proof that the measurement errors cancel. A deviation from the mean is not the same as a discrepancy from an independent reference.

Example 2.12 · A mean that was never displayed

A separate simulated record contains 20, 20, 20 and 24 mm. Its sum is 84 mm and its mean is 21 mm. The two distinct values alone would average to 22 mm, incorrectly giving the single 24 mm entry as much weight as the three 20 mm entries together.

x¯=(3×20+24)mm4=21mm

No reading equals 21 mm. Nevertheless, the deviations from 21 mm are −1, −1, −1 and +3 mm, which sum to zero. Equal dots placed at the four readings would balance at 21 mm on an ideal weightless number line. This balance point gives every observation its proper contribution; it is not necessarily the midpoint between the extremes.

There are useful limits to check before trusting any output. With equal positive weights, a mean lies between the smallest and largest input values, including the endpoints. If all entries are identical, the mean is that common value. A mean outside the observed span therefore signals a calculation, unit or input error for the kind of list considered here. These checks do not identify every fault, but they catch some quickly.

2.15 Two descriptions of spread

A centre is only part of a description. Consider the simulated lists C: 50, 51, 49, 50, 50 mm and V: 49, 52, 48, 51, 50 mm. Both sums are 250 mm and both means are 50 mm. The second list extends further to either side. A report containing only the means would conceal that difference.

The range is the largest value minus the smallest. For C it is 51 − 49 = 2 mm; for V it is 52 − 48 = 4 mm. The observed interval for V is from 48 to 52 mm, while its range is the single difference 4 mm. Giving the endpoints as well as the range makes that distinction clear. The range uses only two extreme values and says nothing about how the remaining entries are arranged between them.

Example 2.13 · The same range can conceal different grouping

Two more simulated lists are E: 48, 50, 50, 50, 52 mm and F: 48, 48, 50, 52, 52 mm. Both have mean 50 mm and range 4 mm. In E, three entries are exactly at the mean; in F, four entries are at the extremes. A mean and range together still do not reconstruct the list. We need a measure that takes every distance from the mean into account if that aspect of the grouping matters.

Why not average the signed deviations? Their total is always zero when calculated from the exact arithmetic mean, even for a widely spread set. To describe the sizes of deviations, use their absolute values: distances without signs. The absolute value of −2 mm is 2 mm, just as the absolute value of +2 mm is 2 mm. Zero remains zero. Vertical bars denote this operation, so |x−x¯| is the distance of a reading from the mean.

We define the mean absolute deviation about the mean, denoted D in this lesson, by averaging those distances. The name “average absolute deviation” is also used. We write the definition in full to avoid confusing it with other statistics that use similar abbreviations.

D=(|x1−x¯|+⋯+|xn−x¯|n

The steps are: calculate the mean of the complete list; subtract it from every entry; take each distance; add the distances and divide by the number of entries. Use the same mean throughout that calculation. The result has the same unit as the readings and cannot be negative. We are defining a descriptive measure of this list, not a bound on measurement error.

Example 2.14 · Average the distances, not the signs

For set V, the signed deviations sum to zero, but the distances sum to 6 mm. Thus:

D=(1+2+2+1+0)mm5=1.2mm

For set C, the distances are 0, 1, 1, 0 and 0 mm, giving D = 0.4 mm. Both calculations count all five entries. The value 1.2 mm is an average distance: it need not occur as any individual distance. In fact, two readings of set V are 2 mm from the mean, beyond that average distance. Therefore “mean ± D” is not an interval containing every reading, and it is not an established uncertainty interval for the measurand.

Zero variation in the recorded numbers is a narrower claim than zero measurement uncertainty.

2.16 What five readings cannot establish

Five readings give us five pieces of information about a stipulated process. They do not reveal every value that process might produce. The largest and smallest observed values delimit this record; a later reading may lie outside them. Adding fresh readings can change the mean and the spread, and there is no rule requiring the observed range to shrink as the record grows. With the same earlier entries retained, the range can only stay the same or increase.

The number of entries matters, but it must be a count of observations for the claimed procedure. Copying each of the five readings into a list a second time produces ten entries with unchanged mean, range and D. It creates no fresh measurement evidence. A single recorded value also gives a numerical range and D of zero, but one value cannot reveal repeatability. An empty list has no mean: there is no observation count by which to divide.

Example 2.15 · An unusual reading changes the summary

Construct a new teaching record G: 126, 127, 126, 125, 136 mm. This is an invented alternative, not a correction to record A. The first four entries match A, while the final entry is 10 mm higher. The sum is now 640 mm and the mean is 128 mm. The observed interval is 125–136 mm and the range is 11 mm.

Measured from the new mean of 128 mm, the distances are 2, 1, 2, 3 and 8 mm. Their total is 16 mm, so D = 3.2 mm. Compare record A’s mean 126 mm, range 2 mm and D = 0.4 mm. One entry has changed all three summaries; the effect on D includes the changed distances of the other four entries because the mean moved.

This sensitivity is a reason to inspect the record. It is not evidence that 136 mm is invalid. A wrong endpoint, movement, a transcription mistake or a real change are different possibilities. The values alone do not identify which, if any, occurred.

A value conspicuously separated from the rest is often called an outlier. Here we use that word descriptively, not as the outcome of a statistical rejection test. Removing a reading because it raises the spread would choose evidence according to the desired result. Instead, retain the original, inspect the procedure and source, and record any independent reason for a correction or exclusion. The next lesson will develop that audit trail more fully.

A clearly labelled sensitivity calculation can still be useful: “what would the summary be if that entry were not included?” It answers a conditional question and must not silently replace the full record. If the two summaries differ substantially for the intended purpose, report the sensitivity and investigate. Do not describe the smaller spread as proof that the shortened list is better.

A dot plot of H and J would be identical. Their numerical summaries would also be identical, because rearranging a list changes neither its sum nor its distances from the mean. Figure 2.15 restores the trial order and exposes a question about drift. A summary that is perfectly correct arithmetically can still be insufficient for the investigation.

Example 2.16 · A common shift survives averaging

Start with simulated set C, whose mean is 50 mm, range 2 mm and D = 0.4 mm. Add 2 mm to every reading, producing 52, 53, 51, 52 and 52 mm—the gauge indications from Lesson 2. The sum increases by five copies of 2 mm, so the mean increases by 2 mm to 52 mm.

The range remains 2 mm because both extremes moved by the same amount. Each reading and the mean also moved together, so every deviation from the mean is unchanged. D therefore remains 0.4 mm. Averaging did not remove the stipulated offset. Conversely, subtracting a justified constant offset would move the mean back without making the individual readings agree more closely.

2.17 Compute a summary without erasing the record

A short program should follow the same reasoning as the hand calculation. We will pass a list into a function, accumulate its total, and divide by its length. Keep units in the dataset names and output labels. The functions below accept real numerical readings expressed in one common unit; they do not check whether the entries describe the same physical quantity. That check remains part of preparing the record.

The empty-list case now matters differently from Lesson 2’s conversion loop. An empty converted list correctly says that no values were converted. A numerical mean for an empty list would invent a summary where none exists. The function checks the count first. The new statement raise ValueError(...) deliberately stops the calculation and gives an explanatory error message when the supplied values cannot support it. Here == tests equality; the following indented line runs only when the count is zero.

def mean_value(values):
    count = len(values)
    if count == 0:
        raise ValueError("A mean needs at least one reading")
    total = 0.0
    for value in values:
        total = total + value
    return total / count

# P101-U02-L01-A: simulated card-edge readings, in trial order.
raw_mm = [126, 127, 126, 125, 126]
mean_mm = mean_value(raw_mm)
print("Number of entries:", len(raw_mm))
print("Mean / mm:", mean_mm)
print("Preserved readings / mm:", raw_mm)

The expected output includes five entries and a mean of 126.0 mm. The total starts at zero inside the function, before the loop. Each pass adds the current value to the running total. The return is outside the loop, so division happens only after every entry has contributed. Returning inside the loop would stop after the first entry. The function reads the list without changing it.

For the range, Python’s max(values) and min(values) select the largest and smallest entries. For absolute deviations, abs(number) returns the nonnegative magnitude of a real number. We use it after subtraction: abs(value - centre). This is different from subtracting absolute values, which generally gives a different result.

def range_value(values):
    if len(values) == 0:
        raise ValueError("A range needs at least one reading")
    return max(values) - min(values)

def mean_absolute_deviation(values):
    centre = mean_value(values)
    total_distance = 0.0
    for value in values:
        total_distance = total_distance + abs(value - centre)
    return total_distance / len(values)

range_mm = range_value(raw_mm)
spread_mm = mean_absolute_deviation(raw_mm)
print("Range / mm:", range_mm)
print("Mean absolute deviation / mm:", spread_mm)

The range is 2 mm and the mean absolute deviation is 0.4 mm. The second function first calls mean_value, which both finds the centre and refuses an empty input. Only then does it accumulate distances. Its name states which spread definition it implements: “spread” alone would not tell another reader whether the output was a range, a standard deviation or some other quantity.

In ordinary floating-point arithmetic, some decimal values cannot be represented exactly. A displayed answer may contain a small final-digit difference from a hand calculation. Check the mathematical result first, and compare small round-off differences appropriately; do not round every intermediate value merely to make the display tidy. The integer-valued teaching records make the independent checks easy. Extreme-magnitude or non-numerical inputs are outside these simple functions’ intended use.

Hint for Activity 2.18

Discussion of Activity 2.18

The practice mean is 11 mm, its range is 5 mm, and its distances are 1, 1, 1, 1 and 4 mm. Thus D = 1.6 mm. Both the singleton and the identical-value list have mean 7, range 0 and D = 0; they do not provide equivalent evidence about repetition. For the signed list the mean is 0, range 4 and D=43, approximately 1.3333333333333333 in Python. These generic test values inherit whichever common unit you assign.

The empty calls should raise ValueError; the refusal in mean_absolute_deviation comes from its call to mean_value. A suitable conversion loop is:

raw_m = []
for reading_mm in raw_mm:
    raw_m.append(reading_mm / 1000)

print("Mean / m:", mean_value(raw_m))
print("Range / m:", range_value(raw_m))
print("Mean absolute deviation / m:", mean_absolute_deviation(raw_m))
print("Original readings / mm:", raw_mm)

The mathematical values are 0.126 m, 0.002 m and 0.0004 m, allowing ordinary floating-point round-off in the displayed results. Keep the original list [126, 127, 126, 125, 126]. These checks support the implementation and unit handling for the intended inputs. They cannot verify a physical instrument, justify deleting a reading or establish that a supplied list came from real measurements.

Do not keep a mysterious final number without its definition. A useful notebook labels the dataset, source, common unit, entry count and the meaning of each summary. It also retains the predicted check values and short explanations. This is the difference between a calculator output and an inspectable calculation.

2.18 Report what was summarised

Return to the two-method comparison in simulated record P101-U02-L02-B. Method A recorded 52, 53, 51, 52, 52 mm; method B recorded 49, 52, 48, 51, 50 mm against a stipulated 50 mm reference. The same observer, fresh placements and 1 mm displayed increments were specified. Chronological order was A1, B1, B2, A2, A3, B3, B4, A4, A5, B5. The operational details distinguishing the two methods were missing. No correction was applied.

We can now replace some qualitative comparisons with explicit descriptions. Method A has mean 52 mm and method B has mean 50 mm. A’s range is 2 mm and D is 0.4 mm, while B’s range is 4 mm and D is 1.2 mm. These are statements about the five entries in each supplied list. They do not remove the missing method information or make the reference exact in a real experiment.

A compact report should identify which question each number answers. The mean describes a centre of the recorded indications. The range describes the difference between their extremes. Stating “52 ± 0.4 mm” without explaining the second number would invite the reader to give it a meaning that has not been established.

Activity 2.19 · From calculations to a defensible paragraph

Hint for Activity 2.19

A report can state agreement with the supplied reference while remaining cautious about what that agreement establishes. Preserve the distinction between a reference, an individual indication and a summary.

Discussion of Activity 2.19

A suitable account is: “This simulated comparison contains five direct indications from each of two methods, in millimetres, against a stipulated 50 mm reference. Method A has mean 52 mm, observed interval 51–53 mm, range 2 mm and mean absolute deviation about its mean 0.4 mm. Method B has mean 50 mm, observed interval 48–52 mm, range 4 mm and mean absolute deviation 1.2 mm. A is more tightly grouped in this record, but its mean is 2 mm above the reference. B’s mean agrees with the reference while its individual readings differ from it by up to 2 mm.

These summaries do not establish an exact true length or future performance. Five entries cannot determine the full variability of a procedure, and the reported spread does not account for a systematic effect shared across readings or uncertainty in a real reference. The operational differences between A and B were not supplied, so I would request those descriptions before attributing their behaviour to a particular cause. A further check with a suitable second reference length, retaining conditions and order, could investigate whether A’s discrepancy behaves like a constant offset.”

The quoted claim could be repaired as: “Method B’s five simulated indications have an arithmetic mean of 50 mm and observed endpoints 48 and 52 mm. These describe this record; they do not establish an exact length or bounds on future readings.” The added decimal in “50.0” supplies no new measurement evidence.

Use the feedback

Use the allocated 30 feedback minutes across the lesson to repair your reasoning. If your mean used only distinct values, return to Example 2.12 and count every observation. If a spread calculation gave zero for a varying list, inspect whether you added signed deviations instead of distances. If an unusually large reading disappeared, restore the original record and label any sensitivity calculation separately. For Python, compare the loop with the hand table one entry at a time before changing the code.

We began with a list that was hard to compare and have ended with a compact, interpretable summary. The gain comes with a responsibility: retain what the summary leaves out. Keep the raw order, method, units, unusual-event notes and source beside the mean and spread. Lesson 4 will use that preserved account to decide how to report a value with justified precision and how to handle awkward or incomplete data. The extra digits generated by today’s arithmetic are working information; they are not yet a final claim about what the instrument can resolve.
