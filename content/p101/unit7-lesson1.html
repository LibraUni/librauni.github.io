P101 · Unit 7 · Lesson 1

Temperature, heat and internal energy

A metal handle and a wooden door can feel different even after spending the night in the same room. Touch responds to what happens between your hand and the material. A temperature measurement needs a more reproducible procedure.

Allow three hours: 60 minutes for the unit introduction, this lesson and examples; 90 minutes for the four activities; and 30 minutes for feedback and correction.

7.1 A reproducible temperature

Imagine placing a small temperature probe in a beaker of water. Its reading initially changes because the probe and water were at different temperatures. After sufficient time, in stable surroundings, the reading settles. We use this settled reading to describe the water near the probe. The probe must respond to the water, be calibrated against a known scale and disturb the water little enough for the measurement to answer our question.

The underlying idea is thermal equilibrium. Two systems in thermal contact are in thermal equilibrium when they have the same temperature and there is no net energy transfer between them caused by a temperature difference. The word “net” matters: microscopic interactions continue. Equilibrium describes the balance of their effects, rather than every particle becoming still.

A thermometer exploits a property that changes reproducibly with temperature. A liquid column expands; an electrical sensor changes resistance. Calibration associates the observed property with a temperature scale. The number on a display therefore rests on a chain of physical comparisons. A display with many decimal places does not itself establish accuracy.

Suppose the same thermometer reaches equilibrium first with body A and then with body B, showing the same reading. We can use it to conclude that A and B have the same temperature. This consistency of thermal equilibrium makes a common temperature scale possible; it is often called the zeroth law of thermodynamics.

A stable reading is one check. Calibration, probe placement and possible disturbance remain separate checks.

Key idea 7.1 · Temperature and equilibrium

Temperature characterises thermal state. Bodies at the same temperature are in thermal equilibrium with one another. A contact thermometer estimates a body’s temperature by approaching equilibrium with the part it measures.

The metal handle feels cooler because it can transfer energy away from your warmer hand more rapidly than wood under the same conditions. The rate depends on material and contact as well as the initial temperatures. This explains why touch can distinguish two surfaces that a reliable thermometer finds to be at the same temperature.

Example 7.1 · Which reading answers the question?

A probe taken from a cool cupboard reads 17.0 °C immediately after entering well-stirred water, then 21.8 °C and finally a stable 22.0 °C. A student reports 17.0 °C because it was the first reading. Assess the choice.

Reasoning. The first reading still reflects the probe’s earlier condition. The later stable reading is the better estimate of the water temperature, provided the water remained approximately steady and the probe was calibrated. Stirring reduces temperature differences within the water. Reporting 22.0 °C should still carry the probe’s stated measurement limitation; the final digit alone is not an uncertainty estimate.

Activity 7.1 · Diagnose a measurement (20 minutes)

A thermometer held against the outside of a mug reads 35 °C; a probe in the centre of its recently poured drink reads 55 °C. Explain why these readings need not contradict one another. Plan three checks before describing the whole drink by one temperature. Then explain why equal readings in two separate mugs do not mean that the mugs contain equal amounts of energy.

Discussion of Activity 7.1

The sensors sample different places: the outer wall exchanges energy with the room while the centre of the drink may remain hotter. Check calibration, allow each sensor to settle and compare several positions after gentle stirring, with measurement times recorded. The temperatures may genuinely differ, so do not average incompatible readings automatically. Equal temperatures describe thermal state; the amount and kind of material also affect its internal energy. The next sections develop that distinction.

7.2 Temperature levels and temperature changes

The SI unit of temperature is the kelvin, symbol K, written without a degree sign. Celsius temperatures are also convenient for everyday thermal measurements. A kelvin and a degree Celsius have the same size as intervals, but the scales have different zeros. If TK and TC denote the numerical values in these two units,

TK=TC+273.15.

Thus 20.0 °C corresponds to 293.15 K. For the ordinary thermal systems considered here, 0 K is the absolute-zero limit. A negative Celsius reading simply lies below the Celsius zero; it does not indicate a negative absolute temperature in this model.

Now compare two temperatures. The fixed offset cancels on subtraction:

ΔTK=(TC,f+273.15)−(TC,i+273.15)=TC,f−TC,i.

A rise from 20 °C to 35 °C is therefore a rise of 15 K, equivalently 15 °C. Adding 273.15 to that difference would change the physical rise. Temperature ratios are different: 40 °C is twice the Celsius number 20 °C, but its absolute temperature is not twice as large. The choice of zero affects ratios of readings, although it does not affect differences.

Example 7.2 · A cooling instrument

An instrument cools from 18 °C to −7 °C. Express the two temperatures in kelvin and find the signed change.

Working. The initial temperature is 291.15 K and the final temperature is 266.15 K. Subtract final minus initial:

ΔT=266.15K−291.15K=−25K.

The Celsius subtraction gives −7 − 18 = −25 °C, the same interval. The negative sign describes cooling. The positive final absolute temperature describes a level on the Kelvin scale.

Activity 7.2 · Levels, intervals and ratios (20 minutes)

Convert −12 °C and 38 °C to kelvin; find the change between them in both interval units. A report says “the temperature doubled from 10 °C to 20 °C”. Calculate the ratio of the absolute temperatures and rewrite the report precisely. Explain why 50 K and a rise of 50 K are different statements.

Discussion of Activity 7.2

The levels are 261.15 K and 311.15 K; the rise is 50 K or 50 °C. The ratio is 293.15/283.15≈1.0353, not 2. A precise report is “the Celsius reading rose by 10 °C, from 10 °C to 20 °C”. A temperature of 50 K is a level close to the absolute-zero end of the scale; a rise of 50 K could begin at many different levels.

7.3 Energy stored and energy transferred

In Unit 6 the kinetic energy K described a body’s motion as a whole, while gravitational potential energy described its arrangement with Earth. A body at rest can also contain energy associated with its internal microscopic motions and interactions. We call this internal energy, written Eint. This notation keeps it distinct from the gravitational potential energy Ug used previously.

Internal energy is a property of the system’s state. Temperature is another property, but it is not an energy measured in different units. Two sealed samples of the same material at the same temperature can contain different amounts of internal energy because they contain different quantities of matter. Even for the same amount of matter, temperature alone may not specify internal energy: phase and microscopic arrangement matter too.

Heat names energy transferred because of a temperature difference. We write the transferred amount as Q, in joules, and take it as positive when it enters the chosen system. If a warm sample transfers 300 J to its cooler surroundings, its Q is −300 J; the surroundings receive +300 J. The sample has internal energy before and after the process. Heat describes what crossed the boundary during it.

Work is another way to transfer energy. A paddle driven through a liquid can increase its internal energy even in a well-insulated container. The transfer enters through the moving shaft; a temperature difference between the shaft and liquid is not needed to account for it. After the paddle stops and the liquid settles, the stored increase can remain. A temperature rise by itself therefore does not identify how the energy arrived.

Key idea 7.2 · Keep state and process separate

Temperature measures thermal state; internal energy describes microscopic energy held by a system; heat and work describe energy crossing its boundary during a process. Temperature is measured in K or °C; internal-energy changes, heat and work are measured in J.

Example 7.3 · The same rise by different routes

In one trial a stationary sample receives 450 J by heat transfer. In another, the same sample in the same initial state receives 450 J of paddle work while thermally insulated. Other energy transfers and changes of bulk motion are negligible. Compare the internal-energy changes.

Reasoning. Both trials add 450 J to the internal account. If the final phase and other state conditions are the same, they can produce the same final temperature. In the second trial Q≈0, even though the sample warms. Calling the temperature rise “450 J of heat stored” would obscure both the state quantity and the different routes.

Activity 7.3 · Follow the boundary (25 minutes)

Consider a warm metal block immersed in cooler water inside an insulated vessel. First choose the block alone; then choose block plus water, neglecting the vessel’s energy change. In each choice identify which exchange crosses the boundary and which is internal. Correct these claims: “the block contains heat”; “insulation prevents all changes of internal energy”; “equal temperature means equal internal energy”.

Discussion of Activity 7.3

For the block alone, heat leaves, so Qblock<0. For block plus water the exchange lies inside the system: one contribution decreases while the other increases, with negligible exchange across the insulated outer boundary. The block contains internal energy. Insulation suppresses heat transfer but a shaft or other work input can still change internal energy. Equal temperatures do not specify equal masses, materials or phases, so do not imply equal internal energies.

7.4 A microscopic picture with a macroscopic boundary

Picture a dilute gas as a large collection of particles moving in many directions and colliding. A gas in a closed container can have zero mean velocity as a whole while individual particles have substantial speeds. Oppositely directed velocities can cancel in an average; their kinetic energies, which involve squared speeds, remain positive. Random microscopic motion and organised movement of the whole container belong to different parts of our energy account.

For an ideal monatomic gas in equilibrium, absolute temperature is proportional to the mean translational kinetic energy per particle. Raising the temperature increases that mean; it does not give every particle one common speed. Adding more particles at the same temperature increases the total microscopic energy without increasing the mean per particle. This restricted model makes the difference between temperature and total internal energy especially clear.

Real materials offer more ways to hold energy. Molecules can rotate and vibrate; particles interact with one another. In a solid, particles fluctuate about equilibrium positions rather than travelling freely through the whole sample. Internal energy includes the relevant microscopic kinetic and interaction energies. A simple gas picture is useful precisely because we know which features it leaves out.

When a pure substance melts at its melting temperature under fixed pressure, energy transfer can change its phase while the temperature stays approximately constant. Some of the change concerns microscopic arrangement and interactions. This provides a concrete reason not to identify internal energy with a universal constant times temperature. In Lesson 2 we will deliberately restrict our temperature-change model to a fixed phase and a stated temperature range.

Activity 7.4 · Explain the microscopic account (25 minutes)

Two containers hold the same dilute monatomic gas at the same temperature; B contains twice as many particles as A. Compare mean particle translational kinetic energy and total translational internal energy. Now move A bodily across the room without changing its thermal state: which energy account changes? Finally explain why an ice–water mixture can receive energy while its thermometer remains nearly steady.

Discussion of Activity 7.4

The mean particle translational kinetic energies are equal because the temperatures are equal. B’s total is twice A’s in this ideal-gas model. Moving A changes its bulk kinetic energy relative to the room; that does not require a changed temperature. During melting, energy changes the phase balance and microscopic arrangement; the fixed-phase temperature-rise model will not apply to that interval. These explanations distinguish a mean from a total and a microscopic state from bulk movement.

We now have names for the quantities an energy balance must distinguish. The next task is quantitative: how much internal-energy change corresponds to a given temperature rise, and how do heat and work combine to produce it?
