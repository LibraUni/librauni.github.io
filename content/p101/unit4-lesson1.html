P101 · Block 2 · Unit 4 · Lesson 1

Systems, interactions
and force diagrams

A tray is being pulled across a bench. Before asking how quickly it will move, draw the tray and ask a different question: what is interacting with it now? That change of question takes us from the motion record of Unit 3 towards a physical explanation.

In this lesson, you will learn to make and check a force diagram. We will sometimes be given force values so that we can practise combining vectors, but we will not yet infer those values from the motion. Newton’s laws supply that next connection in Lesson 2. Keeping the tasks separate makes it easier to see whether an error lies in the physical model or in the arithmetic.

Allow three hours: 70 minutes for the two introductions, explanation and worked examples; 80 minutes for the six activities; and 30 minutes for checking, correction and redrawing. Use paper and, if useful, a calculator. The situations and numerical values below are invented teaching models. No physical experiment or Python work is required in this lesson.

4.1 Choose the system before naming forces

Place a book on a tray and imagine pulling the tray with a cord. There are several objects in the scene: the book, tray, cord, bench, Earth and the person pulling. A question about the book slipping relative to the tray is different from a question about the motion of the loaded tray as a whole. The first useful decision is which objects our calculation will describe.

We call the selected object or collection of objects the system. Everything excluded from that selection belongs to its surroundings. A system boundary is an imagined division between the two; it need not coincide with a physical wall. Write the selection in words, then outline it on a scene sketch. “The tray” and “the tray together with the book” are different systems, even though we have not changed the equipment.

Suppose the system is the book alone. Contact with the tray is an interaction with something outside the system. If we enlarge the system to include both book and tray, their mutual contact is now internal. The cord, bench and Earth remain outside. The boundary has not switched off any physical interaction; it has changed how we classify the interactions for the question we chose.

4.1.1 A point can represent an extended object

A tray has size and shape, and different parts may bend or turn. For now we will ask about its overall motion without analysing rotation or deformation. We can therefore represent the selected system by a dot in a force diagram. This is a particle model: we retain the interactions relevant to that motion while setting aside the object’s detailed geometry.

The dot does not mean that a real tray is tiny, nor that a pull literally acts at the dot. A force applied at the edge of an extended object may turn it; deciding that question would require information our point diagram deliberately discards. A good model records that limitation. Use a scene sketch to retain the geometry and a separate point diagram to organise forces.

Key idea 4.1 · State the system

Identify the objects inside the boundary before listing forces. “External” means an interaction between something in the system and something outside it. The same physical interaction may be internal for a larger system.

Example 4.1 · Choose for the question

Question. A sample container rests on a carrying tray. Which system would let us examine the support supplied by the tray to the container? Which choice would be useful for listing the supports acting on the complete loaded assembly?

Reasoning. For the first question, select the container alone. The tray is then outside, so its support appears explicitly in the external-force inventory. For the second, select container and tray together. Their contact is internal; the external supports are provided by objects such as a bench or a person’s hands, depending on the stated scene.

Interpretation. Neither choice is universally better. Selecting the combined assembly hides a contact we might need to determine; selecting each object separately creates more quantities to track. Let the required answer guide the choice, then keep it fixed while writing one set of equations.

Activity 4.1 · Draw the boundary

Allow 10 minutes. A tray carries two sample pots. A cord is attached to the tray, and the tray rests on a bench. You want to describe (a) one pot, (b) the tray alone and (c) the complete loaded tray. Sketch the three boundaries. In each case, classify the tray–pot contacts as internal or external.

Hint for Activity 4.1

An interaction is internal only when both participating objects lie inside the boundary.

Discussion of Activity 4.1

(a) Only the chosen pot lies inside. Its contact with the tray is external. The other pot’s contact with the tray is between two objects outside this system, so it does not belong in the chosen pot’s force inventory. (b) Only the tray lies inside; each pot–tray contact is external. (c) Both pots and the tray lie inside, so both pot–tray contacts are internal. The cord and bench remain outside all three selected systems.

Your sketches need not be artistic. Check that the boundary encloses exactly the named objects and that your classification follows that boundary, rather than the location of the contact on the page.

4.2 Give every force an agent and a recipient

A force is a vector quantity used to describe a push or pull arising from an interaction. It has a magnitude and a direction. It also has a recipient: the object on which it acts. In these introductory Newtonian models, naming the other participant—the agent exerting the force—is a powerful way to prevent invented or misplaced arrows.

For example, “the pull” is incomplete until we say what pulls what. If a cord pulls a tray, write “force of the cord on the tray”. The tray also interacts with the cord, but “force of the tray on the cord” names a different recipient. These two phrases must not be exchanged when constructing the tray’s diagram. Lesson 2 will establish the relationship between the two forces; we can already distinguish them by their labels.

We will use a bold letter for a vector and put the agent and recipient beneath it. Thus Fcord on tray means the force exerted by the cord on the tray. The word “on” points to the recipient in the label; it does not specify the direction of the physical force. In handwriting, use a clear arrow over the letter or underline it consistently to distinguish a vector from a scalar.

4.2.1 Contact is one possibility, not a requirement

The bench can push on a tray where their surfaces touch. A taut cord can pull at its attachment. Air can exert a force on a moving object. These are contact interactions. In a macroscopic model we usually combine many small effects over a contact surface into one force or a few components, rather than drawing an arrow for every point of contact.

Earth’s gravitational interaction does not require direct contact. It acts on the tray while the tray rests on a bench and after the tray leaves the bench. Near Earth’s surface, its direction is approximately vertically downwards. We call the gravitational force exerted by Earth on an object its weight. Weight is a force; mass, measured in kilograms, is a different physical quantity. We will calculate weight from mass in Lesson 3. Here, simply identify Earth as its agent.

The SI unit of force is the newton, symbol N. A label such as “4 N to the left” supplies a force magnitude and direction. “4 kg to the left” does not: the kilogram is a unit of mass. The definition of the newton in terms of mass and acceleration will become meaningful when we develop Newton’s second law. For now, keep the force unit explicit in all supplied values.

Note 4.1 · Invisible is not absent

A photograph can show a contact or a cord but cannot display gravity as a visible connection. Conversely, two objects that look close may not be touching. Use the stated physical situation and assumptions, not just the appearance of a sketch, to identify interactions.

4.2.2 A force is not carried along with the motion

Suppose a hand pushes a tray and then loses contact. The force of the hand on the tray is present during contact and absent after separation. The tray may still be moving. Its earlier push is part of the history that led to that motion; it is not an additional force stored inside the tray and carried forward.

This gives us a useful audit question: what is exerting this force now? An arrow labelled “motion” cannot answer it. Velocity belongs in a motion description, with units such as metres per second. A force belongs in an interaction description, with units of newtons. Both are vectors, but they are not interchangeable. If a motion arrow helps the scene sketch, draw it separately and label it explicitly as velocity.

Example 4.2 · A dropped marker

Question. A small marker has just left a person’s fingers and is falling. Ignore air resistance and other small air effects. Does its force list include the fingers, Earth, or a “downward motion force”?

Working. Select the marker alone. The fingers no longer touch it, so their contact force has ended. Earth still interacts gravitationally with it, giving one downward weight force. “Downward motion” names no agent and supplies no additional interaction.

Interpretation. The one-force model describes the stipulated interval after release and before the next contact. During the release itself, or after the marker lands, a different inventory is needed. Neglecting air effects is a stated approximation, not a claim that air is physically absent.

Activity 4.2 · Read the label all the way to “on”

Allow 10 minutes. A magnet pulls a steel washer across a horizontal bench without touching it. The washer is sliding to the right. List the possible agents acting on the washer, neglecting air effects. Explain whether “washer on magnet” and “rightward motion” belong in its force list. You need not know a formula for magnetic force.

Hint for Activity 4.2

Look for material contacts as well as non-contact interactions. A nearby magnet does not replace Earth or the bench.

Discussion of Activity 4.2

The agents are the magnet, Earth and the bench. The magnet supplies a non-contact magnetic force; Earth supplies weight; the bench supplies support and, unless neglected, sliding friction. “Washer on magnet” acts on the magnet, so it belongs to a different diagram. “Rightward motion” is not an agent or a force. The direction of magnetic pull must be obtained from the magnet’s stated position; movement to the right alone does not locate it.

This is a classification task, not a lesson in magnetism. No magnetic-force magnitude or law is required. A complete answer should make that limit explicit.

4.3 From a scene to a free-body diagram

Now select an empty tray pulled by a taut horizontal cord across a level bench. It is sliding rightwards. Model the tray as a particle, neglect air effects and retain sliding friction. We have enough information to identify directions without yet knowing the magnitudes.

Begin with Earth: the tray’s weight points downwards. The cord pulls to the right along its length, away from the tray. The bench’s contact force can be separated into a component perpendicular to its surface and a component parallel to it. The perpendicular component is called the normal force; “normal” means perpendicular. Here it points upwards. The parallel component is friction. Because this tray slides rightwards relative to the bench, sliding friction on the tray points leftwards.

This is four arrows, but only three agents: Earth, cord and bench. The bench contributes two components of its contact force. Listing those two components is sufficient; adding a further “total bench force” to the same inventory would count that contact twice.

The right-hand picture is a free-body diagram. “Free” means that we have isolated the selected system in the drawing. It does not mean that the tray has been physically detached, that it is falling freely, or that no forces act. We remove the drawn surroundings while retaining labelled arrows for their effects.

Choose axes beside the diagram: for this horizontal bench, rightwards positive x and upwards positive y are convenient. An axis arrow is a direction convention, not a force. When we reach an inclined surface, we may choose an axis along it instead. The normal direction will remain perpendicular to the surface even when that direction is not vertically upwards.

At this stage, draw arrow lengths schematically unless values or a force scale are supplied. Do not quietly make the upward and downward arrows equal because the picture looks tidier. The geometric directions of the interactions do not by themselves establish their magnitudes. Lesson 2 supplies the motion–force relation needed to impose a balance.

Key idea 4.2 · A free-body diagram is an inventory

Name the system and instant or interval. Include its external forces, label the agents and directions, and state neglected interactions. Keep motion arrows separate. Use either a force or its resolved components in a sum, and do not add the resultant as another interaction.

4.3.1 Conditions that remove an arrow

If a surface is described as smooth or frictionless in these models, its parallel contact force is neglected. Its normal force need not vanish. If the cord is slack, the ideal flexible cord supplies no pull; if it is taut, it may exert tension along its length. We will use the name tension for that pulling force and examine its magnitude later. A flexible cord cannot provide a compressive push in the way a rigid strut can.

For friction, pay attention to relative motion at the contact. The leftward direction above follows from the tray sliding rightwards relative to the bench. It is not a universal rule that friction points opposite a vehicle’s direction of travel. A driven wheel can receive a forward friction force from the ground without sliding. Static friction and its limits are developed in Lesson 3; this lesson’s sliding examples state the relative motion explicitly.

Example 4.3 · Pulling at an angle

Question. A small tray is pulled to the right by a taut cord rising at 30° above a smooth horizontal bench. The tray remains in contact with the bench. Ignore air effects. Identify the force directions, without calculating magnitudes.

Method. Keep the tray as the system and work through its agents. Earth supplies a vertical downward weight. The bench supplies an upward normal force; “smooth” removes the friction component. The cord pulls along the cord, upwards and rightwards at the stated angle.

Check. There are three force vectors. Do not replace the cord by a horizontal arrow merely because the tray’s path is horizontal. The upward part of its pull is real and will affect the support needed from the bench. The value of that support has not yet been found, and it must not simply be labelled equal to the weight.

Activity 4.3 · Support follows the surface

Allow 15 minutes. A small block slides down a smooth ramp that rises to the right at 20° to the horizontal. A taut cord along the ramp pulls the block uphill. The block stays in contact. Neglect air effects. Sketch the scene and the block’s free-body diagram, labelling all agents. Explain why neither the normal nor the cord force points vertically upwards.

Hint for Activity 4.3

Smooth removes friction. Draw a perpendicular to the ramp for the normal and follow the cord for its pull.

Discussion of Activity 4.3

There are three external forces: Earth on block vertically downwards; ramp on block perpendicular to the ramp, outwards from the surface (upwards and leftwards in this scene); and cord on block uphill along the ramp (upwards and rightwards). The normal follows the surface perpendicular, while the cord force follows the cord. Neither is determined by which direction is “up” on the page.

Do not add a downhill “force of sliding”. The block’s motion is stated, but we have not calculated its acceleration or the force magnitudes. An adequate sketch has the correct three directions and recipients, marks the smooth-surface approximation, and keeps any downhill velocity arrow outside the force diagram.

4.4 Combine forces without inventing another one

Once the inventory is settled, M101’s vector addition becomes useful. The resultant force, also called the net force, is the vector sum of the external forces on the selected system. It is a summary of that inventory. It is not a new agent acting alongside the original ones.

Take an idealised tray with supplied forces: the cord pulls 7 N to the right, sliding friction is 3 N to the left, and the upward normal and downward weight are each 12 N. These values are stipulated for this example; they have not been measured from Figure 4.3 or inferred from a speed.

With rightwards positive, the cord’s horizontal component is +7 N and friction’s is −3 N. Add the signed components:

Fnet,x=7N+(−3N)=+4N

With upwards positive, the vertical sum is +12 N − 12 N = 0 N. The resultant is therefore 4 N to the right. The original forces still exist in the model even though two components cancel in the sum. Zero resultant in one direction does not mean zero interaction in that direction.

For several forces, the same procedure gives

Fnet,x=F1x+F2x+⋯Fnet,y=F1y+F2y+⋯

The numerical subscripts 1 and 2 label entries in the force list; x and y label directions. The dots mean “continue adding the remaining entries”. Every term in either sum has units of force. Negative components record direction relative to the axes; a magnitude itself is nonnegative.

4.4.1 A sum may point along neither original force

For perpendicular contributions, adding magnitudes is generally wrong. A rightward force and an upward force cannot be collapsed into a larger purely rightward force. Place copies head to tail, as in M101, or add their components. The copy used for vector addition preserves magnitude and direction; it does not physically move the point at which a force is applied.

Example 4.4 · Two horizontal pulls viewed from above

Question. Two cords attached to a small ring pull horizontally: one exerts 8 N east and the other 6 N north. Find the resultant of these two pulls. This is only their horizontal sum; any vertical support and weight are outside the stated calculation.

Working. Choose east as positive x and north as positive y. The sum has components (8, 6) N. A head-to-tail sketch is a right-angled triangle, so its magnitude is

|Fpulls|=(8N)2+(6N)2=10N

The direction lies between north and east. If θ is measured northwards from east, then

tanθ=6N8N=0.75,θ≈36.9°

Interpretation. The sum is 10 N at about 36.9° north of east, not 14 N. The angle is less than 45° because the eastward component is larger. Both the quadrant and that comparison check the calculator result. If the northward pull were removed, the sum would return to 8 N east.

Note 4.2 · A component is a description

A sloping cord force can be represented by its horizontal and vertical components. Those components describe the same pull. Use the original vector or its components when adding; including all three would duplicate the pull. The same rule applies to the bench’s normal and friction components.

Activity 4.4 · Check a resultant twice

Allow 15 minutes. For one selected tray, the supplied components are 9 N right from a cord, 5 N left from bench friction, 14 N up from bench support and 14 N down from Earth. Find the resultant. Then reverse the horizontal axis and write its new signed component. Finally, for a different horizontal two-cord example with 5 N west and 12 N north, find the magnitude and specify its direction using a sketch or an angle.

Hint for Activity 4.4

Keep the vertical cancellation separate from the horizontal difference. Reversing an axis changes component signs, not the physical force. For the second case, locate the quadrant before taking an inverse tangent.

Discussion of Activity 4.4

The first resultant has horizontal component +9 − 5 = +4 N and vertical component +14 − 14 = 0 N: 4 N right. With leftwards positive, the same resultant has horizontal component −4 N. Its magnitude stays 4 N and its direction remains rightwards.

The second sum has east/north components (−5, 12) N and magnitude

(−5N)2+(12N)2=13N

It points northwest, closer to north than west. The angle is about 67.4° north of west, or 22.6° west of north. A clearly labelled proportional sketch is also acceptable. Adding magnitudes to get 17 N would ignore the perpendicular directions. Adding the computed 13 N as a third force would count the sum twice.

4.5 Change the boundary, change the force list

Return to the book on a tray, but now stipulate a simple vertical situation: the tray rests on a horizontal bench; the book rests on the tray; there is no cord or horizontal push. Neglect air effects. We will list only the vertical forces. First keep the book and tray as separate systems.

For the book, Earth supplies a downward gravitational force and the tray supplies an upward support. For the tray, Earth supplies its own downward gravitational force, the book pushes down on it, and the bench pushes up. “Tray on book” and “book on tray” name the same contact interaction viewed through different recipients. They belong on different individual diagrams.

If the system is enlarged to contain book and tray together, both sides of that contact lie inside the boundary. The external inventory then contains the bench’s upward support and the two downward gravitational forces from Earth, one on each included object. We may draw both weights separately or replace them by their downward sum. We must not include the separate weights and their total as three contributions.

The internal contact has not ceased to matter physically. It is how the tray supports the book. But it no longer crosses the boundary of the combined system. Later, Newton’s third law will explain how the two sides of such an internal interaction cancel in the total force sum for the Newtonian systems considered here. At present the important step is to classify the contact correctly before trying to add anything.

Keep that distinction separate from cancellation on one object. The upward tray force and downward Earth force on the book have the same recipient and can contribute to the book’s resultant. The forces “tray on book” and “book on tray” have different recipients. Placing both on the book’s diagram would create a false cancellation by mixing two systems. Similar-looking opposite arrows need not have the same physical role.

A wider view · One machine, many possible systems

In the unit’s crawler photograph, the ground is external if the system is the complete vehicle. Forces between the tracks and the rest of the vehicle are then internal. If an engineer studies a track assembly alone, some of those same mechanical contacts become external. A large machine does not require a new meaning of “system”; it requires a choice appropriate to the question.

Our dot model cannot determine stresses in the metal, individual contact pressures or whether a part will bend. Those questions need a more detailed model. The photograph supplies a setting for the boundary decision, not numerical evidence for the invented tray examples.

Activity 4.5 · Move one interaction inside

Allow 10 minutes. For the vertical book-and-tray situation, write the external-force list for (a) the book, (b) the tray and (c) both together. A draft combined-system diagram includes “tray on book” but omits “book on tray”. Explain why this is not repaired merely by adding the missing contact arrow.

Hint for Activity 4.5

For the combined system, neither side of the book–tray interaction crosses the boundary.

Discussion of Activity 4.5

(a) Tray on book upwards, Earth on book downwards. (b) Bench on tray upwards, book on tray downwards, Earth on tray downwards. (c) Bench on the combined system upwards, Earth on book downwards and Earth on tray downwards; the last two may be replaced by their sum.

The draft mixes an internal interaction into an external inventory. Both book–tray contact arrows should be absent from the combined free-body diagram. Adding the second internal arrow might later produce cancellation in a calculation, but it leaves the diagram’s stated purpose inconsistent. If the contact force is the quantity you want to find, return to an individual-system diagram where it is external.

4.6 Audit the model before solving it

A diagram is a claim about a particular situation, not a permanent property of an object. Release a cord, lose contact with a surface or reverse the direction of sliding, and the inventory may change. Before carrying a diagram into an equation, read its assumptions again.

Use the labels as a first check. Does every force have a recipient inside the system and an agent outside it? Use the scene as a second check. Have you included gravity as well as relevant material contacts? Use the model statement as a third check. Have you said why air effects or friction are neglected, and have you respected that choice consistently?

Then examine the representation. Are force arrows separate from velocity and axes? Does a perpendicular support actually look perpendicular? Have you counted a force and its components together, or added a resultant as if it were another interaction? Finally, check what the diagram can establish. Directions and a list of agents do not automatically supply magnitudes, a velocity or a future position.

Example 4.5 · The instant after a cord is cut

Question. A tray slides rightwards across a rough horizontal bench under a horizontal cord pull. The cord is cut, and we consider a short interval just after it has become slack while the tray is still sliding rightwards and remains on the bench. Ignore air effects. What changes in the diagram?

Reasoning. The cord no longer transmits a pull, so remove that arrow. Earth’s weight and the bench’s normal remain. Because the tray still slides rightwards relative to the bench, the sliding-friction arrow remains leftwards. Do not add a rightward “leftover pull” or reverse friction merely because the cord has been cut.

Interpretation. The inventory now has three arrows. This lesson alone has not calculated how long the tray keeps moving. To connect the leftward resultant to a change in velocity we need Newton’s laws; to predict a stopping time we also need quantitative force and motion information. A changed diagram is the start of that argument, not its finished answer.

Activity 4.6 · Repair a complete explanation

Allow 20 minutes. A sample capsule rests on a smooth horizontal platform and is pulled by a taut horizontal cord to the right. It remains in contact with the platform. The supplied forces on the capsule are 11 N upward support, 11 N downward weight and 3 N rightward cord pull. A draft adds a 3 N “motion force”, adds a 6 N rightward “resultant force”, and includes the downward force of the capsule on the platform. Repair the force list and diagram; compute the true resultant; explain the three mistakes. Then describe the force inventory immediately after the cord becomes slack, with the capsule still in contact. State what you can and cannot infer about its velocity from this lesson’s force inventory alone.

Hint for Activity 4.6

Use the recipient to reject one arrow and the agent test to reject another. The resultant belongs in a separate calculation. Smooth means no friction in this model.

Discussion of Activity 4.6

The correct inventory has just three forces: platform on capsule 11 N up, Earth on capsule 11 N down and cord on capsule 3 N right. The vertical sum is zero and the horizontal sum is +3 N, so the resultant is 3 N right. A correct sketch labels the capsule as the system and states that friction and air effects are neglected.

The “motion force” has no agent and duplicates the idea of movement as though it were an interaction. The proposed 6 N resultant was obtained from that false inventory; adding any resultant back into its own force list would be a second error. “Capsule on platform” acts on the platform, outside the selected system, and belongs to a different diagram.

After the cord becomes slack, retain only weight and normal support. In this horizontal setup the stipulated 11 N values give zero resultant. That is a statement about their sum, not a measurement of velocity. The capsule’s velocity cannot be read off as “zero” from the absence of a horizontal force. Lesson 2 will show how a zero resultant relates to an unchanged velocity in an inertial reference frame; an initial velocity is still needed to say what that velocity is.

Check your explanation against four criteria: a fixed system, a justified interaction inventory, a correct vector sum and an appropriately limited conclusion. If your first diagram mixed force and motion, redraw it rather than merely changing the final number.

Looking ahead

We began with a moving tray and deliberately postponed its equation of motion. We can now select the system, identify the interactions, represent their directions and compute a resultant when values are supplied. We also know why a change of boundary or a change of contact calls for a fresh inventory.

The next question is what that resultant tells us about the motion. Lesson 2 develops Newton’s laws and the meaning of acceleration. Bring the diagram habits with you: the laws will connect quantities in a model, but they cannot repair a force that was assigned to the wrong object.
