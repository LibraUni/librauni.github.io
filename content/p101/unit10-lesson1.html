P101 · Unit 10 · Lesson 1

Light and rays

A wavefront describes a common phase; a ray follows a direction of travel. We can move between these descriptions to explain why light travels straight through a uniform region but changes direction at a boundary.

Allow four hours: 96 minutes reading and worked examples, 90 minutes for activities and diagrams, and 54 minutes for feedback, correction and retrieval. No Python is required.

10.1 Light as a wave and a ray

A vibrating string needs the string itself to carry the disturbance. Light can cross the nearly empty space between the Sun and Earth. In the classical wave description, electric and magnetic fields vary as the wave passes. A field describes a physical influence at each position; its detailed dynamics will be developed later. Here the relevant result is that electromagnetic waves propagate in vacuum at speed c=299792458ms−1, for which 3.00×108ms−1 is sufficient in our calculations.

Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays belong to the electromagnetic spectrum. Ordered that way, their frequency generally increases and their vacuum wavelength decreases. The names describe useful regions of a continuous range; boundaries between some regions depend on convention. Visible light occupies roughly 400–700 nm in vacuum, with violet towards the shorter-wavelength end and red towards the longer-wavelength end. A nanometre is 10−9m.

The wave relation from Unit 9 still applies. In vacuum it becomes c=fλ0, where the subscript on λ0 identifies vacuum wavelength. Thus a frequency and a vacuum wavelength are two linked ways of identifying the same monochromatic light. “Monochromatic” means a single frequency in the ideal model. A broad source can contain many frequencies at once; a single wavelength is then insufficient to describe it.

Example 10.1 · Connect a colour-scale wavelength to frequency

Light has vacuum wavelength 600 nm. Estimate its frequency and the time for one oscillation.

Convert the length before using the wave relation: 600nm=6.00×10−7m. Then

f=cλ0=3.00×1086.00×10−7s−1=5.00×1014Hz.
T=1f=2.00×10−15s.

The period is far shorter than a camera exposure. An ordinary camera records a response to the light accumulated during an exposure, not a sequence of individual optical oscillations. Later we will distinguish those oscillations from slow changes in a lamp’s brightness.

In a uniform, isotropic transparent medium, propagation is perpendicular to the wavefront. A ray is a line with an arrow showing that propagation direction. Rays are especially useful when wavelengths are much smaller than the surfaces, openings and features under study. This is the geometrical-optics approximation. Unit 9 explains why it becomes inadequate near a sufficiently small opening: diffraction spreads the wave beyond a straight-ray construction.

A ray is not a narrow luminous thread that an observer sees from every direction. To see a beam from the side, some light must be scattered towards the eye, for example by dust or droplets. A ray drawing instead tracks the light that can reach a specified destination. We will use it to connect an object point to a screen or eye.

Activity 10.1 · Choose a useful description (20 minutes)

A 3.0 mm aperture is illuminated with 500 nm light. Calculate the aperture-to-wavelength ratio. Compare it with a 1.0 μm opening. Which case gives more reason to question a sharp ray shadow? Then find the vacuum wavelength of a 1.00×108Hz radio wave. Explain why its different wavelength does not imply a different vacuum speed.

Discussion of Activity 10.1

The ratios are 6000 and 2 respectively. Diffraction is much more prominent for the opening only two wavelengths across; rays are a better starting approximation for the larger aperture, although finite diffraction remains. The radio wavelength is 3.00 m because λ0=c/f. All these vacuum electromagnetic waves use the same speed; their frequencies and wavelengths change reciprocally.

10.2 Reflection and the normal

At a surface, begin by marking the point where the incident ray arrives. Draw the line perpendicular to the surface there. This line is the normal. It is a geometrical reference, not another light ray. The angle of incidence is measured between the incoming path and the normal. The reflected ray remains in the incident medium, with its angle to the normal equal to the incident angle. Incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane.

This convention matters because a surface and its normal differ by a right angle. If a ray makes 25° with a flat surface, its incidence angle is 65°. Substituting 25° into a formula requiring the normal angle would describe a different ray. On a curved surface the normal changes from point to point; the same local reflection rule still applies.

A polished mirror sends initially parallel rays in one common reflected direction. A rough surface has many differently oriented local normals, so the reflected directions vary. That scattered light allows several observers to see the same sheet of paper from different positions. The local angle rule has not failed; the orientations being used differ.

Key idea 10.1 · Start with the normal

At a smooth boundary, θr=θ1. Angles refer to the local normal, and ray arrows distinguish approaching light from departing light. Draw these before calculating.

Example 10.2 · Reconstruct a reflected path

A ray travelling down and right reaches a horizontal mirror at 35° to its surface. What is its reflected direction?

The incidence angle is 90∘−35∘=55∘. Reflection gives 55° to the upward normal, on the opposite side of it. The ray therefore travels up and right, 35° above the surface. The outgoing ray does not retrace the incoming path; retracing occurs at normal incidence.

Activity 10.2 · Make the convention visible (20 minutes)

Draw rays incident at 0°, 30° and 70° to a horizontal mirror’s normal, with their reflections. Label the angles to the surface as well. For each case, explain which observer positions could receive the reflected ray. Then explain why replacing the mirror by white paper changes the range of viewing directions.

Discussion of Activity 10.2

The reflected normal angles are 0°, 30° and 70°; the surface angles are 90°, 60° and 20°. At 0° the light returns along the incoming line. At the other angles an observer must lie along the outgoing path to receive this ideal reflected ray. Paper’s varied surface structure scatters light into many directions, so visibility is not confined to that single outgoing path. Your diagrams should show a normal at the contact point and opposite incoming/outgoing arrow senses.

10.3 Speed, wavelength and refraction

Light entering transparent material interacts with that material, and the wave’s phase propagates at a speed that can differ from its vacuum value. For the transparent media and frequencies used here, define the refractive index by n=c/v. It is dimensionless: both numerator and denominator are speeds. A larger index means a smaller phase speed. We will use supplied indices, since they depend on material and frequency.

At a stationary interface the transmitted sinusoidal wave has the same frequency as the incident wave. Successive cycles arrive at the boundary at the source’s rate; a steady response continues at that rate on the other side. Because v=fλ, a change in speed at fixed frequency changes wavelength. If λ0 is the vacuum wavelength, then λ=λ0/n inside the material.

Oblique incidence introduces a geometrical consequence. One part of a wavefront enters the second medium before another part. If the transmitted wave propagates more slowly, the part already inside advances a smaller distance while the other part is still approaching. The wavefront changes orientation, and its perpendicular ray changes direction. At normal incidence the wavefront enters all at once; the speed and wavelength can change without a change of ray direction.

The quantitative rule for the two directions is Snell’s law:

n1sinθ1=n2sinθ2.

The subscripts identify the incident and transmitted media. For angles between 0° and 90°, sine increases with angle. If n2>n1, the equation requires sinθ2<sinθ1, so the ray turns towards the normal. Passing to a lower index gives bending away from the normal when a transmitted ray exists.

Example 10.3 · Track three quantities through a boundary

A monochromatic wave with vacuum wavelength 540 nm enters a material of index 1.50. Find its frequency, phase speed and wavelength inside.

The source frequency is 3.00×108/(540×10−9)=5.56×1014Hz, to three significant figures. The phase speed is c/1.50=2.00×108ms−1, while the wavelength is 540/1.50=360nm. Multiplying this shorter wavelength by the unchanged frequency recovers the smaller speed. Keep the unrounded frequency for that check.

The same surface can reflect some light and transmit some. Snell’s law determines a transmitted direction; it does not specify the fraction of the energy taking each path. We will need energy per area later, when asking how much light a detector receives.

Activity 10.3 · Predict before calculating (20 minutes)

Light crosses from index 1.60 to index 1.20. Predict the changes in speed, wavelength and frequency. Find both speeds using c=3.00×108ms−1. For an incident angle of 30°, predict whether the transmitted angle is larger or smaller before calculating it in the next section.

Discussion of Activity 10.3

The speed rises from 1.875×108 to 2.50×108ms−1, a factor of 4/3. Wavelength rises by that factor while frequency stays fixed. The transmitted ray bends away from the normal, giving an angle greater than 30°. These predictions provide checks on the calculator result.

10.4 Solving and checking a refraction problem

To find the transmitted angle, first isolate its sine:

sinθ2=n1n2sinθ1.

This right-hand side is a dimensionless number. An inverse sine then recovers the angle. The notation sin−1(x), also written arcsin(x), means the angle whose sine is x; it is different from 1/sin(x). Set the calculator to degrees for the angles in this lesson. We choose the transmitted angle between 0° and 90° measured from the normal into the second medium.

Example 10.4 · Test the algebra against the direction

Complete Activity 10.3 for n1=1.60, n2=1.20 and θ1=30∘.

sinθ2=1.601.20sin30∘=23.
θ2=sin−1(23)≈41.8∘.

The angle increases, as predicted. Substitution gives 1.60sin30∘=0.800, and 1.20sin41.8∘≈0.800. A value of 22° would have the wrong bending sense even before we checked the arithmetic.

There is also a domain check. No real angle has sine greater than one. Suppose the same pair of media is used at incidence angle 60°. The right-hand side is approximately 1.155. The calculator cannot return a real transmitted angle because this simple propagating-ray solution does not exist. For an ideal transparent boundary from higher to lower index, this is the regime of total internal reflection.

The limiting incidence angle, called the critical angle, gives a transmitted direction along the boundary, θ2=90∘. Setting its sine to one gives sinθc=n2/n1, possible when n1>n2. In the present case it is sin−1(0.75)≈48.6∘. Below that angle a transmitted ray exists; above it all incident energy is reflected in this ideal lossless interface model. A ray incident from lower to higher index has no such critical angle.

Activity 10.4 · Diagnose and complete a calculation (30 minutes)

A ray in air, taken as index 1.00, makes 40° with a flat glass surface of index 1.50. A draft solution uses sin−1(1.50sin40∘)=74.6∘. Identify both setup errors, calculate the correct transmitted angle and sketch the path. Then reverse that path and verify Snell’s law. Finally, find the critical angle for glass to air and decide whether an internal incidence of 50° transmits.

Hint

First convert the surface angle to a normal angle. Then put the incident index in the numerator.

Discussion of Activity 10.4

The incidence angle is 50°, not 40°, and the index ratio is 1/1.50. Thus θ2=sin−1(sin50∘/1.50)≈30.7∘. The transmitted ray is closer to the normal. Reversing the path gives 1.50sin30.7∘≈sin50∘, returning to 50° in air. The critical angle is sin−1(1/1.50)≈41.8∘; an internal incidence of 50° gives total internal reflection. The two 50° cases are different because the incident medium differs.

We can now follow a ray through one stated boundary and check the result using direction, frequency continuity and the sine’s domain. A lens uses curved boundaries to redirect many rays. The next lesson asks when rays leaving one object point meet again, and how that meeting produces an image.
