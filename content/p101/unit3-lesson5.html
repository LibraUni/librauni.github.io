3.25 A question the record can answer

Suppose a colleague asks, “Can one constant velocity describe this carriage record?” That is a useful question because it names both the proposed description and the evidence against which it will be examined. “Explain everything about the motion” would be too broad. We do not know the continuous path between observations, the instrument behaviour, or the interactions responsible for the motion. A calculation cannot manufacture that missing information.

Our new record, P101-U03-L05-C, version 1, is entirely invented. It follows one painted mark along a straight rail, with a fixed origin, rightwards positive and one clock. The listed times are seconds and positions are metres. The blank position at 29 s is deliberately unavailable. The other values are stipulated for this mathematical comparison; they carry no measured uncertainty estimate. This record is independent of the earlier A record and its incomplete B copy: do not import their values or model parameters.

Example 3.17 · A rate is chosen for a reason

Question. Find the velocities for C1 and C2. Why should we expect them to agree with some of the entries?

Working. For C1, subtract positions in the same order as times. For C2, use the full elapsed interval, not the last clock reading:

vC1=3−122−20m/s=1m/s
vC2=5−132−20m/s=13m/s

Interpretation. C1 passes through the first two entries because they define its rate and reference event. C2 similarly passes through the first and last. Those agreements are useful arithmetic checks, but they are built into the choices. Look at the remaining available observations to ask whether either continuation is consistent with more than its construction points.

3.26 Build the evidence comparison

Begin with the source description and the five CSV rows. Before running the loader, check their order and units by eye. The supplied clock readings are increasing and distinct, so the first and last entries mean what our model descriptions require. The loader below reuses Lesson 4’s checks. It verifies the expected shape and finite clock readings; it does not establish that the times are ordered correctly, that the units are honest, or that a real instrument was reliable. A named check should be credited only for what it actually tests.

from pathlib import Path
import platform
import numpy as np
import matplotlib
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

project_dir = Path.cwd()
data_path = project_dir / "data" / "record.csv"
print("Working directory:", project_dir)
print("File exists:", data_path.is_file())
raw = np.genfromtxt(
    data_path, delimiter=",", skip_header=1, dtype=float,
    filling_values=np.nan, ndmin=2, loose=False
)
print("Rows, columns:", raw.shape)
if raw.shape != (5, 2):
    raise ValueError("Expected five rows and two columns; inspect the source.")
time_all_s = raw[:, 0]
position_all_m = raw[:, 1]
if not np.all(np.isfinite(time_all_s)):
    raise ValueError("A clock reading is unavailable; inspect the source.")
usable = np.isfinite(time_all_s) & np.isfinite(position_all_m)
time_s = time_all_s[usable]
position_m = position_all_m[usable]
if len(time_s) == 0:
    raise ValueError("No complete pairs remain.")
print("Retained times / s:", time_s)
print("Retained positions / m:", position_m)
print("Omitted times / s:", time_all_s[~usable])

Four pairs remain, at 20, 22, 25 and 32 s. The same mask selects both columns. The 29 s row remains in the source and appears in the omission report. Choosing a model prediction for that missing position would make the plot look more complete, but it would create no new observation. Keep any prediction there labelled as a prediction.

Next calculate C1 using the function already developed in Lesson 3. Notice the explicit reference time. A function that used the clock reading directly would give 21 m at 20 s, failing to return the stipulated 1 m reference position. This is why a simple limiting case is valuable even when the rest of the output looks smooth.

def predict_position(times_s, t_ref_s, x_ref_m, velocity_m_per_s):
    return x_ref_m + velocity_m_per_s * (times_s - t_ref_s)

reference_time_s = 20.0
reference_position_m = 1.0
velocity_c1 = (3.0 - 1.0) / (22.0 - 20.0)
predicted_c1_m = predict_position(
    time_s, reference_time_s, reference_position_m, velocity_c1
)
residual_c1_m = position_m - predicted_c1_m
print("C1 predictions / m:", predicted_c1_m)
print("C1 residuals / m:", residual_c1_m)

The residual is observed position minus predicted position, in metres. Keep that subtraction in the same order for both models. C1 predicts positions 1, 3, 6 and 13 m at the retained times; the corresponding residuals are 0, 0, 0 and −8 m. At the last time, the observed mark lies 8 m on the negative side of the predicted location. This statement describes a difference between positions. It does not mean that the carriage moved at −8 m/s.

Example 3.18 · Agreement beyond the construction points

Question. What does the 25 s entry tell us about C1 and C2?

Method. It was not used to choose either rate. Substitute its time into each model, then compare with its available position, 6 m. For C2 the elapsed time is 5 s. Multiplying its velocity, one third of a metre per second, by 5 s gives a predicted displacement of 5/3 m:

xC2(25 s)=1 m+53 m=83 m
rC2=6m−83m=103m

Interpretation. C1 predicts 6 m and agrees with this further entry; C2 predicts about 2.67 m and leaves a positive residual of about 3.33 m. Thus C1 describes more of the early supplied positions, while C2 captures the overall endpoint change. Neither observation licenses a claim about every instant between the readings. Because the dataset is invented, agreement here is a consistency check in a teaching case, not independent experimental validation.

3.27 Hand over a defensible conclusion

An effective conclusion does more than announce which line looks closer. It links a particular claim to a particular comparison. Start by naming the record and conventions. Then identify the model’s construction, quote evidence that was not merely guaranteed by that construction, and state the limit of the claim. This order allows a reader to distinguish a calculation they can check from an interpretation they may need to question.

For C1, the agreement at 25 s adds information beyond the first pair, but the disagreement at 32 s prevents us from extending that success over the whole record. C2 returns the correct net endpoint change because that is how it was chosen. Its interior residuals reveal what the endpoint description leaves out. A single overall average velocity is still a valid summary of net change over an interval even when it is a poor constant-velocity model of that interval.

Example 3.19 · Repair an overconfident conclusion

Draft claim. “C2 is the true motion because it reaches the correct final position. Python proves it, and the missing point must be 4 m.”

Diagnosis. C2’s final agreement is built in. The two interior residuals contradict exact agreement with the supplied record. At 29 s, C2 predicts 4 m, but no observation is available to test it. Python evaluates the chosen instructions; it cannot promote a predicted value into measured evidence.

A defensible replacement. “For invented record C version 1, C2 uses the 20–32 s average velocity, one third of a metre per second, anchored at 1 m at 20 s. It reproduces its construction endpoints but predicts 8/3 m at 25 s against the available 6 m, leaving residual 10/3 m. The 29 s position is unavailable and was not replaced. Thus C2 does not reproduce every supplied position; the record alone does not determine the continuous path or explain the change in motion.”

For real data, we would also need the measurement method and an appropriate account of uncertainty before deciding whether a numerical discrepancy is physically significant. No uncertainty bound is supplied in this invented example. It would be unjustified to call 3.33 m “within experimental error”, or to assign a confidence percentage. Conversely, deleting the discrepant point because it is inconvenient would hide the very evidence the comparison was meant to examine.

Important distinction · Three different checks

A hand calculation checks a numerical result. A fresh run checks the saved procedure in that environment. An evidence comparison examines a stated model against an identified record. Each supports a different part of the argument; none replaces the others.

A wider view · A photograph is also a record

The photograph has useful provenance: an identified photographer, mission, date and image number. Those details help another person find and interpret the record. They do not turn it into a position–time experiment. To estimate a velocity, we would still need positions tied to times, a spatial reference and a method for dealing with perspective. Metadata makes a source inspectable; it does not supply quantities the source never measured. Our notebook handover follows the same principle on a smaller scale.

Exercise 3.1 · Position is not a journey

P101 · Unit 3 · Suggested attempt: 4 minutes

A tracked mark is at −3 m at 7 s and at 2 m at 12 s, with rightwards positive. Find its displacement and average velocity. A report says “it travelled exactly 5 m”. Explain the additional assumption that claim needs.

Hint for Exercise 3.1

Subtract initial from final values for both position and time. Ask whether the mark might have reversed direction between readings.

Worked solution to Exercise 3.1

The displacement is 2 − (−3) = +5 m and elapsed time is 12 − 7 = 5 s. Therefore the average velocity is +1 m/s. A distance of exactly 5 m requires motion directly between the endpoints without reversals. The endpoints alone permit longer paths. They establish a distance of at least 5 m, not its exact value.

Question 1 · Quantities and units

A predicted position is formed by adding a reference position to velocity × elapsed time. Which units must the product have?

Reasoning for Question 1

Answer A. Velocity in m/s multiplied by elapsed time in s gives metres. That can be added to a position. A result with the right units can still use the wrong reference time; dimensional consistency is one check, not proof.

Question 2 · Clock reading and elapsed time

A model starts at position 2 m when the clock reads 10 s, with constant velocity +0.4 m/s. Its predicted position at 15 s is:

Reasoning for Question 2

Answer B. Elapsed time is 15 − 10 = 5 s. The predicted change is 0.4 × 5 = 2 m, giving position 2 + 2 = 4 m. Multiplying by 15 s would use the wrong time origin.

Question 3 · Distance and displacement

A point travels directly from −2 m to 3 m and directly back to −2 m in a total of 8 s. Its overall average speed is:

Reasoning for Question 3

Answer C. Each direct leg is 5 m, giving distance 10 m in 8 s and average speed 1.25 m/s. Net displacement and average velocity are zero. The stop, if any, is included in the stated total time.

Question 4 · What repeated readings establish

Repeated readings are tightly clustered, but the instrument has not been checked for a possible zero offset. Which claim is justified?

Reasoning for Question 4

Answer B. Repeatability concerns how repeated readings vary. A shared offset could shift every reading while leaving their spread small. Unit 2’s distinction between spread and accuracy remains essential when those readings become a graph.

Question 5 · Source history

You receive two identical-looking graphs. Which extra material best supports inspecting their provenance?

Reasoning for Question 5

Answer C. The identified source and explained processing connect plotted points to input rows and model choices. Appearance alone cannot supply this history. Even a complete provenance record still needs correctness and measurement checks.

Question 6 · Residual sign

The observed position is −1 m and the predicted position is −3 m. Observed minus predicted is:

Reasoning for Question 6

Answer A. Subtract the signed prediction: −1 − (−3) = +2 m. The observation lies 2 m in the positive direction from the prediction. This is a position difference, not an average velocity.

Question 7 · Constructed agreement

A line’s velocity is chosen from the first and last observations and it is anchored at the first. It agrees with the last. This agreement:

Reasoning for Question 7

Answer B. The endpoint rate was chosen precisely to connect those endpoints. Interior observations can challenge that continuation. Neither the endpoint line nor its agreement supplies the continuous travelled path.

Question 8 · Missing data

A position field is blank while its time is known. For the available-pair comparison, use:

Reasoning for Question 8

Answer C. A blank is not a measured zero and a model value is not an observation. Keep the row in the original source and omit that pair only from calculations requiring the position. Report the omitted time and the resulting limitation.

Question 9 · Model reach

Readings cover 10–18 s. A prediction at 23 s is:

Reasoning for Question 9

Answer A. The reference interval is 10–18 s; 23 s is outside it. Evaluating a formula does not extend the observational record. The system might change after the last reading.

Question 10 · Reproducibility and truth

A saved notebook runs in order in a fresh session and reproduces its output. Which conclusion follows?

Reasoning for Question 10

Answer B. The successful run supports reproducibility of that procedure in that environment. Wrong units, mistaken parameters or a poor physical model can still produce repeatable output. Check calculations and the scientific interpretation separately.

What this block has established

The unit began with a journey whose endpoint did not tell its whole story. Positions and times let us calculate changes; graphs made their relationships visible; models added explicit assumptions about what might happen between or beyond readings. Comparing predictions with observations then gave us evidence about those assumptions. Preserving the source and the calculation made that reasoning available to another reader.

Across Block 1, quantities, measurement and computation have become parts of one argument. Units constrain a formula without proving it correct. Repeated measurements reveal spread without excluding a common bias. A successful program makes a procedure repeatable without making its physical interpretation true. These distinctions are productive: they tell us what to check next and what a careful conclusion can say now.
