Field reasoning and unit synthesis

We can now move between a source’s field, the force on a probe, the work during a journey and the change of potential energy. Choosing the representation that answers the question is as important as evaluating it correctly.

Allow three hours: 15 minutes for the opening synthesis, 100 minutes attempting the cumulative exercises, 45 minutes for feedback and correction, and 20 minutes with the reference and conclusion. Full solutions remain closed until you choose to open them.

11.17 Choose a representation before calculating

A question about force at one location calls for the field and the probe’s mass or charge. A question about energy transferred between two positions may be easier through potential difference. A question giving a force as a function of position invites a work integral; one giving a force as a function of time invites an impulse calculation. The word “force” appears in all of these, but it does not determine the method on its own.

Begin by naming the source, the probe and the quantity sought. Sketch the geometry and choose a positive direction. If sources are fixed, say so; if a formula applies only outside a source, mark that boundary. In an energy calculation, state which forces do work and choose a consistent potential reference. These choices make the signs interpretable and prevent the same transfer from being counted twice.

Then look for a check that approaches the calculation from another direction. A graph’s area can check an integral. A potential difference can check conservative work. A hand-computable small partition can reveal a missing width in code. Reversing endpoints tests orientation, and changing the probe charge tests proportionality. Agreement becomes more informative when the two routes do not share the same likely mistake.

The following set mixes those decisions across the unit. Use paper first, with a calculator for numerical values. The numerical questions can be checked using the Lesson 4 notebook after the written attempt. Use ke=8.99×109Nm2C−2 throughout. Model parameters in these exercises are supplied ideal values, not observational datasets.

Exercise 11.1 · Field and probe (5 minutes)

A 0.25 kg probe experiences gravitational force 0.80 N downwards. Find the field. A second probe of mass 0.75 kg is held at the same point. Find its gravitational force and explain whether its acceleration must equal the field.

Hint for Exercise 11.1

Divide force by the first mass. For the held probe, distinguish gravitational force from resultant force.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.1

The field is 0.80/0.25=3.2Nkg−1 downwards. The second gravitational force is 0.75(3.2)=2.4N downwards. If held stationary, an upward support force balances it and acceleration is zero. The field remains nonzero.

Exercise 11.2 · Charge, units and direction (6 minutes)

A source Q=−5.0nC is at the origin and a probe q=−3.0nC at x=+0.50m. Calculate the electric field component at the probe and its force component. Predict how each changes if only the probe charge is doubled.

Hint for Exercise 11.2

Find the field direction from the source, then multiply the signed field by the signed probe charge.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.2

The negative source gives an inward, leftward field: Ex=−8.99×109(5.0×10−9)/(0.50)2=−179.8NC−1. Thus Fx=(−3.0×10−9)(−179.8)=+5.394×10−7N, rightwards, as repulsion requires. Doubling the probe charge doubles its force and leaves the source field unchanged.

Return to Exercise 11.2

Exercise 11.3 · Two sources, two sums (8 minutes)

Fixed sources +1.0nC and −4.0nC lie at x=−0.10m and x=+0.20m. Find the field and potential at the origin, taking potential zero at infinity. Does either sum vanish? Explain why the two source contributions combine differently.

Hint for Exercise 11.3

Draw both field arrows at the origin. Potential contributions use signed charge and unsquared positive distance.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.3

The positive source on the left gives +899 N/C. The negative source on the right attracts a positive probe towards the right and also gives +899 N/C. Total field is +1798 N/C. Potentials are +89.9 V and −179.8 V, total −89.9 V. Neither vanishes. Field directions determine the vector sum; potential is a signed scalar sum.

Return to Exercise 11.3

Exercise 11.4 · A nonzero starting bound (7 minutes)

A resultant force along a line is Fx=2.0N+(1.0Nm−1)x. A 2.0 kg particle moves from x=1.0m to x=3.0m, initially at 1.0 m/s. Find net work and final speed. Check the work using a trapezium on the force–position graph.

Hint for Exercise 11.4

Evaluate the antiderivative at both endpoints. The graph’s endpoint forces are 3 N and 5 N.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.4

In SI numerical values, W=[2x+x2/2]13=(6+4.5)−(2+0.5)=8.0J. The graph gives mean force 4.0 N times displacement 2.0 m, also 8.0 J. Initial kinetic energy is 1.0 J and final 9.0 J; v=2(9)/2=3.0ms−1.

Return to Exercise 11.4

Exercise 11.5 · Work with a reversal (6 minutes)

A position-dependent force is Fx=−kx, where k=5.0Nm−1. Find its work as a particle goes from 0.20 m to 0.50 m and then returns along the same line. Give the outward work, return work and round-trip total. Why would a friction force need different treatment?

Hint for Exercise 11.5

The antiderivative is −kx2/2. A velocity-dependent friction force reverses direction when motion reverses.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.5

Outward work is −(5/2)(0.502−0.202)=−0.525J. Return work is +0.525 J, total zero. The given force at each position stays the same on reversal. Sliding friction instead opposes each direction of motion; its signed model changes on the return and its work can be negative on both legs.

Return to Exercise 11.5

Exercise 11.6 · Exterior gravitational work (8 minutes)

A spherical source has GM=24m3s−2 and radius 1.0 m. A 0.40 kg probe moves from radius 2.0 m to 8.0 m. Find the gravitational work, potential difference and potential-energy change. What external work is needed for unchanged kinetic energy with no other work?

Hint for Exercise 11.6

Use the radial integral, then divide the negative of its work by the probe mass to recover the potential difference.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.6

Wg=(24)(0.40)(1/8−1/2)=−3.6J. The potential difference is 24(1/2−1/8)=+9.0Jkg−1, giving ΔU=+3.6J. The agent supplies +3.6 J for zero net work. Both radii and the full radial path are outside the source.

Return to Exercise 11.6

Exercise 11.7 · Impulse or work? (6 minutes)

A resultant force has Fx(t)=6.0N−(2.0Ns−1)t for 0 to 3.0 s. A 3.0 kg particle starts at velocity−1.0 m/s. Find impulse, final velocity and net work. Explain which result comes directly from the force–time area.

Hint for Exercise 11.7

Integrate over time for momentum change, then compare endpoint kinetic energies.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.7

Jx=[6t−t2]03=9.0Ns. Velocity change is 3.0 m/s, so final velocity is+2.0 m/s. Kinetic energy changes from 1.5 J to 6.0 J: net work 4.5 J. The 9.0 N s time-area is impulse, not work.

Return to Exercise 11.7

Exercise 11.8 · External work and a reference shift (5 minutes)

During a move, potential energy increases by 0.70 J and kinetic energy decreases by 0.20 J. Find the conservative field work and the work of other forces. Explain what changes if a constant 10 J is added to all potential-energy values.

Hint for Exercise 11.8

Use field work for −ΔU and other work for Δ(K+U).

Worked solution to Exercise 11.8

The field work is−0.70 J. Other work is +0.70−0.20=+0.50J. Their net work is−0.20 J, matching the kinetic change. Adding 10 J changes assigned potential-energy values, but neither difference nor either work changes.

Return to Exercise 11.8

Exercise 11.9 · Potential and radial field (7 minutes)

Outside a fixed source, V(r)=A/r with A=−18Vm and zero at infinity. Find the potential and radial electric-field component at r=0.30m. A positive probe moves outwards to 0.60 m. Predict whether its electric potential energy rises or falls, and check using the potential difference.

Hint for Exercise 11.9

Differentiate potential with a minus sign to obtain the radial field; keep the coefficient negative.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.9

V(0.30)=−60V. SinceEr=−dV/dr=A/r2, the field is −18/(0.30)2=−200NC−1, inward. At 0.60 m potential is−30 V, so ΔV=+30V. A positive probe gains potential energy on the outward move, consistent with moving against its inward electric force.

Return to Exercise 11.9

Exercise 11.10 · A negative charge gains energy (6 minutes)

A classical particle with charge −4.0nC and mass 2.0×10−6kg is released from rest at 20 V and reaches 50 V along an accessible path with only electric work. Find its potential-energy change, electric work and final speed. State whether the source field depends on this probe mass.

Hint for Exercise 11.10

Use the signed charge in qΔV, then obtain speed from positive kinetic energy.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.10

ΔV=+30V andΔU=−1.2×10−7J. Electric work and final kinetic energy are+1.2×10−7J. Thusv=2(1.2×10−7)/(2.0×10−6)=0.12≈0.346ms−1. The source field is independent of the probe mass under the fixed-source assumption.

Return to Exercise 11.10

Exercise 11.11 · Read a potential slope (6 minutes)

In a region V(x)=40V+(200Vm−1)x. Find Ex and the work done on a +5.0nC probe moving from x=0.10m to x=−0.10m. Check by force times displacement.

Hint for Exercise 11.11

The field is minus the potential slope. The displacement and potential difference are both final minus initial.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.11

Ex=−200NC−1. The potential difference is 200(−0.20)=−40V, giving work −qΔV=+2.0×10−7J. The force is −1.0×10−6N; multiplying by−0.20 m gives the same positive work.

Return to Exercise 11.11

Exercise 11.12 · An equipotential journey (5 minutes)

A probe moves between two positions on a sphere of radius r around one fixed point charge. Explain the electric work along the spherical surface, the potential difference, and whether the electric field must vanish there. If a separate agent guides the probe, what further information is needed to infer its kinetic-energy change?

Hint for Exercise 11.12

Potential depends on radius; field and potential difference are distinct quantities.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.12

Both positions have the same keQ/r, so ΔV=0 and electric work is zero. The radial electric field is generally nonzero and perpendicular to the surface motion. The net work of any additional forces must be known to infer ΔK; an ideal perpendicular constraint would also do zero work.

Return to Exercise 11.12

Exercise 11.13 · Magnetic evidence (5 minutes)

A compass turns near a magnet, and a beam of charged particles curves in another apparatus. Explain why the compass’s direction is not automatically the beam’s force direction. In a purely magnetic model, what happens to an individual particle’s speed? What would a measured speed increase tell you about that model?

Hint for Exercise 11.13

The compass aligns with B; the magnetic force on a point charge depends on its velocity and is perpendicular to it.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.13

The compass’s north end indicates local field direction. For a moving charge, magnetic force is perpendicular to velocity and field, with charge sign also relevant. The particle can turn at constant speed because this force does no work. A measured increase of speed requires an additional energy-transfer mechanism, such as electric work, or reassessment of the measurement; the purely magnetic point-particle model is insufficient.

Exercise 11.14 · Audit the numerical sum (7 minutes)

A program samples Fr=−8/r2 in SI units at midpoints 3 m and 5 m on the path 2 mto 6 m. It reports the sum of the two force values as work. Repair the calculation and compare with exact work. Predict how reversing the path affects both estimates.

Hint for Exercise 11.14

There are two intervals, each 2 m wide. The analytic coefficient is C=−8Nm2.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.14

The force samples are −8/9 N and −8/25 N. Multiply their sum by 2 m to obtain −544/225≈−2.41778J. Exact work is −8(1/2−1/6)=−8/3≈−2.66667J. The unweighted sum had force units. Reversing the path changes the signed widths and both work signs; midpoint magnitudes are unchanged.

Return to Exercise 11.14

Exercise 11.15 · A finite answer outside the model (5 minutes)

A numerical calculation for the exterior field of a spherical source of radius 0.40 m integrates radially from 0.20 mto 0.80 m. It avoids zero and agrees with the antiderivative to ten decimal places. Is that sufficient to accept the physical work prediction? Specify a repair and one check that remains useful.

Hint for Exercise 11.15

Check the entire physical domain before numerical accuracy.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.15

The interval includes the source interior, where the exterior inverse-square formula has not been justified. Numerical agreement tests that formula’s evaluation, not its physical applicability. Restrict the journey to exterior radii, or obtain and justify an appropriate interior model and split the calculation at the boundary. Against a valid model, analytic comparison or refinement remains useful.

Return to Exercise 11.15

Exercise 11.16 · Choose and defend a method (8 minutes)

A fixed positive source has keQ=12Vm. A +2.0nC probe is guided radially from 0.20 mto 0.60 m outside the source with unchanged kinetic energy. Find the electric work and agent’s work in two ways. Then describe a short computational check and one physical assumption it cannot establish. Would doubling probe mass change these work values?

Hint for Exercise 11.16

Use both −qΔV and the radial integral. Unchanged kinetic energy makes total work zero.

Worked solution to Exercise 11.16

Potentials are 60 V and 20 V, so ΔV=−40V. Electric work is +8.0×10−8J and agent’s work −8.0×10−8J. The integral gives C(1/a−1/b) with C=(12)(2.0×10−9)=2.4×10−8Nm2, again 8.0×10−8 J. A midpoint sum with increasing n, reversed-bounds test and SI units can check implementation/approximation. It cannot establish that the real charge distribution is pointlike or fixed. Probe mass does not enter either work for this specified charge and path. A strong answer names an actual check and separates its conclusion from a physical-model assumption.

11.19 Reference, correction and conclusion

Use the web reference or printable reference PDF for quick retrieval. The offline pack contains the reading, reference, notebook and Python companion. The reference distinguishes what to recall, reasoning to understand and details to consult; return to the full lesson if a compact expression does not remind you why it applies.

When reviewing the exercises, group difficulties by their cause. Direction errors call for a fresh source–probe sketch. Work and impulse confusion calls for checking the horizontal axis and the units of a small contribution. Potential-energy errors often begin with a missing charge sign or an unstated reference. Numerical disagreement calls for an independent small case before a larger run. Correcting the first decision that went wrong is more useful than replacing only the final number.

We began by asking what can be said about a position before a probe is placed there. A field separates the source’s spatial influence from the particular response of a mass or charge. Potential makes a corresponding separation in the energy description. Neither replaces the physical system: both become useful when the source assumptions, the probe and the domain are explicit.

Work connects those descriptions. Accumulating local force–displacement contributions extends the constant-force reasoning of Unit 6, and conservative work becomes an endpoint potential-energy difference. The numerical investigation adds a further distinction: refining a calculation can improve its approximation while leaving its physical assumptions untested. A trustworthy explanation states what each successful check supports.

In Unit 12, electric charge will move through a circuit. Potential difference will describe energy transferred per unit charge, while conservation will constrain what can happen at junctions and around a complete path. The charge, voltage and energy distinctions developed here are the foundation for that next model.
