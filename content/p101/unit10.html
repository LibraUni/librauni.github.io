Light, images and observational limits

An image seems to show us an object directly. Yet light has travelled, changed direction, passed through an aperture and produced a response in an eye or detector before we see the result. Understanding that journey lets us ask what the image reveals—and what would require a different observation.

Imagine photographing two small lamps across a room. Moving the camera changes the amount of light reaching it. Turning the focus ring changes where rays meet. Enlarging the photograph makes every feature bigger, but may still leave the lamps merged into one patch. These are different physical changes. We need a way to keep propagation, image formation and the recording process separate while explaining how they work together.

Unit 9 established wavelength, frequency, superposition and the importance of comparing an opening with a wavelength. Light belongs to the same broad wave description, although its oscillating quantities are electromagnetic fields rather than the displacement of a string. When wavelengths are small compared with the apparatus, a ray diagram gives a useful account of where light travels. Reflection and refraction then connect a physical surface to a calculable change of direction.

We will use those ideas to locate images formed by a converging lens. The aim is to connect a construction on paper with a possible observation: where to put a screen, whether the image is upright, and how its size changes. A supplied thin-lens relation makes these questions quantitative without requiring a detailed theory of electromagnetic fields.

Next we follow energy rather than individual rays. A fixed output spread across a larger area produces a lower intensity. This gives an inverse-square model and a short Python investigation with a hand-computed benchmark. Finally, we examine finite resolution, sampling and background contributions. Two records can look different because of the object, the instrument or the way the readings were taken. A defensible interpretation identifies which of these possibilities the evidence can distinguish.

Allow 18 hours, including two hours of Python in Lessons 3 and 4. Bring the graph-reading, units, ratios, trigonometry and sampling ideas from earlier P101 units. You will need a calculator with degree mode, paper for ray diagrams, and the existing Python environment with NumPy and Matplotlib. No telescope or optical apparatus is required. Activities include discussion; the final lesson contains cumulative exercises, a formative block checkpoint and the compact reference. Attempts, feedback and revision are included in the stated time.

By the end, you should be able to follow a ray through a stated interface, relate a lens calculation to an image, compare intensities under explicit assumptions, and explain a limitation of a recorded signal. These foundations support later P101 spectral evidence and A101 observational work. We begin with the connection between waves and rays.
