Question 1 · What does a motion record tell us? (15 marks)

A vehicle travels on a straight line. Its recorded positions at clock readings 08:15:00, 08:15:30 and 08:16:00 are respectively 120 m, 180 m and 150 m relative to a fixed origin. Between successive readings it moves steadily in the direction indicated, without an additional reversal. Positive position points east.

  1. Calculate the displacement and total distance travelled during the full minute. State why they differ. [4]

  2. Find the average velocity and average speed over that minute, in SI units. Explain the direction information each quantity carries. [4]

  3. A proposed expression for a position change is v+t, where v is a velocity and t a time interval. Explain why it is dimensionally unacceptable. Give a dimensionally acceptable expression for constant velocity, and explain why matching dimensions alone would not establish that the model fits the entire journey. [4]

  4. Now remove the statement about motion between readings. Which of your displacement, average velocity, total distance and average speed results remain determined by the three recorded positions? Explain what can still be said about distance. [3]

Question 2 · A more repeatable measurement? (25 marks)

A team measures the transit time of a moving marker between two fixed lines. Both methods intend to start when the marker’s leading edge crosses the first line and stop when that same edge crosses the second. Method A uses a person pressing a stopwatch. Method B uses two electronic detectors. Each display reports to 0.1 s. The motion is reset for each trial, so the physical transit time might also vary slightly. The methods were used in separate sets of runs. These invented readings are all the available evidence.

  1. For each method calculate the mean, range and mean absolute deviation from its own mean. Show enough working to make the procedure checkable; a calculator or your own code is allowed. State your rounding choice. [9]

  2. A colleague says, “B has a smaller spread, therefore it measures the true transit time more accurately.” Assess this claim. Distinguish observed repeatability, display resolution and accuracy, and give one plausible common bias that repeated readings would not reveal. Do not calculate a confidence interval or infer an uncertainty bound from the display step. [6]

  3. Plan a bounded follow-up comparison that would make the comparison of methods fairer. In about 150–200 words specify what is held fixed, how the start and stop events are defined, how runs would be paired or interleaved, what is retained in the source record, and one check directed at a possible bias. Explain what the check could and could not establish. No new experiment is required. [7]

  4. A third person proposes dropping A’s 5.2 s reading merely because it is the largest. Explain how you would handle that proposal while preserving the evidence. [3]

Question 3 · Put the clock into the model (20 marks)

A separate investigation tracks a marker along a straight rail. The clock was already running when recording began. Positions are relative to a fixed origin. The numerical records below are invented and rounded to 0.01 m; no instrument accuracy or uncertainty specification is supplied. At 49 s the position was not recorded.

Before seeing this record, the investigator proposed a constant velocity of 0.50 m s−1 and a position of 0.60 m at clock reading 40 s. Treat these as the specified model parameters, not quantities to fit. Two candidate implementations are

xA(t)=0.60m+(0.50ms−1)t

xB(t)=0.60m+(0.50ms−1)(t−40s)

Here t denotes the clock reading expressed as a time quantity in seconds.

  1. Test both implementations at 40 s. Which implements the stated model? Explain why the other can have consistent units yet fail this test. [5]

  2. Using the appropriate implementation, calculate the prediction at 45 s and the residual, defined as observed position minus predicted position. Interpret its sign. [4]

  3. Sketch a position–clock graph with labelled axes and units. Include all five measured points and the appropriate model line over 40–55 s. Make clear which marks are observations and which are predictions. Explain why drawing a line through the missing time does not create a measurement. [6]

  4. Find the model predictions at 49 s and 60 s. Classify each as interpolation or extrapolation relative to the observed time range. Explain why even an apparently good comparison within that range does not verify the later prediction. [5]

Question 4 · Build a reproducible test (30 marks)

Write an independent notebook or script that investigates the motion record in Question 3. Use Python, NumPy and Matplotlib as taught. Do not estimate new best-fit parameters. Your work should test both candidate implementations, so a reader can identify the clock-origin error from the results rather than from a claim in a comment. Your hand calculations provide separate checkpoints for the program.

  1. Load data/motion.csv relative to your working folder, preserving the original file. Check for the expected six rows and two columns and finite clock readings; report a helpful failure message if a check fails. Retain the row with the unavailable position in the raw data and explicitly report its clock reading. State which checks you have implemented and one thing they cannot establish. [7]

  2. Write your own function or functions to calculate the two predictions. Apply one common finite-position selection to times and positions for comparisons. Produce a labelled table of retained clock readings, observed positions, both predictions and both observed-minus-predicted residuals. Use a loop meaningfully to report the rows; no particular formatting library is required. [8]

  3. Make and save a readable figure with position and residual panels. Show the measured positions as discrete marks, both model lines across the observed time range, both residual series only where observations exist, and a horizontal zero residual reference. Label units and distinguish the series by labels and line styles or markers as well as colour. The difference in scales may obscure small residuals: add a separately labelled zoom or an additional plot if useful, retaining the full comparison. [8]

  4. Compare at least two numerical program results with independently worked checks from Question 3; explain any discrepancy. Restart and run the notebook from the first cell, or run the script in a fresh process. Repeat from a second folder containing the same relative file layout. Report the actual outcomes, Python/NumPy/Matplotlib versions and where the saved plot is written. If a check fails, retain the failure and explain the remaining problem. [7]

A notebook should contain the executed outputs. With a script, include the saved figure and a text capture of the printed output. Your written explanation may be in notebook text cells or in the answer document; cross-reference it so it is marked once.

How your work will be marked

The bracketed allocations add to 100. Credit is awarded for correct methods, interpretable evidence and reasoned limitations, not merely matching final answers. A carried-forward arithmetic error should not be penalised repeatedly when later work is internally correct. A physically unsupported conclusion does not become correct because the program ran. A failed reproducibility check reported honestly can earn diagnostic credit; full reproducibility credit requires a successful, evidenced rerun.

  • Question 1, 15: quantities and paths (4), rates and direction (4), dimensions and model conditions (4), limits of discrete observations (3).
  • Question 2, 25: six summaries with visible method (9), measurement judgement (6), a feasible controlled comparison (7), evidence-preserving treatment of a suspect reading (3).
  • Question 3, 20: reference-time test (5), numerical residual and sign (4), graph and distinction between data and predictions (6), prediction range and limitations (5).
  • Question 4, 30: loading and validation (7), reusable calculations and aligned rows (8), accurate and readable figures (8), independent checks and reproducibility (7).
  • Question 5, 10: proportionate evidence-based conclusion (6), traceable source and runnable handover (4).

Feedback should identify strengths, specific corrections and the next independent step.
