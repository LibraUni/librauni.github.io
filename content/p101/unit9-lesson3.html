Standing patterns

When one of two equal waves reverses direction, their relative phase changes from place to place. The sum can have a shape that grows, flattens and reverses without travelling along the medium. Its fixed quiet points give us a way to connect superposition to the ends of a vibrating string.

Allow four hours: 75 minutes reading, 55 minutes written activities, 60 minutes Python and 50 minutes feedback and correction.

9.9 Two directions, one standing pattern

We will choose sine profiles because they lead conveniently to a fixed zero at the origin. Sine and cosine describe the same family of sinusoidal shapes with different initial phases: sinθ=cos(θ−π/2). Changing to sine introduces no new kind of wave. Take equal amplitudes A, common wavenumber k and angular frequency ω, with

y1=Asin(kx−ωt),y2=Asin(kx+ωt).

The first moves right and the second left. Applying the supplied identity sin(u−v)+sin(u+v)=2sinucosv, with u=kx and v=ωt, gives

y(x,t)=2Asin(kx)cos(ωt).

This form separates the dependence on position from the dependence on time. At a chosen position, 2Asin(kx) is a fixed coefficient, and cos(ωt) supplies the oscillation. At a chosen time, every point has the same time multiplier. As this multiplier changes sign, the whole profile reverses rather than translating. This is a standing wave.

The snapshots show one complete spatial wavelength of this standing pattern. Initially, the two lobes have opposite signs. After a quarter-period, every displacement is zero. After half a period, the lobes are reversed. The same positions divide the lobes throughout. This differs from a travelling profile, whose crests move to new positions.

Within one lobe, all points have the same sign of sin(kx), so they oscillate in phase, though with different amplitudes. Points in neighbouring lobes have opposite signs of that factor and oscillate in opposite phase. The constituent waves still have propagation speed fλ; the stationary envelope of their sum has no advancing crest to track in that way.

Example 9.6 · Read a standing-wave point

Let each travelling component have amplitude 2 mm, wavelength 1.20 m and frequency 5 Hz. Find the displacement at x=0.30m at times 0, 0.05 and 0.10 s.

Here k=2π/1.20radm−1, so kx=π/2 and the spatial coefficient is 4 mm. The period is 0.20 s. The three times are zero, one quarter-period and one half-period:

y=4cos(10πt)mm=+4, 0, −4mm,

where the numerical expression uses t in seconds. This point oscillates with amplitude 4 mm. At the same times, the point x=0.90m has displacements −4, 0 and +4 mm because its spatial sine factor is −1.

9.10 Nodes, antinodes and zero snapshots

A node is a position whose disturbance remains zero throughout the motion. For our standing wave, this happens when the spatial factor vanishes. Since sine is zero at integer multiples of π,

kx=nπ,x=nπk=nλ2,

for integer n. Adjacent nodes are therefore half a wavelength apart. The position halfway between neighbouring nodes has the greatest local amplitude, 2A, and is an antinode. Its location is

x=(2n+1)λ4.

The local amplitude at any position is 2A|sin(kx)|. This nonnegative quantity describes the maximum displacement magnitude reached over time. Its zeros identify nodes; its maxima identify antinodes. The sign of sin(kx) still matters when describing phase, even though it is removed in the amplitude.

Key idea 9.3 · Zero once or zero always?

A node stays at zero for all times. When cos(ωt)=0, the entire ideal standing wave passes through zero displacement, including its antinodes. That instant does not turn every position into a node.

The zero snapshot is a particularly useful warning about inference from one image. At an antinode, zero displacement is the centre of the oscillation, where the point is moving through equilibrium. Energy can be stored in that motion. In the ideal equal-wave standing pattern, the opposite travelling components carry equal and opposite average energy flows; there is no net time-averaged energy transport along the string. Energy can still move locally and exchange between kinetic and elastic forms during each cycle. We do not need an energy-density formula to distinguish “zero displacement” from “no motion or energy”.

9.11 Fitting a fixed-end string

A string fixed at x=0 and x=L must have zero displacement at both ends. Our sine form already satisfies the first condition. To satisfy the second for all times, we require sin(kL)=0. Hence kL=nπ. A nonzero vibrating pattern uses a positive integer n; n=0 gives zero displacement everywhere in this family.

Substituting k=2π/λ gives the permitted wavelengths and their frequencies:

L=nλn2,λn=2Ln.
fn=vwλn=nvw2L,n=1,2,3,…

The integer n counts the half-wavelength lobes along the string. The longest allowed wavelength is 2L, with one lobe and no interior node. It gives the lowest frequency, called the fundamental. For this ideal uniform string with a common wave speed, the permitted frequencies are integer multiples of the fundamental: its harmonics. We call n=1 the first harmonic, n=2 the second, and so on.

These are possible mode shapes, each a pattern satisfying the same endpoints. An initial disturbance can excite several modes, and a driven string responds according to the driving frequency, location and losses. The boundary calculation determines which ideal patterns fit; it does not determine how strongly a particular experiment excites them. That distinction connects this lesson to resonance in Unit 8.

Example 9.7 · Infer a mode from its pattern

A uniform string of length 0.75 m has fixed ends and a supplied wave speed of 60 m/s. A pattern has three antinodes. Find its mode number, wavelength, frequency and interior node positions.

Three antinodes mean three half-wavelength lobes, so n=3. Then

λ3=2(0.75m)3=0.50m,f3=60ms−10.50m=120Hz.

Adjacent nodes are 0.25 m apart. The nodes are 0, 0.25, 0.50 and 0.75 m, of which the middle two are interior. The fundamental is 40 Hz, so 120 Hz is also 3f1, an independent arithmetic check.

Discussion of Activity 9.10

For n=2: 80 Hz, 0.75 m, three nodes including one interior node. For n=4: 160 Hz, 0.375 m, five nodes including three interior nodes. There are n+1 nodes including both ends and n−1 interior nodes. More lobes fit by reducing wavelength; at fixed vw=fλ, halving wavelength doubles frequency. This reasoning uses an unchanged string and the same supplied propagation speed.

9.12 Test the boundary conditions

We can calculate a standing pattern in two ways: add its opposite travelling components, or evaluate the separated product. Use the same phase convention in both. Our cosine function needs a phase of −π/2 to reproduce each sine component.

A, wavelength, frequency = 0.002, 1.20, 5.0
x = np.linspace(0.0, 1.20, 241)
t = 0.03
right = travelling(x, t, A, wavelength, frequency, phase=-np.pi/2)
left = travelling(x, t, A, wavelength, frequency,
                  phase=-np.pi/2, direction=-1)
summed = right + left
product = (2*A*np.sin(2*np.pi*x/wavelength)
           * np.cos(2*np.pi*frequency*t))
print(np.max(np.abs(summed - product)))

nodes = np.array([0.0, 0.60, 1.20])
for t in (0.0, 0.03, 0.05, 0.10):
    y_nodes = (2*A*np.sin(2*np.pi*nodes/wavelength)
               * np.cos(2*np.pi*frequency*t))
    assert np.allclose(y_nodes, 0.0, rtol=0, atol=1e-12)

The node test uses several times, including times when antinodes are not zero. Testing only at a zero snapshot would allow many wrong node predictions to pass. An even stronger check tests a predicted antinode as well, so a faulty function returning zero everywhere cannot appear successful.
