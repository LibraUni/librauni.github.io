P101 · Block 2 · Unit 7

Thermal transfers and testing a motion model

A sliding object comes to rest. Its kinetic energy decreases, but the energy has not vanished. Some may now be held in the microscopic motion and interactions of the object and its surroundings. How could we describe that change, and what observations would test our account?

Unit 6 taught us to draw a system boundary and follow energy through a process. We now give the receiving side of that account more detail. A thermometer can tell us that a body has become warmer, but its reading is not an amount of energy. The same energy transfer can produce quite different temperature changes in different bodies. To make a useful prediction we need to know what the system contains, how energy crosses its boundary and how its temperature responds.

The first two lessons develop this distinction between a state and a transfer. You will interpret temperature, heat and internal energy, use specific heat to connect an energy change to a temperature change, and build balances for a body or a pair of bodies. An idealised microscopic picture helps explain why a warm body can contain internal energy even while it rests on a table.

We then return to motion. In earlier P101 units you compared positions at separated times and used constant-acceleration relations. M101 Unit 5 now supplies a more general mathematical language: a derivative describes the local rate of change of a function. We will apply it to position and velocity, check the resulting predictions by hand and in Python, and distinguish an exact derivative of a model from an estimate made using separated readings.

The final investigation asks a practical question: when two proposed descriptions disagree, what comparison would help us choose? A documented historical stopping-distance record lets us compare predictions with observations, inspect residuals and ask what missing experimental details prevent us from concluding. You will produce a short reproducible comparison and a conclusion whose scope matches its evidence. The same habit underlies the thermal and motion work: make the model explicit, find an independently checkable prediction, and examine what a disagreement tells you.

Preparation and study route

Bring the system and energy reasoning from P101 Unit 6, the measurement distinctions from Unit 2 and the tables, residuals and Python methods from Unit 3. Before Lesson 3, work through M101 Unit 5, especially polynomial differentiation and the derivative as a local rate. The first two lessons need algebra but no calculus.

The unit has 18 study hours, including three hours of Python. Lesson 1 takes three hours; Lesson 2 four; Lesson 3 three; Lesson 4 four; and Lesson 5 four. These allowances include reading, attempts, feedback and correction. The closing lesson brings together a cumulative exercise set, the formative iCMA 02 and a printable reference. Keep your own explanations and corrected calculations alongside the notebooks: they will prepare you for the separate Block 2 assessment, TMA 02.

Begin with a question that sounds simple until we try to measure it: what does it mean for two objects to have the same temperature?
