10.17 From oscillation to evidence

Block 3 began with a repeating motion. Period and phase let us describe what happened at one position. A travelling wave added spatial change: wavelength described a snapshot while frequency described the record at a fixed point. Superposition then connected several contributions to one observed displacement, making phase and boundary conditions essential.

Light extends that chain. Its wavelength helps determine when a ray construction is useful and when diffraction matters. Refraction redirects rays and permits image formation. Intensity describes a power budget over area, while a detector records a response that depends on exposure and background. The final record therefore contains information about both the source and the observing process.

When solving a mixed question, first identify the quantity sought. A direction calls for an angle convention and a boundary law. An image position calls for a ray construction and signed lens distances. Received energy needs intensity, area and exposure. A claim about an unobserved interval requires attention to sampling and alternative histories. Choosing the relevant representation is part of the physics.

Spend the synthesis allocation annotating one result from your notebook: identify the physical assumption, numerical operation, independent check and remaining limitation. Then attempt the exercises without opening their solutions. During correction, locate the first incorrect decision, repair it, and retry the affected part before moving on.

Exercise 10.1 · Spectrum and a boundary (4 minutes)

Light has vacuum wavelength 450 nm. Find its frequency using c=3.00×108ms−1. It enters index 1.25 material: find speed and wavelength, and state what happens to frequency.

Hint for Exercise 10.1

Use the vacuum wavelength to establish frequency first.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.1

f=c/λ0=6.67×1014Hz. In the material, v=c/1.25=2.40×108ms−1, and λ=450/1.25=360nm. Frequency is unchanged at the stationary boundary. The shorter wavelength compensates for the smaller speed.

Exercise 10.2 · An angle convention (5 minutes)

A ray arrives at a horizontal mirror at 25° to its surface, travelling down and right. Draw incident and reflected arrows, the normal and both normal angles. Explain why 65° and 25° can both correctly describe the incident path.

Hint for Exercise 10.2

A surface and its normal are perpendicular.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.2

The incident and reflected normal angles are 65°. The reflected ray travels up and right, 25° above the surface. The two numbers use different reference lines; quoting an angle without its reference makes the description ambiguous.

Return to Exercise 10.2

Exercise 10.3 · Refraction and reversal (6 minutes)

Light passes from index 1.00 to 1.40 at a 40° normal angle. Find the transmitted angle and the wavelength ratio. Reverse the path and check the original angle.

Hint for Exercise 10.3

Isolate the sine, then use inverse sine in degree mode.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.3

θ2=sin−1(sin40∘/1.40)≈27.3∘. The wavelength ratio is λ2/λ1=1/1.40≈0.714, since frequency stays fixed. Reversing gives sinθ1=1.40sinθ2, recovering 40° using the unrounded result. The outward ray bends away from the normal.

Return to Exercise 10.3

Exercise 10.4 · A missing transmitted ray (6 minutes)

For a boundary from index 1.50 to 1.00, compare incidence angles 30° and 50°. Find any transmitted angle and the critical angle. Explain a calculator domain failure physically.

Hint for Exercise 10.4

Check whether the required sine exceeds one.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.4

At 30°, the transmitted sine is 1.50sin30∘=0.75, so the angle is 48.6°. The critical angle is sin−1(1/1.50)=41.8∘. At 50°, the required sine is about 1.149, so there is no propagating transmitted ray in this model; total internal reflection occurs. A real angle cannot have sine above one.

Return to Exercise 10.4

Exercise 10.5 · Locate and draw an image (5 minutes)

A 2 cm upright object is 36 cm from a converging lens of focal length 12 cm. Find image distance, magnification and signed height. Sketch two rays from the object tip and locate a screen.

Hint for Exercise 10.5

Find the reciprocal image distance before inverting.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.5

1/di=1/12−1/36=1/18 in inverse centimetres, so di=18cm. Then m=−18/36=−0.50, and hi=−1cm. The rays meet below the axis 18 cm beyond the lens; a screen there receives the real inverted image.

Return to Exercise 10.5

Exercise 10.6 · Choose a magnifier position (6 minutes)

A converging lens has focal length 12 cm. Where must a real object be placed to give an upright image three times its height? Find the image distance and explain how it can be seen.

Hint for Exercise 10.6

Use m=−di/do=+3 in the lens equation.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.6

di=−3do, so 1/12=1/do−1/(3do)=2/(3do). Thus do=8cm and di=−24cm. Outgoing rays diverge as though from that upright virtual image. An eye can collect them; a screen cannot intercept a focused outgoing image at the backwards intersection.

Return to Exercise 10.6

Exercise 10.7 · At and far from the focus (4 minutes)

For a converging lens, explain the image-distance behaviour when a real object is exactly at the focus and when it is extremely far away. Why is zero not the image distance in the first case?

Hint for Exercise 10.7

Examine 1/di=1/f−1/do.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.7

At do=f, the reciprocal image distance is zero; rays from a point leave parallel and there is no finite image plane. Zero distance would require an undefined reciprocal, not zero. For a very distant object, 1/do is small and di approaches f.

Return to Exercise 10.7

Exercise 10.8 · A detector budget (6 minutes)

A 24 W isotropic source illuminates a perpendicular 3 cm² detector 4 m away. Find intensity, incident power and energy in 2 s. State the geometrical and transmission assumptions.

Hint for Exercise 10.8

Convert square centimetres to square metres.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.8

I=24/(4π42)=3/(8π)≈0.119Wm−2. With A=3×10−4m2, received power is 3.58×10−5W and energy 7.16×10−5J. The source is small relative to 4 m, steady and isotropic; losses are negligible and intensity nearly uniform over the small perpendicular detector.

Return to Exercise 10.8

Exercise 10.9 · Background before ratios (5 minutes)

A detector gives 10 units at 2 m and 4 units at 4 m. A matched source-off measurement gives 2 units. Compare raw and corrected ratios. Is the result consistent with inverse-square spreading?

Hint for Exercise 10.9

Subtract the background from both readings.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.9

The raw ratio is 0.4. Corrected source readings are 8 and 2 units, giving 0.25, consistent with doubled distance for an unchanged isotropic source. These two readings do not establish the law across all distances; background stability and linear comparable response are needed.

Return to Exercise 10.9

Exercise 10.10 · An exposure ambiguity (5 minutes)

Equal quarters of one exposure have intensities 1, 3, 3 and 1 units. Find its mean. Give a different time history with the same mean and propose an observation that distinguishes them.

Hint for Exercise 10.10

An average preserves a total but not the order of contributions.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.10

The mean is 2 units. A steady 2-unit source or the ordering 3, 1, 1, 3 has the same mean. Shorter exposures within the interval could distinguish these histories, provided their precision and cadence resolve the changes. The original full-exposure value alone cannot do so.

Return to Exercise 10.10

Exercise 10.11 · Sampling a hidden oscillation (5 minutes)

For R(t)=50+2sin(2πt/6), with time in seconds, find values at 0, 3, 6 and 9 s. Then evaluate 1.5 and 4.5 s. Explain why the first record is insufficient to claim a steady source.

Hint for Exercise 10.11

The integer multiples of π have zero sine.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.11

The first four values are all 50. At 1.5 s the value is 52; at 4.5 s it is 48. A 3 s cadence at the original phase misses the variation. A steady 50-unit source and this 6 s oscillation fit those four samples; shifted or denser observations can distinguish them.

Return to Exercise 10.11

Exercise 10.12 · Which improvement? (6 minutes)

Two nearby marks appear as one broad patch. Compare digital enlargement, finer pixels, refocusing and increasing aperture. For each, state which limitation it might address and why it need not solve every case.

Hint for Exercise 10.12

Distinguish stored display, spatial sampling, defocus and diffraction.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.12

Digital enlargement changes display size only. Finer pixels can sample an already sharp image more faithfully but cannot remove optical blur. Refocusing can remove defocus. A larger aperture can reduce diffraction at fixed wavelength, but atmospheric or lens imperfections may still dominate. A strong answer identifies the cause before selecting a change.

Return to Exercise 10.12

Exercise 10.13 · Repair a numerical model (6 minutes)

A program uses power/(4*np.pi*r), plots it from 0 to 5 m and reports a smooth curve as validation. Identify the exponent and domain errors. Give one absolute benchmark and one scaling test for a 12 W source.

Hint for Exercise 10.13

Use the sphere area and exclude the point-source origin.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.13

The denominator needs r**2; zero distance is outside the model and creates a singularity. At 2 m the value should be 3/(4π)≈0.2387Wm−2. Doubling distance should divide intensity by 4. A smooth curve can arise from the wrong formula; measured evidence is still needed to test the physical approximation.

Return to Exercise 10.13

Exercise 10.14 · An evidence-based comparison (6 minutes)

A second image looks larger and has twice the total detector count. Its exposure is twice as long, and the saved image was digitally enlarged. Write a short assessment of the claim “the object grew and became twice as bright”. Include what is known, what is missing and one useful follow-up.

Hint for Exercise 10.14

Track each reported change through the optical and recording chain.

Worked solution to Exercise 10.14

Digital enlargement explains a larger display without object growth. A doubled exposure can explain doubled source counts for unchanged brightness in a linear unsaturated detector. Background, response settings, optical geometry and the definition of the counted region must be checked. Compare matched exposures/settings with background correction and an unchanged image scale; report a physical change only if those controlled records support it. Alternative sound follow-ups are acceptable when they separate the competing explanations.

iCMA03 · Question 1

A sinusoidal motion has period 0.20 s. Its frequency is

Explanation for Question 1

Frequency is cycles per second: 1/0.20=5Hz. Period and frequency are reciprocals.

iCMA03 · Question 2

A travelling wave has frequency 3 Hz and wavelength 2 m. Its speed is

Explanation for Question 2

Use v=fλ=3×2=6ms−1. Spatial wavelength and temporal period describe different graph axes.

iCMA03 · Question 3

Two equal-amplitude coherent waves arrive at opposite phase at a point. Their displacement sum is

Explanation for Question 3

At opposite phase their signed contributions cancel pointwise in the linear model. Unequal amplitudes would leave a residual.

iCMA03 · Question 4

A fixed-end standing wave has adjacent nodes 0.30 m apart. Its wavelength is

Explanation for Question 4

Adjacent nodes are half a wavelength apart, so λ=0.60m. A zero snapshot everywhere is not evidence that every point is a node.

Explanation for Question 5

Frequency stays fixed; speed and wavelength decrease. Snell’s law requires a smaller angle to the normal.

iCMA03 · Question 6

Explanation for Question 6

Outgoing rays diverge and their backwards extensions meet on the object side. The image distance is negative in our convention.

10.20 Reference and conclusion

Use the web reference or printable reference PDF to identify what to recall, what reasoning to understand, and what to consult. Return to the worked teaching when a compact formula does not remind you why its conditions apply. The offline pack includes the reading, notebook, Python companion and reference.

Our opening photograph showed that an image is made by light’s journey. We can now describe that journey in stages: wave propagation, reflection or refraction, the meeting or apparent origin of rays, and the distribution of power across a collecting area. Image position, size and intensity answer different questions, even when they appear together in one observation.

The last stage is the record itself. Finite image width, exposure and sampling limit the distinctions it preserves. A convincing interpretation therefore combines a physical model with checks on the observing process. It can explain both what the evidence supports and which comparison would resolve a remaining ambiguity.

This completes Block 3’s progression from repeated motion to recorded light. Later P101 work will use light as evidence about physical systems, and A101 will build more detailed observational methods on these foundations. The next P101 block turns to fields and potentials, using the earlier mechanics and the required mathematical preparation. Keep the habit developed here: define the quantity, choose the model, test its conditions and interpret the result.
