TMA 03 · Patterns, light and the limits of a record

A repeating pattern can be described by a formula, but an observation records only selected aspects of it. This assignment asks you to connect those descriptions: follow an oscillator through its cycle, test a standing-wave account, trace light and its energy, and decide whether a sampled record distinguishes two possible histories. Your reasoning should explain both the prediction and the evidence needed to trust it.

Complete this assessment after Units 8–10 and formative iCMA03. There are five questions and 100 marks, within six separate assessment hours. Assessment version 1.0 · 27 October 2026.

Question 1 · Motion, energy and driving 16 marks · about 45 minutes

A 0.200 kg trolley moves on a horizontal track. Displacement x is measured to the right from equilibrium. Its initial ideal motion is

x(t)=(0.0300m)cos[(4πs−1)t+π3].

The spring is linear and has negligible moving mass. For parts (a) and (b), neglect damping and other horizontal forces. For x=Acos(ωt+ϕ), you may use v=−Aωsin(ωt+ϕ), a=−ω2x and k=mω2.

  1. Identify the amplitude, angular frequency, period and frequency. Find the first time after t=0 at which the trolley reaches its positive extreme. Show how you select the appropriate phase, rather than reporting only a calculator value. [4]

  2. Calculate displacement, velocity and acceleration at t=0, and the spring stiffness. Describe the initial direction of motion and of the restoring force. Explain why the signs of displacement and velocity need not agree. [4]

  3. Now consider a freely decaying run with the same spring. Two positive turning displacements are 0.0300 m and, later, 0.0180 m. Calculate the mechanical energy at each turning point, the energy that has left the oscillatory mechanical account and the fraction remaining. Explain why the peak heights alone do not determine how long that loss took. [4]

  4. In a driven test, a larger steady displacement is recorded at 2.0 Hz than at 1.5 Hz, but the applied force amplitude was also doubled at 2.0 Hz. Assess the claim that this comparison locates the resonant peak. Propose a controlled frequency comparison and explain the energy balance that allows a damped oscillator to maintain a steady amplitude. About 100–150 words is sufficient. [4]

Question 2 · What makes a point a node? 22 marks · about 60 minutes

On a uniform string, x measures distance along the equilibrium line and y measures transverse displacement upwards. Two ideal small-amplitude contributions are

y1(x,t)=Asin(kx−ωt),
y2(x,t)=Asin(kx+ωt),

where A=0.00200m, k=(5π/2)m−1 and ω=15πs−1. Superposition applies. The proposed string has fixed ends at x=0 and x=L=1.20m. You may use

sinu+sinv=2sin(u+v2)cos(u−v2).

  1. Determine the wavelength, period, frequency and propagation speed of each contribution. Establish their propagation directions by following a constant phase. Distinguish that propagation from the motion of a marked element of string. [5]

  2. Obtain a product expression for y1+y2. Test both fixed ends for all times. List every node and antinode position on the stated length, identify the harmonic number and give the largest displacement magnitude at an antinode. Sketch the profile at t=0, with positions and displacement scale labelled. [6]

  3. A program tests the proposed node x=0.30m only at t=1/30s and finds zero. Decide whether this is a node by evaluating the same point at t=0 and explaining what happens along the whole string at the first test time. Give a genuine node test and a nonzero test that together would reject a faulty program returning zero everywhere. Numerical test expectations must follow from your reasoning. [5]

  4. A revised report keeps y1 unchanged but replaces the second amplitude by 0.00150m, with its other parameters unchanged. Derive the resultant at x=0 and find its maximum magnitude over a cycle. Is that revised two-wave account compatible with the fixed end? Explain why an instantaneous zero or a smaller return wave is insufficient to establish the proposed fixed-end standing pattern. [6]

Question 3 · Where does the light go? 20 marks · about 55 minutes

Use c=3.00×108ms−1. In the first arrangement, monochromatic light of vacuum wavelength 600 nm travels in a transparent material of refractive index 1.40 towards a flat boundary with air, taken as index 1.00. The incident ray makes 55° with the boundary surface. The interface is stationary, and the indices apply at the stated wavelength.

  1. Draw the normal, reflected ray and any transmitted ray, marking their angles to the normal and directions of travel. Calculate the reflected and transmitted angles and explain the direction of bending. Your sketch should make clear which region contains each ray. [5]

  2. Calculate the frequency, speed and wavelength in the material, and state their values in air. The incident direction is then changed to 40° with the surface. Determine whether a propagating transmitted ray is possible, using a numerical test; interpret the result physically. [5]

  3. Use the thin, paraxial lens model of Unit 10. In a separate arrangement wholly in air, a converging lens of focal length 8.0 cm views an upright 1.5 cm object 24 cm in front of it. Use the Unit 10 sign convention to find image distance, magnification and signed image height. Draw two rays from the object tip on a labelled diagram and show where a screen could receive a focused image. Explain what makes this image real. [6]

  4. Keep the lens but move the object to 6.0 cm in front of it. Find image distance and magnification. Explain how the image is seen and why moving a screen to the numerical image location does not give the same observation. Use the directions of outgoing rays or their backwards extensions in your explanation. [4]

Question 4 · Light collected and detail preserved 16 marks · about 40 minutes

A compact steady source emits 18.0 W isotropically. A small detector of collecting area 4.00cm2 is 3.00 m from the source, perpendicular to the arriving rays. Neglect absorption and obstruction, and treat intensity as uniform over the detector. Exposure duration is 0.250 s. The source is small compared with the distances used.

  1. Calculate intensity at the detector, incident power on it and incident energy during the exposure. Show the area conversion and distinguish the three quantities by their units and meaning. Explain why the total power through a complete enclosing sphere differs from the power intercepted by this detector. [6]

  2. A second observation doubles the distance, doubles the collecting area and quadruples the exposure duration. Predict the second-to-first ratio of incident energy. The recorded source count above matched background doubles. Under a linear unsaturated response with unchanged conversion from incident energy to count, does this establish a brighter source? Support your answer with the separate geometric and exposure factors. [5]

  3. In a separate imaging observation, two nearby illuminated marks appear as one broad patch even after the best focus is found. Compare three proposed changes: enlarging the stored display, using finer pixels and increasing aperture at fixed wavelength. Explain which limitation each could address and why the available record does not identify a single guaranteed remedy. State one useful comparison to diagnose the limiting factor. About 120–170 words is sufficient; no quantitative diffraction formula is required. [5]

Question 5 · Can the record distinguish two periods? 26 marks · about 110 minutes

A target channel records light from a varying source together with background. A simultaneous control channel records that same additive background on the same linear response scale. In this stipulated model, subtract the control from the raw target channel to obtain the corrected source record. Samples are treated as instantaneous and unsaturated. For a real instrument the matching of channels and the finite exposure would need checking.

RA(t)=40+5cos(πt2),
