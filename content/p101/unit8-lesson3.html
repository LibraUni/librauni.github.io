Why real oscillations change

The ideal trolley repeats its motion indefinitely. A released real trolley usually settles, while a driven object can continue vibrating. Following energy into, within and out of the oscillation explains why these situations differ.

Allow three hours: 65 minutes for reading and examples, 75 minutes for activities and 40 minutes for feedback and correction.

8.9 Energy through a cycle

Choose the trolley and spring together as the system. While the trolley moves towards equilibrium, the spring’s deformation decreases and the trolley speeds up. Near an extreme, the trolley slows while the spring becomes more deformed. The motion continually exchanges kinetic and elastic potential energy.

For an ideal linear spring, take elastic potential energy to be zero at its undeformed equilibrium. The supplied energy relation is U=12kx2. The square makes the energy positive for both extension and compression. The trolley’s kinetic energy is K=12mv2, as in Unit 6. For the horizontal undamped system with a fixed support,

E=K+U=12mv2+12kx2.

At either turning point, v=0 and |x|=A, giving E=12kA2. At equilibrium, x=0, so all this mechanical energy is kinetic and 12mvmax2=12kA2. Hence vmax=Ak/m=Aω, agreeing with the motion formula from Lesson 2. Two different descriptions have produced the same check.

At an intermediate position, energy conservation gives

v2=km(A2−x2).

This determines speed after taking a nonnegative square root. The direction of velocity still depends on which part of the cycle the trolley occupies. It passes most positions twice per cycle, with opposite velocities but the same kinetic energy.

Example 8.8 · A speed with two possible directions

An ideal spring has k=50N/m, and its trolley has mass 0.50 kg. Amplitude is 0.040 m. Find the total mechanical energy and the speed at x=0.020m.

At an extreme, E=12(50)(0.040)2=0.040J. At the specified position, U=12(50)(0.020)2=0.010J, leaving K=0.030J. Therefore

|v|=2Km=2(0.030J)0.50kg≈0.346m/s.

Velocity is negative on the journey from the positive extreme towards equilibrium, and positive on the return. At half-amplitude displacement, potential energy is one quarter of the total; it is not half, because energy depends on displacement squared.

Activity 8.9 · Read an energy account (25 minutes)

For Example 8.8, calculate kinetic and potential energy at x=0, x=−0.020m and x=−0.040m. Find the maximum speed. If amplitude is halved with mass and stiffness unchanged, by what factors do total energy, maximum speed and period change? Explain the different factors.

Discussion of Activity 8.9

The energy pairs (K,U) in joules are (0.040,0), (0.030,0.010) and (0,0.040). Maximum speed is 2(0.040)/0.50=0.40m/s. Halving amplitude reduces energy by a factor of four, maximum speed by a factor of two and leaves the ideal period unchanged. Energy depends on A2, speed on A, and period on m/k.

8.10 Damping a free oscillation

In a real apparatus, air resistance, friction and deformation within materials can transfer energy out of the organised mechanical motion. The oscillation then becomes smaller. We call this loss of oscillatory mechanical energy damping. The complete energy account still balances: energy may become internal energy in the moving system or be transferred to its surroundings.

A lightly damped free oscillation can retain an approximately steady spacing between cycles while its successive peaks decrease. Amplitude and period are separate features, so a shrinking height does not by itself mean a longer period. Stronger damping can change the timescale appreciably. With sufficiently strong damping, a displaced system can return towards equilibrium without repeated crossings. Such a response cannot be assigned an oscillation period merely because it moves.

For a particular model with a damping force proportional to and opposite velocity, a lightly damped displacement has an exponentially decreasing envelope. The envelope is a pair of curves that bounds the oscillation. We will use the shape as a qualitative illustration here; you do not need an exponential-decay fitting method or a damped differential-equation solution.

To compare two free decays fairly, keep the starting displacement and release method the same, and record both the peak sizes and their times. A record of smaller later peaks supports loss of mechanical oscillation energy. It does not identify which physical mechanism caused it without additional evidence. Changing air resistance while keeping the contact arrangement fixed could help distinguish mechanisms.

Example 8.9 · What two turning points tell us

A spring with k=80N/m has positive turning displacements 0.050 m and, later, 0.030 m. The trolley is momentarily at rest at both. How much mechanical energy has left the oscillation between them?

At each turning point kinetic energy vanishes. The change in elastic energy is

ΔE=12k(0.0302−0.0502)m2=−0.064J.

The initial and final energies are 0.100 J and 0.036 J, so 0.064 J has left the oscillatory mechanical account. This does not say how much became internal energy in the spring rather than in its surroundings. The broader boundary chosen in Unit 7 would determine where those transfers appear.

Activity 8.10 · Size and spacing (25 minutes)

An invented free-decay record has positive peak times 0.0, 1.2, 2.4 and 3.6 s, with turning displacements 8.0, 6.4, 5.1 and 4.1 mm from a fixed equilibrium. Estimate its period and frequency. For the same linear spring, estimate the fraction of initial mechanical energy remaining at the final peak. Would doubling all the time intervals change that fraction? Give one useful follow-up measurement.

Discussion of Activity 8.10

The observed peak intervals are all 1.2 s, giving approximately 0.833 Hz. At turning points energy is proportional to squared displacement, so the fraction is (4.1/8.0)2≈0.263, or about 26%. Doubling the times doubles the period and halves frequency but does not change the energy ratio obtained from the endpoint displacements. It would change the average rate of energy loss. A useful follow-up could repeat the release with reduced air drag while preserving mass, spring and starting amplitude, then compare peak sequences. The record is invented for reasoning, not evidence about an actual device.

8.11 Driving and resonance

A free oscillation follows an initial release. A driven oscillation receives a continuing external input. For example, a motor can apply a repeated force to a spring-supported platform. The driver has its own frequency, and changing that frequency can change the size and phase of the platform’s response.

The natural frequency of the ideal undamped spring system is f0=(1/2π)k/m. It belongs to the system’s mass and restoring interaction. The driving frequency belongs to the imposed force. They need not be equal. After the initial transient has died away, a linearly driven, damped system responds at the driving frequency, with an amplitude that depends on the frequency, force amplitude and damping.

To see why timing matters, consider work over a cycle. A force acting in the same direction as velocity transfers energy into the motion; a force opposing velocity removes it. The relative timing of force and motion determines the net transfer. Near a system’s resonant response, a fixed-amplitude periodic force can produce a much larger motion than at frequencies well away from it.

Resonance refers here to a strong response over a range of driving frequencies. For a lightly damped spring oscillator, the displacement response peaks near its undamped natural frequency. Damping limits the peak and broadens the range of appreciable response. The exact frequency of the displacement maximum depends on damping, so we use “near” rather than imposing exact equality for every system.

Key idea 8.3 · Sustained motion needs an energy account

During a steady driven response, the energy supplied per cycle balances the energy removed by damping. A large steady amplitude is compatible with continuing energy loss. During growth or decay, the oscillation’s stored mechanical energy changes.

In the ideal model with no damping and an exactly resonant continuing force, amplitude can grow rather than settle to a fixed steady value. A real apparatus eventually encounters damping, nonlinear response or other limits. A controlled test therefore records the applied forcing, the response and the conditions, rather than treating “resonance” as a complete explanation for any large vibration.

Example 8.10 · Identify what is held fixed

A test keeps the amplitude of an applied periodic force fixed. After waiting for a steady response at each setting, the observed displacement amplitudes are 1.4, 4.8, 2.6 and 1.0 mm at driving frequencies 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 Hz. What can be concluded, and what should be tested next?

The largest sampled response is at 1.5 Hz. The rise and fall suggest a resonant region nearby, but the four settings do not locate the exact maximum. Additional frequencies between 1.0 and 2.0 Hz would resolve the response more finely. Keep force amplitude fixed, allow the same settling criterion and report amplitude measurement limits. Increasing the driver’s force at 1.5 Hz instead would confound a frequency comparison.

Activity 8.11 · Explain a sustained response (25 minutes)

A driven oscillator has a constant-amplitude steady response at 2 Hz. Its driver supplies 0.015 J per cycle. Find the mean supplied power and the energy removed by damping over 10 s. Explain what happens initially if the driver is switched off, and design a comparison to investigate the effect of added damping on the response near resonance.

Discussion of Activity 8.11

There are two cycles each second, so mean supplied power is 0.015×2=0.030W. In 10 s, 20 cycles transfer 0.30 J; the same amount is removed from the mechanical motion in steady state. Switching off removes the continuing supply, leaving a free response that decays if damping remains. To compare damping, use the same oscillator and force amplitude, measure steady displacement amplitudes across the same frequency range and wait for settling at each setting. More damping generally lowers and broadens the lightly damped displacement-resonance peak; its maximum can shift. Keep changes to stiffness or mass separate.
