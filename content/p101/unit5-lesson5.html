Gravity across scales and unit review

A curved path tells us about acceleration. It does not, by itself, tell us which interaction is responsible. This distinction connects the rotating laboratory, the supported object and the orbiting moon—and gives us a way to check whether an explanation is complete.

Allow three hours: 35 minutes for the synthesis, reference and conclusion, 100 minutes for written work, and 45 minutes to read feedback and correct your reasoning. The written allocation consists of a ten-minute audit and the 90-minute cumulative exercise set below. There is no additional Python assignment or assessment at this point.

5.20 Gravity across scales

We began with a geometric question: how can velocity change when speed is constant? The answer depended on the direction of a tangent, not on the identity of the moving body. A puck attached to a cord and a satellite in a circular orbit therefore share the radial acceleration v2/r. They do not share the same force inventory. The cord pulls the puck; gravity attracts the satellite. “Centripetal” describes the inward resultant needed for the path, not a new kind of interaction to add to either diagram.

Newton's gravitational law also links scales. Two small, separated spherical masses attract each other, just as a planet and a moon do. The constant G is small in SI units, so a tabletop pair can exert a very small gravitational force even though a planetary source exerts an appreciable force on the same test mass. Do not infer from “hard to detect” that the interaction is absent. Conversely, the fact that gravity is present does not establish that it dominates every motion: a table can support a mass against its weight and a cord can redirect it.

Figure 5.16 extends the astronomical context without extending our mathematics. Before assigning a circular speed to Europa, we would need to ask which body supplies the principal attraction, where the distance is measured from and whether a circular, fixed-source approximation answers the intended question. A visible round disc is the shape of a body's projected surface, not evidence that its path is a circle. Photographs, trajectories and force models answer different questions.

Near Earth's surface we often used one constant value of g. In this unit that becomes a local approximation to a field that depends on distance. For a spherical Earth model, moving upward changes centre distance from R to R+h. If h is a very small fraction of R, the field changes little. If the change in centre distance is substantial, carrying the surface value into an orbital calculation is no longer justified. The earlier approximation remains useful in the range where its variation is unimportant for the question.

Example 5.15 · When is one value of field adequate?

Take a hypothetical spherical source and compare its field at the surface with its field at height 0.02R. The ratio is

g(R+0.02R)g(R)=R2(1.02R)2=11.022≈0.9612.

The field has fallen by about 3.88%, not 2%. A calculation seeking only a rough estimate might accept the surface value; one needing accuracy within 1% could not ignore this difference. At height R, centre distance doubles and field is one quarter of the surface value. Approximation quality depends on the required accuracy as well as the size of the neglected effect.

The circular-orbit model is another bounded approximation. It excludes changing radius and treats the central source as fixed. It can give useful scales of speed and time without describing every real trajectory. Recognising that limit is part of using a model competently. A more detailed theory would need more assumptions and evidence; adding decimal places to these equations would not supply them.

5.21 Choose a method, then audit the claim

Start by separating what is observed or stipulated from what must be inferred. If a problem gives a radius and a period, kinematics supplies speed and radial acceleration. If it asks whether a cord or contact can maintain that motion, make a force inventory and compare the required force with what the interaction can supply. If it specifies a gravity-only circular orbit, speed is constrained by the source parameter and radius; it is not an independent arbitrary input.

Figure 5.17 is a route through the reasoning, not a substitute for drawing the situation. A force diagram also fixes the signs. At the top of a vertical circle, inward is downward; at the bottom it is upward. The gravitational force has not reversed between those positions. Our radial coordinate has changed direction. Naming the positive direction beside the equation prevents a correct magnitude from acquiring the wrong sign.

After solving, ask whether the result can belong to the assumed interaction. A cord can pull but cannot push. A surface can exert a compressive normal force but cannot pull an unattached body towards itself. Static friction may take any required value up to its capacity; it is not automatically at its maximum. A negative required tension or normal force means the proposed constraint fails under the assumptions. Do not repair it by taking an absolute value.

Activity 5.19 · Audit an explanation

Allow 10 minutes. A learner writes: “An orbiting satellite feels gravity downwards and an equal centripetal force upwards. These cancel, which is why it keeps moving at constant speed. I used the altitude in GMm/r2 because that is its distance above Earth.” Identify the errors and replace the passage with three or four connected sentences. Include the assumptions needed for a circular speed.

Discussion of Activity 5.19

Gravity supplies the inward resultant; there is no separate balancing centripetal interaction. Constant speed around a circle still means changing velocity and therefore nonzero acceleration. Radius in the spherical-source formula is centre distance, R+h, not altitude. One possible repair is: “In a gravity-only circular model around a dominant, spherical, fixed Earth, gravity is the inward resultant. It changes the direction of the satellite's velocity while leaving its speed constant. At centre distance r=R+h, the speed must satisfy v2/r=GM/r2, so v=GM/r. A different speed or an additional important interaction would require reconsidering this circular model.”

Use the same audit for a computer result. Inputs need units, outputs need labels, and an assertion needs an independently justified comparison. A program that reproduces its own formula in a second line has checked consistency, not established the formula's physical validity. The benchmark and scaling predictions in Lessons 3 and 4 give you checks that precede execution; the Moon comparison introduces a different question about evidence.

Exercise 5.1 · Timing and direction · 5 minutes

A marker at radius 0.45 m completes 15 uniform revolutions in 30 s. Find its period, frequency, speed and radial acceleration. If it is at the rightmost point of a counterclockwise circle in the page, give the directions of velocity and acceleration. Explain why zero change in speed does not mean zero acceleration.

Hint for Exercise 5.1

Find the time for one revolution before using its circumference.

Solution to Exercise 5.1

Period is 30/15=2.0 s and frequency is 15/30=0.50 Hz. Thus v=2π(0.45)/2=0.45π≈1.41 m/s and ar=v2/0.45=0.45π2≈4.44 m/s². Velocity points upwards and acceleration left, towards the centre. Speed is the magnitude of velocity; its direction changes even though that magnitude stays constant.

Exercise 5.2 · A finite change · 7 minutes

A particle of speed 3.0 m/s moves counterclockwise on a circle of radius 1.5 m, from its rightmost point to its top. Use horizontal-right and vertical-up components to find its change in velocity and average acceleration over that quarter turn. Compare the magnitude of that average with the instantaneous radial acceleration. Why should they differ?

Hint for Exercise 5.2

Subtract final minus initial velocities, then divide by one quarter of the period. Do not replace a vector difference with a difference of speeds.

Solution to Exercise 5.2

The velocities are (0,3) and (−3,0) m/s, giving Δv=(−3,−3) m/s. Period is 2π(1.5)/3=π s, so Δt=π/4 s and average acceleration is (−12/π,−12/π) m/s². Its magnitude is 122/π≈5.40 m/s². Instantaneous radial acceleration has magnitude 32/1.5=6.0 m/s². The finite average combines accelerations whose directions change during the interval; it is not the instantaneous acceleration at either endpoint.

Exercise 5.3 · A friction limit · 8 minutes

A 0.60 kg block follows a horizontal circle of radius 0.50 m on a rotating flat platform without slipping. The coefficient of static friction is 0.32. Is a speed of 1.0 m/s feasible? Find the limiting speed and explain what changes if the block's mass is doubled. Include the vertical balance in your reasoning.

Hint for Exercise 5.3

Compare the needed friction with its maximum; do not assume equality until finding the limit.

Solution to Exercise 5.3

Vertical balance gives N=mg=5.88 N. Friction capacity is μsN=1.8816 N; demand at 1.0 m/s is mv2/r=1.20 N. The required static friction is therefore 1.20 N, within capacity. At the limit, mvmax2/r=μsmg, so vmax=0.32(9.8)(0.50)≈1.25 m/s. Doubling mass doubles demand and capacity together, leaving the limiting speed unchanged in this model. This is not a statement about sliding friction after slipping begins.

Exercise 5.4 · Which radius? · 8 minutes

A 0.25 kg bob on a 1.20 m string moves in a horizontal circle with the string at 40° to the vertical. Find the circle's radius, string tension and speed. Explain why using 1.20 m as the circle radius would be wrong.

Hint for Exercise 5.4

Resolve the one tension into vertical and inward components. The string length is the sloping side of a triangle.

Solution to Exercise 5.4

The horizontal radius is r=1.20sin40∘≈0.771 m. With no vertical acceleration, Scos40∘=mg, so S=2.45/cos40∘≈3.20 N. Inward, Ssin40∘=mv2/r, which gives v=rgtan40∘≈2.52 m/s. The string length reaches the support above the plane of the circle; only its horizontal projection reaches the circle's vertical axis. Tension components are not additional forces to add beside tension.

Exercise 5.5 · A proposed constraint · 10 minutes

At the top of a vertical circle of radius 0.80 m, a 0.20 kg body on a light cord has a proposed speed of 2.0 m/s. Determine the required tension and interpret it. Find the minimum speed for a nonnegative tension there. Does this local calculation prove that a body launched from the bottom will complete a full circle?

Hint for Exercise 5.5

Both weight and a taut cord pull inward at the top. A cord cannot supply a push.

Solution to Exercise 5.5

Taking downward as inward gives S+mg=mv2/r. The proposed tension is S=0.20(2.0)2/0.80−0.20(9.8)=−0.96 N. This is impossible for a cord: gravity alone already exceeds the required inward resultant for that stipulated speed and radius, and a cord cannot provide an outward push. The circular constraint therefore fails. Nonnegative tension requires v≥gr=7.84=2.8 m/s at the top, with zero tension the limiting case. This is a condition at one position. It supplies neither the speed changes around the path nor a sufficient launch condition at the bottom; those require further dynamical information.

Exercise 5.6 · Interaction and response · 10 minutes

Two non-overlapping, spherically symmetric bodies of masses 8.0 kg and 20 kg have centres 0.40 m apart. Find the gravitational force magnitude on each and the acceleration caused by this interaction on each. What happens to these three magnitudes if the centre distance triples? State why a supported laboratory body need not actually accelerate at the value you calculate.

Hint for Exercise 5.6

The interaction forces have equal magnitudes; divide by the receiving mass to find each acceleration contribution.

Solution to Exercise 5.6

The common force magnitude is F=G(8)(20)/(0.40)2=1000G≈6.67×10−8 N. Directions are opposite, each towards the other body's centre. The acceleration contributions are F/8≈8.34×10−9 m/s² for the 8 kg body and F/20≈3.34×10−9 m/s² for the 20 kg body. All three magnitudes fall to one ninth when separation triples and masses stay fixed. A support and Earth's gravity are other interactions; actual acceleration follows the total force, not this isolated pair contribution.

Exercise 5.7 · Height, field and an orbit · 10 minutes

A hypothetical spherical planet has radius 3.0×106 m and parameter μ=7.2×1013 m³/s². A small satellite is to follow a gravity-only circle at altitude 1.0×106 m. Find the centre distance, field, circular speed and period. Compare the field there with the surface field. State two assumptions behind the calculation.

Hint for Exercise 5.7

Convert altitude to centre distance before any inverse-square or circular-speed calculation.

Solution to Exercise 5.7

Radius is r=4.0×106 m, giving g=μ/r2=4.5 m/s². The circular condition gives v=μ/r=1.8×107≈4243 m/s. Thus T=2πr/v≈5924 s, or 98.7 minutes. Surface field is 7.2×1013/(3.0×106)2=8.0 m/s²; the orbital field is 4.5/8=0.5625 of that value. Assumptions include a spherically symmetric dominant source, negligible satellite influence on its motion, negligible other forces and an exactly circular exterior path. The speed must be tangential as well as have this magnitude.

Exercise 5.8 · Same time, different system · 10 minutes

In a second hypothetical system the central mass is 27 times larger and the exterior circular radius is three times larger than in a reference system. Predict the ratios of speed, period and field. A report says, “The equal periods show the forces are equal.” Assess this claim for satellites of equal mass, and then for unspecified satellite masses.

Hint for Exercise 5.8

Use both a source factor and a radius factor. A force also depends on the receiving mass.

Solution to Exercise 5.8

Here q=27 and k=3. Speed ratio is 27/3=3, period ratio is 33/27=1, and field ratio is 27/32=3. Equal periods arise because both circumference and speed triple. For equal satellite masses, gravitational force triples because F=mg. With unspecified satellite masses, force ratio is 3mB/mA; equal periods alone cannot determine it. In neither case does the stated conclusion follow.

Exercise 5.9 · Diagnose a numerical answer · 10 minutes

A notebook uses SI parameter μ=8.0×1012 m³/s² but sets radius = 2000.0 while its author means 2000 km. It evaluates the correct formulas for speed and period and prints many digits. Compared with the intended model, by what factors are the printed speed and period wrong? Give two checks that would expose the error. Would agreement between vT and 2πr necessarily reveal it?

Hint for Exercise 5.9

The entered radius is one thousandth of the intended radius. Apply the radius scaling powers.

Solution to Exercise 5.9

Since k=10−3 at fixed source, speed is too large by k−1/2=1000≈31.6. Period is k3/2≈3.16×10−5 of its intended value, about 31623 times too small. An explicit kilometres-to-metres input audit and comparison with the independent benchmark 2000 m/s and 2000π s expose it. With the hypothetical source radius 106 m in Lesson 4's function, the exterior-domain check also rejects 2000 m. The identity vT=2πr can still hold for the same wrongly entered radius; self-consistency alone does not guarantee correct physical inputs. More printed digits cannot repair the unit error.

Exercise 5.10 · Infer, compare and qualify · 12 minutes

A fictional data sheet lists two nearly circular satellite paths around one dominant source. Path A has centre radius 2.0×106 m and period 6.0×103 s. Path B has centre radius 8.0×106 m and period 4.8×104 s. Treat the tabulated values as exact for this exercise.

  1. Infer the source parameter independently from each pair (r,T) by rearranging the circular-period formula.
  2. Check the period ratio without evaluating π. Explain what agreement supports and what it does not prove.
  3. A revised B entry says 2.4×104 s at the same radius. By what factor would its inferred parameter differ from A's? Name at least two possible issues to investigate before deciding that the gravitational law is wrong.
Hint for Exercise 5.10

Square the period relation and solve for the source parameter. At fixed radius, the inferred parameter varies as inverse period squared.

Solution to Exercise 5.10

From T=2πr3/μ, we obtain μ=4π2r3/T2. A gives approximately 8.773×1012 m³/s². B has four times A's radius and eight times its period, so its parameter is multiplied by 43/82=1 and gives the same result. The predicted period ratio is 43/2=8, matching the table.

This supports consistency of the entries with one common circular-model parameter. It does not prove exact circularity, establish the observational quality or exclude other influences. Halving B's period multiplies its inferred parameter by four. Investigate transcription and units, whether period means one complete orbit, whether the radius is a centre distance, departure from circularity, and the assumed dominant-source description. These invented values have no stated measurement uncertainties; a real comparison would require them before judging significance.

When reviewing, classify a mistake by its first cause: direction, geometry, force inventory, model assumption, algebra, units or interpretation. Correct that cause and redo the reasoning. If most difficulty lies in force inventories, return to Lesson 2; for centre distance and circular speed, revisit Lesson 3; for comparisons and computational claims, revisit Lesson 4. A corrected explanation is stronger evidence of learning than a page of unchecked numerical answers.

Looking back and looking ahead

You should now be able to explain inward acceleration from a change in velocity direction, derive a radial force equation from real interactions, and decide whether a proposed constraint is feasible. You have also connected gravity to a position-dependent field and a conditional circular-orbit model. The same source parameter predicts speed and period, and a comparison becomes interpretable only when the held-fixed quantities and centre distances are clear.

The computational work adds a discipline as much as a technique. A transparent function states its domain; an independent benchmark can expose an implementation error; a scaling prediction checks how the result changes. None replaces observational evidence or the judgement needed to choose an appropriate model. Keep your two-scenario report and one corrected exercise as useful records of your own reasoning; there is no requirement to submit them here.

Before leaving the unit, try explaining in words why a constant-speed satellite accelerates, why an astronaut can have a zero support reading where gravity is appreciable, and why a negative tension cannot be repaired by changing its sign. Use your explanations, not just a tally of completed questions, to decide what needs revisiting.
