P101 · Block 3 · Unit 8

Oscillation and sampled signals

Suppose a camera records a vibrating object only once every second. In each frame it appears in the same place. Is the object stationary, or is it completing a whole number of oscillations between frames? To answer, we need both a description of the motion and a description of how it was observed.

Repeated motion offers a useful change of perspective after the journeys, collisions and stopping models of Block 2. We can describe where an object is now, but we can also ask where it lies within a repeating cycle. That second description is its phase. Once a cycle is understood, a few parameters can specify a prediction for many later instants.

We will begin with physical motion about equilibrium and learn to read its size and timescale from a graph. A sinusoidal model will then connect the graph to calculations. You will see why a body can have its largest acceleration when momentarily at rest, and why crossing equilibrium does not make it stop. Force and energy reasoning from earlier units will explain these features. Real oscillations can decay or be sustained by a driver, so the conditions of the ideal model matter.

The second half of the unit turns the question around. Instead of generating a graph from a known model, we ask what a finite list of samples can tell us. You will estimate a period using identifiable events, compare with a known benchmark and construct a case in which two different motions produce the same samples. The final notebook asks you to defend a sampling choice and a limitation with evidence.

Before starting, be comfortable with signed displacement, velocity, acceleration, system boundaries and energy transfers from Units 4–7. The Python work uses functions, loops, arrays and plotting from Units 1–3. This unit introduces the radian phase convention needed for its models and supplies their velocity and acceleration formulas. You need not derive a differential-equation solution.

Allow 18 hours in total, including three hours of Python. Lessons 1–5 take 3, 4, 3, 4 and 4 hours respectively. Those allocations include activities, feedback and correction. The final lesson includes the cumulative exercise set and reference revision; it is part of the unit’s workload. By the end, aim to describe an oscillation consistently, interpret a supplied model, explain damping and resonance, and produce a reproducible sampling comparison whose conclusion respects the available evidence.
