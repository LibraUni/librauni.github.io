TMA 01: Represent, solve and check

A vector, a matrix and a system of equations can describe different aspects of the same problem. In this assessment, choose and use those representations, explain what your calculations mean, and decide how much a computational check establishes.

Units 1–3 · 100 marks · Six hours. Complete all five questions. These are the separate assessment hours allocated after Block 1, beyond its 54 study hours. No new physical law or experimental work is required.

The block began with the problem of describing a place. Figure T1 returns to that setting: a real view can suggest a grid, while perspective changes apparent lengths and angles. The questions use explicitly stated ideal coordinates; do not take measurements from the photograph.

Question 1 · Positions and paths (15 marks)

Allow about 40 minutes.

An ideal plan uses east as positive x and north as positive y, with both coordinates measured in metres. A marker travels along straight segments from A=(−2,1) to B=(1,5), then to C=(5,2). It makes no detour between these points. Figure T2 shows the given positions; all coordinates are exact for this model.

  1. Find the displacement vectors for the two legs and for the complete journey. Calculate the total distance travelled and the magnitude of the overall displacement, explaining why these last two quantities differ. [5]

  2. Give a unit vector in the direction of the overall displacement and check that it has magnitude one. Find the direction of the overall displacement, measured anticlockwise from east. Use the signs of its components to justify the quadrant of your angle. [6]

  3. Now choose a new origin O′ at old coordinates (3,−2), retaining the same axis directions and metre scale. Give the new coordinates of A and C and use them to recalculate the overall displacement. Explain which of position coordinates, displacement and distance depend on this origin change. [4]

Question 2 · Products and projections (20 marks)

Allow about 55 minutes.

In a right-handed three-dimensional coordinate system, let

a=(−231),b=(122).

  1. Calculate a·b and the angle between the vectors. Explain how the sign of the scalar product is consistent with your angle. [5]

  2. Find the signed scalar projection of a along b, the vector projection p, and the remaining vector q=a−p. Check both p+q=a and q·b=0. Explain the difference between your scalar and vector projection answers. [7]

  3. Calculate a×b and use it to give the area of the parallelogram with adjacent sides a and b. State how the cross product and the area change when the order is reversed, and relate the vector sign to orientation. [5]

  4. A proposed extension replaces b by the zero vector. Which of the scalar product, cross product, angle between the vectors and projection onto the direction of b remain defined? Give the defined products and explain the undefined quantities. [3]

Question 3 · Ordered transformations (20 marks)

Allow about 60 minutes.

Two transformations of the same plane have matrices

S=(1201),R=(0−110).

A point or vector is transformed first by S and then by R. Call the matrix of the complete transformation T.

  1. Use the columns of each matrix to state the images of the coordinate unit vectors e1 and e2. Describe geometrically what each transformation does. [4]

  2. Find T, explaining the order of multiplication. For v=(2,−1), calculate the result in two ways: by applying the two transformations successively, and by applying T directly. Then calculate the result when the order of transformations is reversed. Explain what this example shows about order, without making a claim about every possible pair of matrices. Treat the component pair as a column vector when multiplying. [6]

  3. Calculate detT, find T−1 and check your inverse by multiplication with T in both orders. State why every output has exactly one input. [6]

  4. A student argues, “The area scale is one, so this transformation preserves the length of every vector.” Decide whether that conclusion is valid for T. Support your decision with a calculated example and state what the determinant does tell you about area and orientation. [4]

Question 4 · Solving and classifying (25 marks)

Allow about 75 minutes.

In this question the unknowns and the parameter k are real numbers. Use elimination and retain each original system so that your checks refer to the given equations.

  1. Solve the following system by elimination. Explain why your equation operation preserves the simultaneous solutions, check the result in both original equations and justify its uniqueness. [8]

    2x−y=1,x+2y=8.

  2. Classify the following system for every value of k. Where solutions exist, describe the complete family with one real parameter. Verify that the family satisfies both original equations and explain why it includes every solution. State why a zero coefficient determinant alone would not settle whether this system is consistent. [9]

    x+2y=3,2x+4y=k.

  3. For the three-unknown system below, first eliminate x to obtain two equations in y and z. Continue elimination, back-substitute and check the result in all three original equations. State whether it is the unique solution and justify your answer from the elimination. [8]

    x+y+z=4,2x+y−z=3,x−y+2z=1.

Question 5 · An independent computational check (20 marks)

Allow about 75 minutes, then use part of the final checking allowance for the fresh run.

Write a short Python program that checks candidate solutions by substitution. Use ordinary numbers, flat lists, functions, conditions and a loop. No imported package, matrix solver or symbolic solver is needed or permitted for this question. Your program is to check supplied candidates, not search for solutions.

Use these inputs first:

M=(21−13),c=(77).

The two candidates are (2,3) and (3,1). Store the four entries of M in row order in the flat list [2, 1, -1, 3]. Targets and candidates each have two entries. Define the residual to mean Mv−c. All required test values are small integers, so exact equality with zero is suitable here.

  1. Before executing your program, calculate by hand the two residuals and predict which candidates it should accept. Keep these predictions visible in your written work or notebook text cells; do not overwrite them after running. [4]

  2. Write a reusable function that takes a flat four-entry matrix list, a two-entry candidate and a two-entry target and returns the two residual entries. Write a second function that uses those residuals to return a Boolean result for satisfaction of the whole system. Use a loop to print each candidate, its residual and the Boolean result. The functions must use their arguments, rather than numbers belonging to one particular case. [6]

  3. Choose one additional integer candidate that satisfies the second equation but fails the first. Show its expected residual by hand, then run your program on it. Explain how the given failing candidate and your additional candidate together test that neither equation is ignored. [3]

  4. Reuse the same functions, without changing their definitions, for the system below. Choose two distinct integer solutions, predict and print their residuals and acceptance results. Use these results to assess the claim “If my checker returns True, it has established a unique solution.” [4]

    x−y=4,2x−2y=8.

  5. Restart the notebook kernel and run all cells in order, or run the script in a fresh Python process. Save the outputs and give brief run instructions. Explain why success on these few tests does not establish correctness for every possible input, and why exact equality is not a general prescription for calculations with rounded measurements. Report any unresolved discrepancy honestly. [3]

You do not need an input-validation framework, a graphical interface or additional packages. Submit a notebook with outputs, or a script together with its saved text output. Put the explanations in your answer document or notebook text cells and cross-reference them, so the same evidence is marked once.

How your work will be marked

The bracketed marks total 100. Credit rewards method, correct calculation, checks and interpretation. Show enough reasoning for another reader to follow the decisions. A carried arithmetic error can earn subsequent method credit when the later work is consistent; it should not be penalised repeatedly. An unsupported assertion of uniqueness, completeness or program correctness does not earn the corresponding reasoning marks.
