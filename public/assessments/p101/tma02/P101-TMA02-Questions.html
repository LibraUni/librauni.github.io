TMA 02 · Systems, balances and model tests

A useful physical account must do more than produce a plausible number. It must identify the system, connect the relevant interactions or transfers, and explain what a calculation or observation can establish. In this assignment you will choose and test those accounts across contact forces, orbital motion, a collision, a thermal assembly and a recorded motion.

Question 1 · A pull changes the contact 18 marks · about 40 minutes

A compact 3.0 kg block rests on a stationary horizontal bench. A light cord pulls it to the right at 37° above the horizontal. Model the block as a particle, neglect air effects and take g=9.8m/s2. The bench is nonadhesive. The stipulated dry-contact coefficients are μs=0.35 and μk=0.25. Use an approximately inertial laboratory frame.

  1. Draw a free-body diagram of the block while it remains at rest. Label each force by its agent and direction, and choose axes. Identify the Newton’s-third-law partner of the bench’s normal force on the block. Explain why the block’s weight is not that partner. [5]

  2. For a cord tension of 10.0 N, determine the normal force and the friction required for rest. Decide whether the block can remain at rest under the stated model. Include the inequality that supports your decision and explain why setting the static friction equal to its maximum at the outset would be inappropriate. [5]

  3. The tension is raised to a constant 14.0 N at the same angle. Test whether rest is still possible, then calculate the horizontal acceleration as the block begins sliding right. Check that your assumed contact with the bench is possible. [4]

  4. During the resulting slide, the block travels 1.50 m horizontally from rest under that constant pull. Calculate the work of each force on the block and find its final speed using work and kinetic energy. Check that speed using the acceleration in part (c). [4]

Question 2 · Which distance belongs in an orbit? 16 marks · about 45 minutes

A spherical, nonrotating model planet has radius R=3.0×106m and gravitational parameter GM=8.0×1013m3/s2. A 250 kg satellite travels in a circular orbit at altitude h=1.0×106m above the surface. The planet’s centre is treated as fixed, its exterior field is spherical, and other forces are negligible. Use an inertial frame centred on the planet.

  1. Draw the satellite’s force diagram and state the radius needed in the force and circular-acceleration equations. Starting with those equations, derive expressions for orbital speed and period. Explain why adding a second inward “centripetal force” to gravity would count the same physical requirement twice. [6]

  2. Calculate the gravitational field magnitude at the satellite, its speed and its period. Give the period in seconds and minutes. A proposed calculation uses h, rather than your radius, in the speed expression. Find the factor by which that calculation changes the speed and explain the geometric mistake. [6]

  3. Compare with a 500 kg satellite in the same circular orbit: what changes in gravitational force, acceleration, speed and period? Explain how the original satellite can be in free fall while maintaining a constant altitude. [4]

Question 3 · Two checks on one collision 20 marks · about 55 minutes

Two trolleys meet on a straight, level track. Trolley A has mass 1.20 kg and initial velocity +3.00 m/s; trolley B has mass 0.80 kg and initial velocity −1.00 m/s. Positive is to the right. The velocities refer to the same laboratory frame immediately before and immediately after contact. No material leaves the pair. Initially use a zero-external-horizontal-impulse model.

Two candidate final records have been proposed for this same initial state: record I gives vA=−0.50m/s, vB=+4.25m/s; record II gives vA=+1.00m/s, vB=+2.00m/s. The intended model is a passive encounter: it supplies no external mechanical work and releases no previously stored energy into translational motion.

  1. Calculate the initial total momentum and the total for each proposed final record. State what this check establishes about each candidate and what it leaves undecided. [5]

  2. Calculate the initial translational kinetic energy and the value for each final record. Decide which candidate is compatible with the passive-encounter assumption as well as momentum balance. For any incompatible candidate, quantify the additional energy that would have to enter the translational account. Explain why momentum agreement alone cannot settle the choice. [5]

  3. Use record II and a contact duration of 0.060 s. Find the horizontal impulse and average horizontal resultant force on each trolley. Relate the two results to the chosen system and the third law; explain why these averages do not determine the peak contact forces. [5]

  4. Now examine the neglected external interaction. Suppose a credible bound on the magnitude of the external horizontal resultant on the pair is 0.10 N. Bound its impulse over 0.060 s and over 1.0 s. An investigation aims to resolve momentum changes of 0.010 kg m/s. Assess the approximation over each interval and explain why comparing remote video frames can weaken an otherwise useful collision model. [5]

Question 4 · An energy account for a thermal assembly 18 marks · about 50 minutes

A warm solid body has constant heat capacity 480 J/K and initial temperature 70.0 °C. A cooler body of capacity 720 J/K and a vessel of capacity 120 J/K both start at 25.0 °C. They are placed in thermal contact. Throughout this question the materials remain in fixed phases, the given capacities apply, and the initial and final temperatures of each body are spatially uniform. Bulk kinetic and gravitational energy changes are negligible.

  1. First treat the whole assembly as thermally isolated with no work crossing its boundary. Derive and calculate the common final temperature, including the vessel. Calculate each body’s signed internal-energy change and check the total. [5]

  2. In a separate trial with the same initial state, a paddle does 600 J of work on the assembly. The reported common final temperature is 40.0 °C after the motion settles. Infer the net heat transfer for the whole assembly, stating your sign convention. Explain how the assembly can receive paddle work while having the internal-energy change you found. [5]

  3. For the cooler body in part (b), express the final temperature in kelvin and the temperature change in kelvin. Explain why the paddle input is work rather than “heat stored”, and why adding 273.15 to a temperature difference would give the wrong energy change. [3]

  4. A colleague concludes that the inferred heat transfer proves the thermal insulation is defective. Give one alternative explanation involving the temperature measurement, then propose a bounded follow-up that helps distinguish it from heat transfer to the room. In about 100–150 words identify a controlled condition, the temperature/timing records to retain and a comparison or calibration check. State one limit on what that check would establish. No new experiment is required. [5]

Question 5 · Does the motion require changing acceleration? 28 marks · about 115 minutes

A marker is tracked along a straight rail. The origin and positive direction are fixed to the laboratory; time is measured from the same release event in each run. The record below combines independent runs sampled at specified elapsed times. In particular, the two readings at 2.4 s are different runs, not a transcription duplicate. Positions are recorded to 0.01 m. No independent accuracy, calibration or timing-uncertainty specification is provided.

The record is invented for this assessment and is not evidence from a real apparatus. Treat it as the supplied record when comparing the two stipulated accounts below; the final conclusion must retain that provenance. Their coefficients are fixed for the task, not parameters for you to fit. Both models are restricted to 0≤t≤4s. With numerical time in seconds and position in metres,

xA(t)=0.40+1.60t−0.30t2,
xB(t)=0.40+1.60t−0.30t2+0.040t3.

The four coefficient units are respectively m, m/s, m/s² and m/s³. The CSV headers give the numerical units.

  1. Differentiate each model to obtain velocity and acceleration. Evaluate both at 3.0 s, and state the direction of motion and whether speed is increasing or decreasing there. For Model B at 2.0 s, compare its exact velocity with centred estimates using steps 0.40 s and 0.10 s. Explain why this is a test of a numerical derivative rather than an independent test of the physical model. [6]

  2. Write your own notebook or script. Load the unchanged CSV by relative path, checking its seven rows, expected columns, finite numerical entries and model time domain; fail with a helpful message if a numerical check fails. Preserve both readings at 2.4 s. Implement your own named prediction functions with documented units, then produce a labelled row-by-row table containing the run identifier, time, observed position, both predictions and both observed-minus-predicted residuals. Use a loop meaningfully in reporting or checking the rows. [6]

  3. Calculate each model’s mean absolute residual over all seven rows. Make and save a figure with position and residual panels: discrete observations and both model curves over 0–4 s above, both residual series and a zero reference below. Distinguish the series by markers or line styles as well as colour and label axes with units. Explain why the repeated-time spread should remain visible and why the smaller mean absolute residual alone does not identify the physical cause of disagreement. [7]

Include the corresponding derivative/check calculation in your program. Record actual outcomes, Python/library versions and output paths. A failed check should be retained and explained. [5]

  5. In about 150–200 words, conclude which specified account better describes the supplied record, using numerical evidence and the residual pattern. Separate a computation check from the evidence needed to establish a real changing acceleration. Propose one useful next observation or instrument check, explaining its purpose.
