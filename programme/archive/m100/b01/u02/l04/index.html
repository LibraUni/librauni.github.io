SECTION 1 · ABOUT 60 MINUTES

What changes and what stays fixed?

Direct proportionality means y=kx with fixed k: doubling x doubles y. Inverse proportionality means y=k/x, for non-zero x: doubling x halves y. Inverse-square dependence means y=k/x²: doubling x divides y by four. These statements require other parameters to remain fixed. “Increases with” does not by itself mean direct proportionality; y=x+5 increases with x but does not preserve y/x.

Worked example

For supplied travel time t=d/v at fixed d, replacing v by 3v gives t/3. At fixed v, replacing d by 3d gives 3t. For supplied intensity I=k/r², increasing r from 2 to 6 multiplies distance by 3 and intensity by 1/9.

Change a scale, predict the response

With all other parameters fixed, choose the factor multiplying the input. Predict each output factor before moving the slider.

Input ×1; direct output ×1; inverse output ×1; inverse-square output ×1.

This explores the supplied mathematical laws, not a derivation of a physical inverse-square law.

Work it through

  1. For y=4x, predict the factor in y when x is multiplied by 5.
  2. For y=12/x, predict the factor when x is halved.
  3. For y=k/r², predict the factor when r is halved.
  4. Explain why the fixed quantity must be named in a travel-time comparison.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. 5.
  2. 2.
  3. 4.
  4. Changing both distance and speed could increase, decrease or preserve the time; the ratio argument needs its conditions.

SECTION 2 · ABOUT 60 MINUTES

Dimensions: a necessary check

Dimensions identify kinds of quantity: write L for length and T for time. Speed has dimensions L/T; area has L². Terms added or equated must have compatible dimensions. A dimensionless numerical constant does not change dimensions. Dimensional compatibility is necessary for a physical equation written in ordinary quantities, but it cannot determine every numerical coefficient or establish a physical law.

Worked example

The model d=vt is dimensionally compatible because(L/T)T=L. So is d=2vt. Dimensions cannot choose between them. But d=v+t attempts to add L/T to T, so it is incompatible. This is a structural error before any values are substituted.

Work it through

Show intermediate steps and give units or restrictions where relevant. For code, record a prediction before running.

  1. Check t=d/v and t=dv dimensionally.
  2. Can length be added to area?
  3. If A is area and r is length, are A=r² and A=7r² both dimensionally compatible?
  4. Give a dimensionally compatible equation that is not the supplied distance model.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. d/v has T;dv has L²/T, so only the first fits time.
  2. No, not as physical quantities in a valid sum.
  3. Yes; dimensions cannot determine the coefficient.
  4. d=3vt is compatible but differs from the specified d=vt relation.

SECTION 3 · ABOUT 30 MINUTES

Two scenarios, one transparent calculation

Use code to evaluate stated scenarios after predicting their ratio. This separates understanding of the dependence from arithmetic execution. Name the fixed inputs explicitly. Compare outputs through a ratio as well as their individual values. Keep the original scenario visible so that the ratio has a clearly defined reference.

Worked example

For distance 120 m, speeds 4 and 12 m/s give times 30 and 10 s. The speed ratio is 3 and the time ratio is 1/3. Agreement with this prediction is a useful check, but does not validate the physical assumptions of the model.

Type, predict, run

distance_m = 120
time_a_s = distance_m / 4
time_b_s = distance_m / 12
print(time_a_s)
print(time_b_s)
print(time_b_s / time_a_s)

Work it through

Show intermediate steps and give units or restrictions where relevant. For code, record a prediction before running.

  1. Type both time calculations using named inputs.
  2. At fixed speed 4 m/s, replace distance 120 m by 60 m; predict and calculate.
  3. Use I=100/r² at r=2 and r=4; predict before running.
  4. Write one sentence stating what your code check establishes and one stating what it does not.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. 30.0 and 10.0 s.
  2. 15.0 s, half the time.
  3. 25 and 6.25; the latter is one quarter.
  4. It checks the numeric evaluation against a scaling prediction; it does not demonstrate that a real source obeys this law.

SECTION 4 · ABOUT 60 MINUTES

Travel time and inverse-square scaling

A model makes a conditional prediction. Inverse-square examples here assume a fixed source and a supplied law; we do not derive that law or infer its universal validity. Keep the difference between a predicted ratio and an observed result visible. An observation would require data and information about how it was obtained.

Worked example

A supplied model gives I=80 at r=3 in consistent units. At r=9, the distance triples and the model predicts I=80/9≈8.89. The exact ratio 1/9 is clearer than the rounded output. Changing the source strength at the same time would invalidate this particular comparison.

Work it through

Show intermediate steps and give units or restrictions where relevant. For code, record a prediction before running.

  1. Predict intensity at r=6 and r=1.5 relative to the given 80 at r=3.
  2. For t=d/v, compare d=200,v=5 with d=300,v=10.
  3. Explain why neither time alone can be ranked from distance alone in the second task.
  4. State one condition that would have to hold before applying the inverse-square comparison to observations.
Feedback and worked answers
  1. 20 and 320.
  2. 40 s and 30 s.
  3. Both distance and speed changed; the ratios must be combined.
  4. The supplied source parameter and applicable geometry/model conditions must remain fixed.
